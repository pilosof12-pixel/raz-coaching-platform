# Cluster Q — Plyometrics and Explosive Power

## Purpose
Use plyometric and ballistic work to improve rapid force expression, stretch-shortening-cycle ability, jumping, sprint/COD support and sport-specific power. This is a support layer for strength and combat sport, not a mandatory conditioning block.

## Classification

* Low amplitude elastic: pogo, ankling, low hops.
* Bilateral jump/land: squat jump, CMJ, box jump.
* Reactive: drop/depth jumps and rebound work.
* Unilateral/lateral: bounds and single-leg/lateral hops.
* Throws: medicine-ball chest, overhead and rotational throws.

## Programming logic

1. Select direction and contraction demand from the goal.
2. Put priority power work early while output is high.
3. Stop on meaningful drop in height, distance, speed, stiffness or landing quality.
4. Start with low effective volume and increase only when quality and recovery support it.
5. Count sport contacts. Hard wrestling, takedown work, sprinting, jumping and striking footwork can already supply explosive and tendon load.
6. Progress complexity from stable landing and bilateral control toward reactive, unilateral and multidirectional tasks as the athlete earns it.
7. Use contrast/PAPE only when an advanced athlete demonstrably performs better after the primer; do not make it a ritual.
8. Strength and plyometrics can coexist: heavy strength raises force capacity, plyometrics train rapid expression. Protect quality of the primary adaptation.

## Combat sport routing

* BJJ/MMA primary: keep plyometric dose small and high quality unless explosive power is a named weakness/goal.
* Hard sparring/takedown week: reduce redundant lower-limb impact first, not necessarily all power work. Medicine-ball throws or low-impact explosive options can remain.
* Fight camp: bias toward familiar, low-soreness, confidence-building power exposures as competition approaches.
* Active lower-limb pain: use the Pain/Return-to-Loading route; do not use blanket lockouts based solely on a pain number.

## Evidence interpretation
Plyometric training can improve jump, sprint, change-of-direction and strength/power outcomes, including in combat-sport athletes. The evidence does not justify one universal contact count or frequency for every athlete; dose must account for training age, exercise intensity, sport contacts and recovery.
