# Overcoming Gravity decision layer for Phase 15

Purpose: distilled coaching rules for gymnastics and bodyweight-strength goals. This file is a paraphrased implementation guide, not a reproduction of the book. Primary reference: Steven Low, *Overcoming Gravity: A Systematic Approach to Gymnastics and Bodyweight Strength* (2011), especially Chapters 5-9 and the exercise-reference progressions in Appendix B.

## 1. Demonstrated ability controls starting level

Use the athlete's clean demonstrated performance as the main starting anchor. Progression charts are maps, not reasons to regress an athlete who already owns a harder variation.

Competency heuristics from Chapter 5:

* Static strength progression: roughly 6 seconds of clean control at the next level is enough evidence to begin treating that level as competent training territory.
* Dynamic strength progression: roughly 3 clean repetitions at the next level is meaningful competency evidence.
* Eccentric progression: several controlled negatives of about 3 seconds indicate readiness to use the movement, but eccentrics are a bridge, not mandatory once full concentric reps are owned.
* Balance-heavy skills need repeated quality practice and longer-term motor learning; do not treat strength prerequisites as the whole skill.

These are decision heuristics, not universal pass/fail tests. Technique, pain, fatigue and sport schedule still govern the prescription.

## 2. Exercise order

Default hierarchy from Chapter 6:

1. Warm-up and position-specific preparation.
2. Skill / technique work while fresh.
3. High-quality strength work: power, isometrics, eccentrics and difficult concentric strength.
4. Conditioning/endurance if required.
5. Low-cost prehabilitation, mobility and flexibility.

For a primary gymnastics strength skill, specific attempts or holds should normally occur before fatigue-producing hypertrophy/accessory work. A non-fatiguing power primer may precede the skill if it does not reduce skill quality.

## 3. Specificity plus support

The actual target skill family remains the anchor once a measurable version is available. Easier progressions, weighted basics and antagonist work can support the skill but do not replace direct practice.

Lower progressions may be retained as warm-up, technique rehearsal or back-off volume when they are approximately 1-3 meaningful progression steps easier and genuinely low cost.

Do not spread training across too many unrelated goals. Preserve a small number of primary skill exposures, then use support exercises to address weak links and structural balance.

## 4. Quality and fatigue

Stop skill sets before technical breakdown. In bodyweight strength, poor position changes the actual exercise and can increase joint/connective-tissue stress.

Most strength work should remain short of technical failure. As difficulty rises, preserve repetition/hold quality and manage weekly fatigue rather than chasing exhaustion.

Before adding volume, ask whether the athlete is progressing, recovering within roughly 24-48 hours, sleeping adequately, and maintaining quality in other sport sessions.

## 5. Advanced programming

For advanced athletes, frequency can remain moderate while intensity and volume are varied across the week. Chapter 9 supports light/medium/heavy or daily-undulating approaches rather than forcing the same difficulty and rep structure every exposure.

Practical implementation:

* One exposure may emphasize harder progressions / lower reps / shorter holds.
* Another may use a slightly easier progression for more clean volume.
* A third, when recovery allows, may be technique or low-fatigue specific practice.
* If a hard progression can be briefly achieved but not accumulated for useful volume, an advanced athlete may combine a short hard hold/attempt with an immediate easier progression to complete the set. Use this only when transitions are technically stable.

Do not automatically deload every fourth week. Use fatigue, pain, stalled performance and accumulated sport stress to decide whether to hold, reduce, or restructure.

## 6. Structural balance and GPP

Structural balance matters even when the athlete has one dominant skill goal. Antagonist, scapular, trunk, elbow/wrist/shoulder and lower-body support can be used when it addresses an identified weak link or sport need.

At advanced level, small isolation or accessory doses are legitimate for known weak links, hypertrophy goals or connective-tissue load management. Examples can include curls for elbow-flexor capacity, triceps/pressing support, Pallof press, carries, Copenhagen work, neck work or local hypertrophy. These are optional micro-doses and are the first items removed when they displace primary skill quality, exceed the session range or worsen sport recovery.

## 7. Concurrent sport

Full-body training usually remains efficient, but split structure can be used when another sport consumes meaningful recovery resources. Gymnastics work must be scheduled against the whole week, not in isolation.

Keep the hardest skill/strength exposures away from the most fatiguing sport sessions when possible. When same-day training is unavoidable, protect the primary skill by reducing secondary volume first.

## 8. Family-specific implementation

### Planche

Use the verified planche graph and demonstrated level. Keep straight-arm position, scapular protraction, pelvic/body-line control and pain-free wrist/shoulder loading as quality constraints. A primary planche goal normally receives at least two specific weekly exposures when recovery permits. One may be harder intensity work and another slightly easier accumulated-quality work.

Do not force Planche Lean or Tuck Planche on an athlete who already demonstrates a clean Advanced Tuck/Straddle level. Easier rungs may appear only as warm-up/back-off if useful.

### Front lever

Use direct front-lever holds/pulls at or near the demonstrated level as the anchor. Pull-ups, rows and weighted pulling are support strength, not substitutes for direct lever practice when front lever is a primary goal. Use antagonist/pressing work as needed for structural balance.

### One-arm pull-up / one-arm chin-up

Once the athlete owns full concentric reps, direct unilateral concentric work is the highest-specificity anchor. Assisted reps can provide additional clean volume. Weighted pull-ups/chin-ups are valuable strength-reserve support. Eccentrics are primarily a bridge for athletes who do not yet own the full concentric and should not replace direct work once multiple strict reps are demonstrated.

### Handstand and freestanding HSPU

Separate balance/skill from strength. Freestanding handstand/HSPU goals require direct practice of the free skill, while wall HSPU, strict overhead pressing and other presses can build strength reserve. Do not count a wall-supported press as identical to freestanding skill exposure.

### Human flag

Use the verified flag progression at the athlete's demonstrated level. Direct flag holds/controlled transitions are required for a primary flag goal. Pulling, pressing, carries and trunk work support the position but do not replace direct side-specific flag practice.

## 9. Anti-hallucination / product rules

Only prescribe named exercises that exist in the verified exercise dictionary and demo resolver.

Never invent an unverified banded, wall, eccentric, one-leg, deficit or partial variation solely because it sounds plausible.

Never output internal review markers or unresolved exercise rows. If a useful accessory lacks a verified demo, choose a verified equivalent or omit it until the library is completed.
