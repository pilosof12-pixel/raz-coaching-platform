## ARTICLE 1 — Bioenergetics and Energy-System Interaction

Core model: muscular work is continuous ATP turnover supplied by overlapping phosphagen, glycolytic and oxidative pathways. They do not switch on sequentially. Relative contribution changes with intensity, duration, recovery, substrate availability, training status and modality. [K7 Ch.2; M8 Chs.5-7]

Programming implications:
- Start from the required output pattern: meaningful effort duration, proximity to maximal output, repetition pattern, recovery available and total event duration. Then select the missing adaptation rather than labelling a sport simply aerobic or anaerobic. [K7 Ch.14; LB Chs.1,3,4]
- For isolated maximal actions, preserve high output and enough recovery. Do not accidentally turn power work into glycolytic conditioning by shortening recovery.
- Repeated explosive performance is not only a phosphagen problem. Oxidative metabolism supports PCr restoration and recovery between bursts.
- Longer hard efforts increase glycolytic and oxidative contribution; interval structure should follow the desired adaptation, not a universal time chart.
- Do not use blood lactate or discomfort as the goal. Lactate is produced, transported, oxidized and reused; fatigue is task-specific and multifactorial.
- Modality changes local muscular and mechanical cost even when nominal interval timing is identical.

Failure modes: rigid energy-system time charts; calling a whole sport purely aerobic/anaerobic; incomplete recovery during peak-power work; duplicating glycolytic work already supplied by sport; maximizing lactate; assuming long easy and short hard work create identical adaptations.

Decision rules:
IF the event requires isolated maximal actions with generous recovery, THEN prioritize output quality and sufficient recovery.
IF the event requires repeated near-maximal actions with limited recovery, THEN train both action quality and the oxidative recovery processes supporting repeatability.
IF sport practice already supplies frequent severe metabolic exposure, THEN assess aerobic base/recovery/another missing quality before adding more glycolytic work.
IF a universal energy-system percentage table is tempting, THEN use it only as teaching context and program from event demand, athlete response and modality.

Unresolved source gap: no universal exact phosphagen/glycolytic/oxidative percentage table for every sport and duration.

## ARTICLE 2 — Physiological Determinants of Aerobic Performance

VO2max is an important ceiling, not the whole of endurance performance. Performance also depends on sustainable fraction/threshold, economy, local muscular characteristics and event-specific skill. The Fick framework organizes central delivery and peripheral extraction: cardiac output, stroke volume, blood volume/oxygen delivery, capillary supply, mitochondrial/oxidative machinery and a-vO2 difference. [K7 Chs.5,8,11; M8 Chs.11,17,21]

Programming implications:
- Do not prescribe VO2max intervals simply because endurance is poor. Ask what failed in the target task: maximal aerobic power, sustainable fraction, late pace maintenance, local muscle fatigue, economy or technical execution.
- IF VO2max is clearly low for the event and athlete, THEN use training that meaningfully overloads oxygen delivery/utilization plus sufficient aerobic volume.
- IF VO2max is adequate but sustainable pace/power is poor, THEN investigate threshold/prolonged submaximal work before simply adding more maximal-aerobic intervals.
- IF general aerobic testing is good but target-modality performance is poor, THEN increase specific exposure and examine economy, skill and local muscular limitations.
- Cross-training can support central capacity but cannot be assumed to reproduce event economy, local oxidative adaptation or tissue tolerance.
- A lower-impact mode can preserve aerobic stimulus during impact limitation, but event-specific exposure remains necessary when the event is impact-based and tolerated.

Monitoring: improvement can appear as more external pace/power at the same or lower HR/RPE/oxygen cost. Do not automatically increase intensity after every easier session; interpret the external-output/internal-cost relationship.

Unresolved source gap: no single universal diagnostic test identifies one isolated central versus peripheral limiter in every athlete. Use converging indicators.

## ARTICLE 3 — Intensity Domains, Thresholds and Critical Power / Speed

Operational rule: threshold labels are useful only when their test/definition is known. LT1, VT1, LT2, VT2, MLSS, CP/CS, FTP and OBLA are related but must not be encoded as mathematically identical. [M8 Ch.14; K7 Chs.5,8; LB Chs.3,4,8]

Practical behavior model:
- Below the first threshold: relatively stable oxidative response, controlled breathing and long sustainable duration.
- Between the first threshold and upper sustainable boundary: higher metabolic strain/carbohydrate use with a steady response still possible at appropriate workloads.
- Above CP/CS or upper steady-state boundary: finite above-threshold capacity is expended, oxygen uptake may move toward VO2max and task failure becomes inevitable if maintained.
- Very high outputs add increasing phosphagen, glycolytic and neuromuscular demand; the supplied sources do not justify one hard universal extreme-domain boundary.

Prescription hierarchy: use the best available modality-specific anchor—measured speed/power, individual threshold test, RPE/breathing, HR, or a combination. Internal metrics are responses to external work, not interchangeable measures.

Decision rules:
IF LT1/VT1 is measured, THEN use that individual marker for low-intensity control rather than a population HR percentage.
IF CP/CS or another well-established upper sustainable marker is available, THEN use it to organize below-versus-above sustainable work without claiming equivalence to MLSS/VT2.
IF HR and external pace/power disagree during short intervals, THEN prioritize correct external work plus RPE because HR lags.
IF a threshold was measured in cycling but the event is running, THEN recalibrate in running before transferring the exact value.
IF no lab threshold exists, THEN use repeatable field performance, RPE/talk/ventilatory cues and trend data rather than fabricating a precise breakpoint.

## ARTICLE 4 — Low-Intensity Aerobic Training / Zone 2

Zone 2 is not one universal number because zone systems differ. Runtime concept: sustained low-intensity aerobic work clearly below the first major threshold (LT1/VT1 when known), allowing meaningful volume at lower acute recovery cost. [K7 Ch.11; M8 Chs.17,21; LB Ch.8]

Programming implications:
- Purpose is accumulated oxidative work, not fatigue.
- Preferred intensity hierarchy: individual LT1/VT1 or validated sport-specific anchor; then repeatable external pace/power plus RPE/breathing; HR is useful but imperfect.
- Cardiovascular drift means HR can rise during prolonged steady external work. Interpret HR with pace/power, RPE, heat, hydration and fatigue rather than in isolation.
- Choose a mode that permits stable low-intensity work without excessive local fatigue/impact.
- When event specificity matters, preserve enough target-modality work for economy and tissue tolerance. Cross-training can add aerobic volume but should not automatically replace specific exposure.
- Progress useful accumulated duration/frequency when current dose is well tolerated and more aerobic volume is actually useful. Recalibrate external pace/power as fitness improves.
- IF higher-intensity work becomes the priority, THEN hold low-intensity work nearer maintenance rather than expanding both blindly.
- Count easy sport practice and existing aerobic work before adding separate sessions.
- Reduce low-intensity volume when it degrades priority strength, power, technical or interval sessions.

Failure modes: universal 65-75% HRmax definition; easy work drifting harder; endless volume despite deteriorating recovery; HR-only prescription; replacing all specific endurance with convenient cross-training; assuming low intensity has zero recovery cost.

Unresolved source gap: no universal weekly Zone-2 dose for all endurance, strength or combat athletes.
