## ARTICLE 9 — Conditioning for Combat and Intermittent Sports

Combat conditioning should not be a punishment circuit. BJJ, MMA and boxing already create highly specific intermittent stress; supplemental work should target a quality sport practice does not develop efficiently enough while preserving technical/sparring quality. [LB Chs.8,9,11; K7 Ch.14]

Programming sequence:
- Map the actual sport week first: technical/light, moderate rounds and hard sparring.
- Identify the performance failure: overall pace loss, poor recovery between bursts, lost peak explosiveness, or technical inefficiency under fatigue.
- Decide whether the limitation is general or sport-specific. General aerobic capacity can be trained on a bike; grappling economy, grip coordination or striking efficiency require sport practice.
- Select the lowest-cost method that develops the missing quality. General modalities give control and often lower technical fatigue; sport-specific intervals increase transfer but reduce control of exact physiological dose.
- Place supplemental high-intensity work away from the most important sparring/technical sessions and reduce redundant general work as fight/competition practice becomes more intense and complete.
- Official round duration alone is not enough to prescribe intervals; burst/recovery structure inside the round matters.

Decision rules:
IF hard sparring already supplies repeated severe efforts, THEN count it as high-intensity conditioning and add formal hard intervals only for a remaining deficit.
IF the athlete is strong in early exchanges but fades across rounds, THEN assess aerobic support, pacing and repeat-effort recovery before adding more peak-power work.
IF pace is maintained but decisive explosive actions are weak, THEN prioritize strength/power/high-quality explosive exposure rather than simply adding conditioning volume.
IF a general modality can train the missing aerobic quality with less orthopedic/technical fatigue, THEN use it while preserving sport practice for specificity.
IF competition approaches and sport practice becomes more specific/intense, THEN reduce redundant generic high-intensity conditioning and preserve freshness.

Unresolved source gap: no universal work:rest ratio for every BJJ ruleset, boxing bout, MMA format, opponent/tactical style.

## ARTICLE 10 — Modality Translation: Running, Cycling, Rowing, Swimming and Multisport

Central aerobic physiology transfers across modes, but local muscular, mechanical, technical and measurement demands are not interchangeable. Transfer is strongest for central capacity and weaker where event economy, skill and tissue adaptation dominate. [K7 Chs.5,11; M8 Chs.10,11; LB Chs.6,8,14,16,19]

Running:
- Weight-bearing with meaningful eccentric/mechanical cost; generally more lower-body musculoskeletal/neuromuscular cost than cycling.
- Use pace/speed as primary external anchors, with RPE/HR as response/context where appropriate.
- Economy and tissue tolerance are event-specific. Cycling can supplement aerobic load but not fully replace running preparation.

Cycling:
- Lower eccentric impact than running and highly controllable by external power.
- Power meters provide precise workload; HR/RPE are responses, not substitutes for watts when power is available.
- High-power cycling can still create substantial quadriceps fatigue and interfere with lower-body strength.

Rowing:
- Large muscle mass and substantial lower/upper-body/back involvement; low impact does not mean low systemic or pulling fatigue.
- For short HIIT, prescribe rowing pace/power and stroke rate rather than HR alone because HR lags.
- Indoor ergometer work is controllable, while on-water performance also depends on technical/crew context.

Swimming:
- Low impact but technically dependent; body position, drag and stroke mechanics strongly affect economy.
- A weak swimmer may be technique-limited before the intended metabolic target is reached.
- Do not copy land HR anchors blindly into swimming.

Multisport/triathlon:
- Calibrate swim, bike and run separately. One HR/pace zone cannot simply be copied across modes.
- Allocate training according to event duration, transition/cumulative fatigue, current limiter and each discipline’s technical/local requirements.
- Longer formats emphasize sustainable output/pacing more than short-format high-speed/power demands, but detailed Ironman volume/fueling/periodization is beyond current source resolution.

Event translation:
- 3K/5K/10K-type running depends on a combination of VO2max, sustainable fraction/threshold, economy and event-specific speed. Shorter events require more high-speed/high-aerobic-power contribution; longer events place more weight on sustainable threshold/economy. Detailed race plans are a specialist-source gap.
- Marathon examples are low-intensity-volume dominant with smaller marathon-pace/faster work, but elite examples are NOT templates for hybrid athletes. Running-specific impact resilience, economy, fueling and specific volume matter.
- Rowing performance should use rowing-specific pace/power/stroke rate and can progress from general aerobic/threshold emphasis toward more race-specific intervals as competition approaches, without copying elite sample weeks universally.
- Long cycling events require sustainable cycling-specific power/duration plus appropriately selected high-intensity work; detailed road-cycling periodization is a source gap.

Decision rules:
IF running impact is the limiting cost but additional aerobic volume is useful, THEN supplement with cycling/swimming while retaining enough running for event economy/tissue preparation.
IF cycling interval precision matters, THEN use direct power as primary external prescription and HR/RPE as response markers.
IF rowing short HIIT is prescribed, THEN use rowing speed/power/stroke rate rather than HR to control each effort.
IF swim technique collapses before the metabolic target can be held, THEN treat technique/economy as a limiter rather than interpreting the session as pure aerobic work.
IF preparing for multisport, THEN create separate intensity anchors and sufficient specific exposure for each discipline.

Unresolved source gap: detailed high-resolution 3K/5K/10K/marathon, rowing-race, cycling-event and Ironman systems require specialist sources.

## ARTICLE 11 — Testing, Monitoring and the Conditioning Decision System

Testing is useful only when it changes a decision. No single test measures all conditioning qualities. Combine sport-specific performance, external workload, internal response and recovery trends; then use reassessment to update prescription. [K7 Chs.5,11,14; M8 Chs.11,21; LB Chs.2,8,9]

Testing principles:
- Prefer modality-specific testing for highly trained specialists when the result will prescribe sport-specific intensity.
- Field time trials, speed/power tests and sport-specific incremental tests can provide practical anchors when lab testing is unavailable.
- CP/CS can characterize a power-duration relationship where an appropriate source-supported protocol exists; do not improvise exact protocols beyond source support.
- HR is useful for steady aerobic work but lags during short intervals. Lactate can inform metabolic participation but is often impractical. Session RPE is a practical global internal-load tool but not a magical readiness score.
- External output tells what the athlete produced; internal response tells what it cost; recovery context helps explain why. Use all three.
- Reassess when enough training has accumulated for meaningful change and when the result would alter prescription. No universal “retest every 4 weeks” rule is supported.

THIRTEEN-STEP CONDITIONING DECISION SYSTEM:
1. Identify goal/event: duration, target pace/power, burst pattern, technical and mechanical demands.
2. Define physiological demands: aerobic capacity, sustainable threshold/fraction, economy, repeated-high-intensity recovery, peak power and glycolytic contribution as relevant.
3. Assess current performance using the most specific practical test/benchmark available.
4. Identify the likely limiter using multiple indicators rather than one score.
5. Identify recovery constraints: sport practice, strength training, sleep, schedule, impact/tissue tolerance and recent fatigue.
6. Select modality by balancing specificity with mechanical/local fatigue cost.
7. Select intensity domain using an individual/source-supported anchor: threshold, CP/CS, v/pVO2max, race pace/power, RPE/breathing/HR as appropriate.
8. Select session type: low-intensity continuous, threshold/sustainable work, long intervals, short intervals, repeated sprints, sprint intervals or sport-specific intervals.
9. Prescribe dose: intensity, work duration, relief, repetitions and weekly frequency. Do not fabricate universal volume rules.
10. Place session in the week to protect priority sport, strength, power and technical work from predictable fatigue.
11. Monitor execution by comparing prescribed versus actual external output and internal response.
12. Progress/regress one main variable according to adaptation and recovery.
13. Reassess when the result is meaningful/actionable and recalibrate.

Decision rules:
IF a test result will not change prescription, THEN do not test merely to collect data.
IF short-interval HR is below a nominal target despite correct speed/power and high RPE, THEN recognize HR lag and do not increase work solely to chase HR.
IF external performance improves at the same/lower internal cost, THEN treat that as positive adaptation and recalibrate targets when appropriate.
IF performance falls while training load, soreness, poor sleep and subjective fatigue rise together, THEN reduce/redistribute stress before concluding fitness has declined.
IF a block is complete or old zones no longer match performance, THEN reassess the relevant marker when the result will guide the next prescription.

## ENGINE-WIDE DECISION HIERARCHY

IF goal/event demands are not defined, THEN do not prescribe conditioning yet.
IF sport practice already provides a large amount of the target stress, THEN count it before adding supplemental conditioning.
IF two methods can create the desired adaptation, THEN choose the better combination of specificity, controllability and recovery cost for that athlete.
IF a numerical range comes from an elite sport example, THEN do not generalize it automatically to another athlete/sport.
IF a threshold term is used, THEN preserve its operational definition/test rather than assuming equivalence with every threshold label.
IF the athlete is improving while recovering well, THEN avoid unnecessary change and progress the smallest useful variable.
IF fatigue rises and priority performance falls, THEN reduce redundant volume/local overlap before adding more intensity.
IF a recommendation is not adequately supported by the current authored sources, THEN flag/avoid the unsupported precision rather than inventing a rule from generic model memory.
