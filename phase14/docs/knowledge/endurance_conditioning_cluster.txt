ARTICLE 1
Bioenergetics and Energy System Interaction
The useful coaching model is not three engines that switch on in sequence. It is continuous ATP turnover supplied by overlapping pathways whose relative contribution changes with intensity, duration, recovery, substrate availability, and training status.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
Muscle contraction requires a continuous supply of adenosine triphosphate, or ATP. Stored ATP is small, so muscular work depends on rapid ATP resynthesis. The three conventional pathways are the phosphagen system, glycolytic metabolism, and oxidative metabolism. They differ most importantly in rate and capacity: the fastest pathways can supply ATP at very high rates but cannot sustain that rate for long, while oxidative metabolism produces ATP more slowly but with far greater sustainable capacity. [K7, Ch. 2; M8, Chs. 5 to 7]
ATP and the phosphagen system
The ATP PCr system uses phosphocreatine to rapidly restore ATP from ADP. Its practical importance is maximal when force and power must be produced immediately, because it can respond without waiting for oxygen transport or a long sequence of metabolic reactions. Its limitation is finite intramuscular PCr availability. As repeated explosive work continues, the ability to restore PCr between efforts becomes increasingly important. [K7, Ch. 2; M8, Ch. 7]
Glycolytic metabolism
Glycolysis breaks carbohydrate down to provide ATP rapidly. As the required rate of ATP turnover rises, carbohydrate use rises and glycolytic flux becomes increasingly important. Pyruvate and lactate are not evidence that the oxidative system has switched off. Lactate formation is part of a dynamic system in which production, transport, oxidation, and reuse occur simultaneously. [K7, Ch. 2; M8, Chs. 6 to 7; LB, Ch. 3]
Oxidative metabolism
Oxidative ATP production occurs primarily in the mitochondria and uses carbohydrate and fat as major fuels. Oxygen delivery, mitochondrial capacity, capillary supply, and enzyme activity influence how much ATP can be generated aerobically. Oxidative metabolism dominates sustained work because it can continue at high total capacity, but it contributes from the beginning of exercise rather than appearing only after an arbitrary time point. [K7, Ch. 2; M8, Ch. 7]
Direct source principle: McArdle explicitly describes energy transfer as a smooth blending with considerable overlap rather than systems that switch on and off. Kenney likewise states that no exercise is 100 percent aerobic or 100 percent anaerobic. This is the base rule for the entire cluster.
2. Mechanisms: why contribution changes
The relative share of ATP supplied by each pathway changes because power demand and time constrain the system. Very high mechanical output requires a high ATP turnover rate, favoring the rapid phosphagen and glycolytic pathways. As effort duration increases, the finite stores and disturbance associated with very high anaerobic flux become limiting, while oxygen uptake rises and the oxidative system supplies an increasing fraction of total ATP. Trained athletes can reach a high oxidative rate more quickly at exercise onset, which reduces the relative oxygen deficit. [M8, Ch. 7]
The interaction becomes especially important in repeated effort sports. A short explosive action may rely heavily on ATP PCr, but the recovery between those actions depends substantially on oxidative metabolism. Therefore, a sequence of repeated 5 second efforts is not simply a repeated phosphagen problem. The ability to restore high energy phosphates and maintain output across the sequence depends on the recovery interval, aerobic capacity, local muscle characteristics, and how much fatigue is allowed to accumulate. [K7, Ch. 5; LB, Chs. 3 to 5]
Carbohydrate and fat
Both carbohydrate and fat can support oxidative ATP production, but intensity alters their relative use. Kenney presents the crossover concept: as exercise intensity rises, carbohydrate contributes more and fat contributes relatively less. Training status and nutritional state can shift this relationship. High intensity work also places greater demand on muscle glycogen because carbohydrate can support ATP production at rates that fat oxidation cannot match. [K7, Ch. 2; LB, Ch. 3]
Lactate production, oxidation, and reuse
Lactate is not metabolic garbage. Lactate can be transported to other fibers or tissues, converted back to pyruvate, oxidized as fuel, or contribute to gluconeogenesis. Blood lactate concentration reflects the balance between appearance and removal, not a binary transition from oxygen present to oxygen absent. The books differ in some older wording around lactic acid, acidity, and fatigue, so the safest synthesis is that high lactate is a marker of high glycolytic participation and turnover, while fatigue during intense work is multifactorial. [K7, Chs. 2 and 5; M8, Chs. 6, 7, and 14; LB, Ch. 3]
Source reconciliation: Kenney and McArdle both discuss H+ and acidity as possible contributors to fatigue in short intense exercise, while also rejecting lactate itself as a simple toxic cause. Laursen and Buchheit present competing interpretations of acidity. The engine should therefore avoid the rule “lactate causes fatigue.” It should treat fatigue as task specific and multifactorial.
3. Programming implications
Conditioning begins with the required output pattern, not with an energy system label. First identify the duration of each meaningful effort, how close it is to maximal output, how often it repeats, the recovery available, and the total event duration. Then choose training that reproduces or develops the limiting physiological quality without unnecessarily reproducing every stress of competition. [K7, Ch. 14; LB, Chs. 1, 3, and 4]
For very short maximal actions, preserve high power and adequate recovery when the objective is peak explosive ability. Do not turn a power session into glycolytic conditioning by shortening recovery unintentionally.
For sustained hard efforts, glycolytic and oxidative contributions rise. Interval duration and recovery should therefore be selected according to whether the goal is high aerobic power, high glycolytic stress, or sustainable performance.
For repeated explosive sports, train both the ability to produce a high quality action and the ability to recover enough to repeat it. The second problem is partly aerobic.
For long events, the oxidative system is the major energy source, but race surges, hills, finishing efforts, and starts can still demand substantial anaerobic contribution.
Do not equate a painful or high lactate session with a superior conditioning stimulus. The target is an adaptation, not discomfort.
4. Prescription variables
Intensity: the higher the required work rate, the greater the relative contribution from rapid ATP producing pathways and the greater the likely carbohydrate demand.
Work duration: increasing duration at a fixed high output increases oxidative contribution and eventually constrains the output that can be maintained.
Recovery duration and type: shorter recovery increases carryover of metabolic stress and can shift a session away from pure speed or power toward repeated effort conditioning.
Repetitions and total work: determine the accumulated exposure. More total work is not automatically better if quality or the intended physiological target deteriorates.
Modality: the same nominal interval structure can create different local muscular and mechanical stress in running, cycling, rowing, swimming, or sport drills.
5. Athlete modifiers
Training status changes oxygen uptake kinetics and the ability to tolerate or recover from a given external workload.
A better trained athlete may need a higher absolute workload to create the same internal metabolic stress.
Concurrent sport practice can already supply substantial glycolytic and neuromuscular stress. Supplemental conditioning should fill a missing quality rather than duplicate it.
Low impact modalities can add oxidative work with less mechanical cost when running impact or local tissue tolerance is a constraint.
Low glycogen availability can alter the quality of high intensity work. The source base supports high carbohydrate reliance during HIIT, but this cluster does not create a detailed sports nutrition prescription.
6. Failure modes
Using a rigid time chart that says one pathway owns each duration.
Calling an entire sport “aerobic” or “anaerobic” and ignoring its repeated changes in intensity.
Training a 5 second action with incomplete recovery when the intended quality is maximal power.
Adding repeated glycolytic sessions because the sport feels glycolytic, despite sport practice already providing that exposure.
Using blood lactate as a score to maximize rather than as information about metabolic participation.
Assuming that long easy training develops the same adaptations as short hard training, or that short hard training is a complete replacement for event specific endurance volume.
7. Decision rules
IF the event requires isolated maximal actions with generous recovery, THEN prioritize high output quality and sufficient recovery rather than deliberately accumulating metabolic fatigue
IF the event requires repeated maximal or near maximal actions with limited recovery, THEN develop both action quality and the oxidative recovery processes that support repeatability
IF the work duration increases while intensity remains high, THEN expect a larger glycolytic and oxidative contribution and choose interval structure accordingly
IF the sport already supplies frequent severe metabolic exposure, THEN do not automatically add more glycolytic conditioning; assess whether aerobic base, recovery, or another limiting quality is the better target
IF a coach is tempted to prescribe from a universal energy system percentage table, THEN use the table only as a rough teaching aid and base the actual session on event demand, athlete response, and modality
Source traceability
Direct source support: [K7] Ch. 2 on ATP, three energy systems, crossover, system interaction, and lactate as fuel; Ch. 5 on oxygen deficit, fatigue, and critical power. [M8] Chs. 5 to 7 on energy transfer and explicit overlap of systems. [LB] Ch. 3 on oxidative, glycolytic, and neuromuscular targets.
Source synthesis: The event demand to pathway to session sequence combines Kenney training specificity with the Laursen and Buchheit target based HIIT framework.
Unresolved source gap: The supplied texts do not justify one universal table of exact phosphagen, glycolytic, and oxidative percentages for every sport and duration. The engine must not fabricate one.

ARTICLE 2
Physiological Determinants of Aerobic Performance
Aerobic performance is not one number. VO2max sets an important ceiling, but performance also depends on how much of that ceiling can be sustained, how economically movement converts metabolic energy into speed or power, and whether oxygen delivery and local muscle use are matched to the event.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
VO2 is the rate of oxygen consumption. VO2max is the highest rate of oxygen uptake reached during maximal or exhaustive exercise and is a central marker of cardiorespiratory capacity. It is valuable, but it does not completely determine endurance performance. Athletes with similar VO2max values can perform very differently because of threshold, economy, local muscle characteristics, and event specific skill. [K7, Chs. 5, 8, and 11; M8, Chs. 11 and 17]
The Fick framework
The Fick equation organizes aerobic performance into central delivery and peripheral extraction: VO2 = cardiac output x arterial venous oxygen difference. Cardiac output is heart rate x stroke volume. This framework does not mean every athlete has one isolated bottleneck, but it gives the coach a disciplined way to ask whether the current limitation is primarily delivery, extraction, sustainable fraction, or movement economy. [M8, Ch. 17; K7, Ch. 11]
Coaching model: Use VO2max as a ceiling, threshold or sustainable fraction as the fraction of that ceiling available for prolonged work, and economy as the external speed or power obtained for a given metabolic cost. This is a source synthesis, not a claim that performance can be perfectly reduced to three variables.
2. Mechanisms
Central oxygen delivery
Endurance training can increase plasma and total blood volume, ventricular filling, stroke volume, maximal cardiac output, and the capacity to deliver blood to active muscle. Kenney describes increased capillarization, recruitment of existing capillaries, more effective blood flow redistribution, and increased total blood volume. McArdle likewise summarizes training induced increases in stroke volume and cardiac output. [K7, Ch. 11; M8, Chs. 17 and 21]
Peripheral oxygen extraction and mitochondrial function
The arterial venous oxygen difference expands during exercise as active tissue extracts more oxygen from blood. Endurance training increases mitochondrial size and number, oxidative enzyme content, capillary supply, and the ability to oxidize carbohydrate and fat. These changes increase the muscle’s capacity to receive and use oxygen. Kenney also presents evidence that mitochondrial oxidative capacity is associated with VO2max across people with widely different fitness levels, while emphasizing that oxygen supply remains an important limitation. [K7, Chs. 2 and 11; M8, Ch. 21]
Muscle fiber adaptations
Type I fibers possess high oxidative capacity, while type II fibers can produce higher force and power and differ in oxidative characteristics. Endurance training can increase oxidative machinery within recruited fibers and improve the ability to sustain aerobic ATP production. The practical point is not to assign an athlete a fixed fiber identity, but to recognize that the muscle recruited by the event must acquire sufficient oxidative capacity for the task. [K7, Chs. 1, 2, and 11; M8, Ch. 7]
Economy
Economy describes the metabolic cost of producing a given external speed or power. Kenney shows that two runners with nearly identical VO2max values can differ substantially in oxygen cost at the same running speed and therefore in race performance. Swimming makes the same point even more strongly because technique, drag, and effective force application greatly affect the energy required to move at a given velocity. [K7, Ch. 5; M8, Ch. 10]
3. Programming implications
The correct conditioning method depends on the suspected limiter. A program should not reflexively prescribe VO2max intervals every time endurance performance is poor. The coach should ask what changed or failed in the actual task: was maximal aerobic power low, was the sustainable fraction low, did pace collapse late, did local muscle fatigue dominate, or did economy deteriorate?
If VO2max is clearly low relative to the event and athlete level, use training that meaningfully overloads oxygen delivery and utilization, including appropriately selected interval work and sufficient aerobic volume.
If VO2max is adequate but sustainable pace is poor, threshold or prolonged submaximal work may be more relevant than simply increasing peak oxygen uptake.
If metabolic capacity appears adequate but event pace is poor, examine economy, technique, and modality specificity.
If the athlete has good general aerobic fitness but poor sport specific endurance, increase specific exposure because central fitness does not guarantee local muscular, technical, or mechanical transfer.
If sport practice already supplies substantial high intensity cardiovascular stress, lower intensity volume may provide additional peripheral adaptation at a lower recovery cost.
4. Prescription variables
Intensity determines which cardiovascular and metabolic limitations are stressed. Higher intensity can be necessary for trained athletes to overload VO2max, while lower intensity permits greater volume.
Duration and weekly volume determine accumulated oxidative exposure. More is useful only while adaptation and event preparation improve without excessive fatigue.
Frequency should be high enough to provide repeated exposure but must fit total training load. McArdle notes that the optimal frequency is not fixed and that total volume and intensity interact.
Mode should recruit the muscles and movement pattern relevant to the event when specificity matters. Cross training can support central capacity but cannot be assumed to reproduce event economy.
Progression can occur through more work at the intended intensity, a higher external pace or power at the same internal response, or more specific work as competition approaches.
5. Athlete modifiers
Initial fitness: untrained athletes often improve with a broad range of aerobic methods; trained athletes may require a stronger or more specific overload.
Body size and modality: absolute cardiac output and stroke volume are influenced by body size, so comparisons across athletes should be interpreted carefully.
Skill: technical sports such as swimming can show large changes in economy independent of VO2max.
Concurrent lifting: local muscular fatigue from endurance mode can affect the quality of strength training and vice versa.
Injury and impact tolerance: a lower impact modality can preserve aerobic stimulus when event specific impact cannot be tolerated, but some specific exposure is still needed when the event itself is impact based.
6. Failure modes
Treating VO2max as the whole of endurance performance.
Assuming that a higher heart rate during training necessarily means a better central adaptation.
Ignoring economy because laboratory aerobic capacity is high.
Using a non specific ergometer test as the only performance measure in a highly trained specialist athlete.
Prescribing the same interval method for a central delivery limitation, a threshold limitation, and an economy limitation.
Confusing an acute performance drop from fatigue with a true loss of aerobic fitness.
7. Decision rules
IF VO2max is low and the event strongly depends on sustained aerobic power, THEN select training that overloads oxygen delivery and utilization, then verify improvement with mode specific performance
IF VO2max is high but pace or power at submaximal effort remains poor, THEN investigate threshold, economy, and local muscular specificity before adding more maximal aerobic intervals
IF the athlete performs well on a general aerobic test but poorly in the target modality, THEN increase event specific training and assess technical or local muscular limitations
IF a low impact modality produces the desired cardiovascular load but the competition is running, THEN use the low impact mode to add volume while retaining enough running to develop running specific economy and tissue tolerance
IF the same external workload produces lower HR, lower RPE, or lower oxygen cost over time while performance is stable or better, THEN treat that as evidence of improved economy or reduced internal cost rather than automatically increasing intensity immediately
Source traceability
Direct source support: [M8] Ch. 17 for the Fick equation and oxygen extraction; Ch. 21 for central and peripheral training adaptations. [K7] Ch. 11 for stroke volume, blood volume, capillarization, and mitochondrial adaptations; Ch. 5 for economy and performance.
Source synthesis: The “ceiling, sustainable fraction, economy” coaching model integrates the books’ VO2max, threshold, and economy discussions.
Unresolved source gap: The three books do not provide a universal diagnostic test that can identify a single central versus peripheral limiter in every athlete. The engine should use multiple converging indicators rather than claim certainty from one test.

ARTICLE 3
Intensity Domains, Thresholds, and Critical Power / Speed
Threshold language is useful only if the terms are kept operationally distinct. The coaching engine should anchor training to observable physiological behavior and the test used, not assume LT1, LT2, VT1, VT2, MLSS, and critical power are interchangeable labels for the same number.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
The supplied sources support several related ways to divide exercise intensity. Kenney and McArdle discuss lactate threshold, ventilatory threshold, and critical power. Laursen and Buchheit describe first and second ventilatory or lactate turn points, critical power or speed, and maximal lactate steady state as practical anchors for training. However, the books do not provide a single rigorous modern taxonomy that proves all threshold terms are identical. [K7, Chs. 5 and 8; M8, Ch. 14; LB, Chs. 3 and 4]
Unresolved source gap: The requested four domain vocabulary, moderate, heavy, severe, and extreme, is not defined with enough precision across the supplied books to justify exact universal boundaries. The framework below uses a conservative source synthesis around first threshold and critical power / maximal lactate steady state, and it explicitly avoids inventing a precise extreme domain boundary.
Lactate threshold and ventilatory threshold
Blood lactate increases when the rate of appearance exceeds the rate of removal. This does not mark the first moment of anaerobic metabolism. McArdle specifically warns that the lactate threshold is not necessarily the threshold for anaerobic metabolism, and distinguishes lactate threshold from OBLA at a fixed 4 mmol/L. Kenney notes that ventilatory behavior can provide a reasonably close estimate of lactate threshold because ventilation changes as carbon dioxide buffering demands rise. [M8, Ch. 14; K7, Ch. 8]
First and second turn points
Laursen and Buchheit use VT1 / LT1 as a lower physiological demarcation and VT2 / a higher threshold region to organize intensity. Their five zone and three zone models are practical communication systems rather than proof that every athlete has the same percentage boundaries. They also note individual and activity specific variation in the relation between heart rate, lactate, and external work. [LB, Chs. 3 and 8]
Critical power and critical speed
Critical power is derived from the inverse relationship between sustainable power and duration. Kenney describes it as the asymptote of the power duration relationship and notes that it occurs somewhat above lactate threshold in the framework used there. Laursen and Buchheit describe CP as a sustainable intensity roughly in the 30 to 60 minute range and relate it to MLSS, while also presenting W prime as a finite work capacity above CP. These are useful but approximate relationships, not evidence that CP and MLSS are numerically identical in all athletes. [K7, Ch. 5; LB, Ch. 4]
2. Mechanisms and domain behavior
A practical source grounded model can be built from the behavior below. The labels are less important than the physiology.
Below the first threshold: oxidative metabolism can reach a stable response at a relatively low disturbance. Lactate appearance and removal remain well balanced, breathing is controlled, and long durations are possible.
Between the first threshold and the upper sustainable boundary: metabolic strain is higher, carbohydrate use rises, and the athlete can still achieve a form of steady response at appropriately selected workloads, but the recovery cost is greater.
Above critical power / speed or the upper steady state boundary: a finite capacity is progressively expended, oxygen uptake may move toward VO2max, metabolites and fatigue accumulate, and task failure becomes inevitable if the work rate is maintained.
At very high outputs: duration becomes extremely short and neuromuscular, phosphagen, and glycolytic demands become increasingly dominant. The supplied books support this intensity duration relationship but do not provide a sufficiently precise universal definition of the extreme domain to justify a hard boundary.
3. Programming implications
Intensity prescription should begin with the desired physiological behavior. The coach does not need to force every athlete into the same heart rate zone chart. Instead, use the best available anchor for the modality and goal: measured speed or power, an individual threshold test, RPE, breathing response, heart rate, or a combination. [M8, Ch. 21; LB, Chs. 2 and 8]
Low intensity volume should remain clearly below the first threshold when the objective is inexpensive oxidative work.
Threshold oriented work should be tied to an individually observed threshold or sustainable performance marker, not an assumed 4 mmol/L value. McArdle explicitly states that 4 mmol/L is not proven to be an ideal universal training concentration.
VO2max intervals sit above the upper sustainable boundary and should be structured to accumulate high quality time at a high oxygen uptake without requiring one specific protocol.
Repeated sprint and sprint interval work extends further into the high power end of the continuum and adds substantial glycolytic and neuromuscular demands.
For field sports and combat sports, intensity may fluctuate too rapidly for heart rate to prescribe individual bursts. External work and RPE become more useful.
4. Prescription variables
External intensity: running speed, cycling power, rowing speed or power, swim pace, or sport specific work rate.
Internal intensity: heart rate, RPE, breathing, and lactate where practical. These are responses to the external work, not interchangeable measures.
Duration: determines whether a target intensity can be maintained long enough to create the intended response.
Recovery: changes how much physiological strain carries into the next repetition.
Environment and modality: can change heart rate, pace, economy, and local fatigue at the same nominal zone.
5. Athlete modifiers
Training shifts the external speed or power associated with a given physiological threshold.
A fixed percentage of HRmax can misclassify intensity because threshold location varies between athletes.
Running, cycling, rowing, and swimming can have different threshold outputs and heart rate responses in the same athlete.
Fatigue, heat, dehydration, altitude, and glycogen status can change the internal response to an external workload.
Experienced athletes can often use RPE more effectively, while objective external anchors can improve control when precise pacing matters.
6. Failure modes
Treating LT1, VT1, LT2, VT2, MLSS, CP, FTP, and OBLA as mathematically identical.
Using a generic percentage of maximum heart rate as if it identifies an individual threshold perfectly.
Calling any comfortable session “Zone 2” without defining the zoning model.
Using heart rate to control very short intervals despite response lag.
Testing one modality and transferring the exact threshold output to another modality without verification.
Turning threshold training into repeated severe domain efforts because the prescribed pace is based on an outdated test.
7. Decision rules
IF the athlete has a measured first threshold, THEN use that individual marker as the main anchor for low intensity prescription rather than a population heart rate percentage
IF the athlete has a measured CP / critical speed or a well established upper sustainable performance marker, THEN use it to organize work below versus above the sustainable boundary, while recognizing that CP is not automatically identical to MLSS or VT2
IF heart rate and external pace disagree during short intervals, THEN prioritize the prescribed external work and RPE because heart rate has substantial response lag
IF the threshold marker was established in cycling but the target event is running, THEN retest or calibrate in running before treating the cycling value as a running prescription
IF no laboratory threshold data are available, THEN use repeatable field performance, RPE, talk or ventilatory cues, and trend data rather than fabricate a precise physiological breakpoint
Source traceability
Direct source support: [M8] Ch. 14 distinguishes LT from OBLA and rejects the onset of anaerobic metabolism interpretation. [K7] Ch. 8 relates ventilatory and lactate thresholds; Ch. 5 discusses critical power. [LB] Chs. 3, 4, and 8 organize intensity around VT1, VT2, CP, MLSS, and practical zone models.
Source synthesis: The below first threshold, between thresholds, and above CP behavioral model is a cautious integration of the three source frameworks.
Unresolved source gap: The supplied sources do not adequately standardize the modern four domain taxonomy or prove equivalence among LT2, VT2, MLSS, and CP. Exact domain boundaries should not be encoded as universal facts.

ARTICLE 4
Low Intensity Aerobic Training / Zone 2
Zone 2 is useful only after it is defined. For programming, the physiologically important idea is sustained low intensity aerobic work below the first major threshold, performed at a workload that can accumulate meaningful volume without creating the recovery cost of harder training.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
“Zone 2” is not one universal physiological number because zone systems differ. Laursen and Buchheit explicitly show both three zone and five zone models and note that standardized heart rate and lactate zones do not fully account for individual or activity specific variation. For this cluster, low intensity aerobic training is anchored conceptually below the first lactate or ventilatory turn point rather than to one fixed percentage of HRmax. [LB, Ch. 8]
Terminology rule: Never prescribe “Zone 2” without stating which zone model or physiological anchor is being used. In this cluster, the preferred engine concept is low intensity aerobic work below LT1 / VT1 when that boundary is known.
2. Mechanisms
Low intensity endurance work stresses oxygen delivery and local oxidative metabolism while allowing substantially more total work than high intensity intervals. Endurance training is associated with increased plasma volume, stroke volume, capillarization, mitochondrial content, oxidative enzyme activity, and improved fat oxidation. Not every adaptation is unique to low intensity training, but low intensity work is an efficient way to accumulate the volume that supports these adaptations with relatively low acute metabolic disturbance. [K7, Ch. 11; M8, Ch. 21]
Central and peripheral adaptation
The central side includes changes in blood volume, ventricular filling, stroke volume, and the ability to deliver blood to active muscle. The peripheral side includes capillary supply, oxygen extraction, mitochondrial content, and oxidative enzymes. Because exercise mode determines which muscles perform the work, local peripheral adaptation is more specific than general cardiovascular strain. [K7, Ch. 11; M8, Ch. 17]
Cardiovascular drift
During prolonged exercise, heart rate can rise despite unchanged external work. McArdle describes cardiovascular drift as part of prolonged exercise physiology. Therefore, a rigid heart rate ceiling can force pace or power progressively downward even when the original workload remains appropriate, while chasing a fixed pace in heat or fatigue can push internal stress upward. The coach should interpret HR together with pace, power, RPE, and environmental context rather than in isolation. [M8, Ch. 17]
3. Programming implications
The purpose of low intensity work is not to make the athlete tired. It is to accumulate enough high quality oxidative work to improve aerobic capacity and support recovery between harder efforts while preserving the rest of the training plan. A strength or combat athlete often values this low recovery cost more than an endurance specialist, because the weekly fatigue budget is shared with lifting and sport practice.
Use a modality that allows the athlete to hold a stable low intensity response without excessive local muscle fatigue or impact.
When event specificity matters, retain enough event specific work to build economy and tissue tolerance. Cross training can add aerobic volume but cannot be assumed to replace the target movement completely.
Progress by increasing useful accumulated work before forcing intensity upward. Once the session drifts into a higher domain, it is no longer the same training stimulus.
Reduce low intensity volume when it begins to reduce the quality of priority strength, power, technical, or interval sessions. More volume is not automatically more adaptation.
For athletes already doing large sport volumes, count easy sport practice and other aerobic work before adding separate sessions.
4. Prescription variables
Intensity prescription
Preferred hierarchy: individual LT1 / VT1 or a validated sport specific low intensity anchor when available; then repeatable external pace or power plus RPE and breathing; then heart rate as a useful but imperfect guide. McArdle supports RPE and talk test as practical tools, while Laursen and Buchheit emphasize that HR is an internal response and can lag or vary with context. [M8, Ch. 21; LB, Ch. 8]
Duration and frequency
The books support the interaction among intensity, duration, frequency, and initial fitness rather than one universal athlete dose. McArdle states that no single threshold duration exists for optimal aerobic improvement and that more time does not necessarily produce greater improvement in already active people. Kenney likewise emphasizes the intensity volume interaction. Therefore, the engine should increase duration or frequency only when the athlete is recovering and performance data justify more volume. [M8, Ch. 21; K7, Ch. 14]
Progression
Establish a stable low intensity workload that is clearly below the first threshold.
Build repeatability. The athlete should finish with the ability to recover for the rest of the training week.
Increase accumulated duration or frequency when the present dose is well tolerated and a larger aerobic volume is useful.
Recalibrate external pace or power as fitness improves, because the workload associated with the same internal response should rise.
If higher intensity work becomes the current priority, hold low intensity training at maintenance rather than continuing to expand both.
5. Athlete modifiers
Endurance specialist: can tolerate and often requires much greater specific low intensity volume than a strength or combat athlete.
Strength / hypertrophy priority: favor low mechanical cost modes when extra leg fatigue would compromise lifting.
Combat sport priority: count mat time and hard sparring before adding extra conditioning; low intensity work is most useful when it adds aerobic volume without duplicating hard exchanges.
Poor running tolerance: cycling or swimming can add oxidative volume, with running reintroduced according to event needs and tissue tolerance.
Technical mode: a novice swimmer may be limited by technique before the desired metabolic load is reached; a bike can be easier to dose accurately for general aerobic work.
6. Failure modes
Defining Zone 2 as a universal 65 to 75 percent of maximum heart rate.
Letting easy sessions drift into moderate or threshold work because the athlete feels good.
Adding low intensity volume indefinitely while strength, sport, sleep, or hard session quality deteriorates.
Using only heart rate and ignoring external pace, power, heat, fatigue, and drift.
Replacing all event specific endurance with a convenient cross training mode.
Assuming low intensity means no recovery cost. Large volumes still create systemic and local fatigue.
7. Decision rules
IF LT1 / VT1 is known, THEN keep low intensity aerobic work below that marker rather than using an arbitrary population heart rate percentage
IF the athlete cannot tolerate additional running impact but needs more aerobic volume, THEN add a lower impact modality while preserving the amount of running needed for event specificity
IF pace or power is stable but HR drifts gradually upward during a long session, THEN interpret the change in the context of duration, heat, hydration, fatigue, and RPE rather than immediately declaring that the athlete left the intended domain
IF additional low intensity volume reduces priority session quality, THEN reduce low intensity duration or frequency before adding more conditioning stress
IF low intensity work becomes easier at the same external workload, THEN recalibrate pace or power while keeping the physiological intent unchanged
Source traceability
Direct source support: [K7] Ch. 11 for aerobic adaptations; Ch. 14 for intensity and volume interaction. [M8] Chs. 17 and 21 for cardiovascular drift, aerobic training variables, RPE, talk test, and non universal duration. [LB] Ch. 8 for three and five zone models and the limitations of standardized zones.
Source synthesis: The preferred definition of Zone 2 as “low intensity work below the first threshold” is a practical reconciliation of the source frameworks.
Unresolved source gap: The books do not support one universal weekly Zone 2 dose for strength athletes, grapplers, or all endurance athletes. The engine should scale volume from goal, baseline, sport load, and recovery response.

ARTICLE 5
VO2max Development and Interval Training
There is no single best HIIT protocol. The correct interval structure is the one that creates the desired oxidative stress, permits enough high quality accumulated work, fits the athlete’s modality and training status, and does not consume more recovery than the adaptation is worth.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
HIIT is a family of interval structures, not one workout. Laursen and Buchheit organize the main tools into long intervals, short intervals, repeated sprints, sprint intervals, and game based formats. For VO2max development, the primary question is how to create a large enough oxygen delivery and utilization stimulus, often by accumulating time at a very high fraction of VO2max, without allowing the session to collapse into poor quality work. [LB, Chs. 1, 4, and 5]
Why time near VO2max matters
Laursen and Buchheit treat time accumulated at or near VO2max as an important acute target for sessions designed to stress maximal aerobic power. They emphasize that the time required to reach very high VO2 varies substantially among athletes and depends on intensity, mode, training status, warm up, and oxygen uptake kinetics. Therefore, a fixed work interval duration cannot guarantee the same physiological exposure in every athlete. [LB, Ch. 5]
Direct source caution: Laursen and Buchheit report large between athlete variability in the time needed to approach VO2max. This is one reason the engine must not encode “4 x 4” or any other interval format as the universal VO2max session.
2. Mechanisms
Intervals can stress maximal cardiac output, stroke volume, oxygen delivery, local oxygen extraction, mitochondrial metabolism, and high rates of carbohydrate oxidation. Higher intensity also increases glycolytic and neuromuscular stress. The objective is therefore not merely to raise heart rate. It is to manipulate work and recovery so the desired physiological systems remain highly engaged for enough accumulated time while mechanical quality remains acceptable. [LB, Chs. 3 to 5]
Long intervals
Long intervals make it easier for oxygen uptake to rise toward a high steady value within each repetition, but the work intensity must be sustainable enough to preserve multiple repetitions. Laursen and Buchheit note that repeated long intervals often use intensities around or above the speed or power associated with VO2max, with lower percentages than an isolated time to exhaustion effort because successive intervals and the slow component raise oxygen uptake. Their sport chapters commonly use work periods in the range of minutes, but the exact prescription is sport and athlete dependent. [LB, Chs. 5 and 14 to 19]
Short intervals
Short intervals can also accumulate high oxygen uptake because recovery is incomplete and oxygen consumption stays elevated across repetitions. A warm up and early repetitions can “prime” oxygen uptake. Short intervals may allow higher external speed or power and can manipulate mechanical and neuromuscular stress differently from long intervals. [LB, Ch. 5]
3. Programming implications
Choose long intervals when the athlete can sustain high aerobic power for several minutes with good pacing and the desired mechanical pattern.
Choose short intervals when higher external speed or power, lower continuous discomfort, or a different neuromuscular profile is useful, while still allowing oxygen uptake to remain high across the set.
Use repeated sprint or sprint interval formats only when their extra glycolytic and neuromuscular stress matches the objective. They are not automatically superior VO2max tools.
For team and combat sports, game or skill based intervals can increase specificity but reduce control over the exact physiological dose. Use controlled modalities when individual calibration is the priority.
For trained athletes, the source base supports the need for sufficiently high intensity to create new overload. Simply adding more moderate work may not raise VO2max once the athlete is well trained.
4. Prescription variables
Work intensity
Laursen and Buchheit recommend high fractions of v/pVO2max for VO2max targeted work, but the exact percentage depends on interval duration, training status, and whether the effort is isolated or repeated. For repeated long intervals, values around 90 percent or more of v/pVO2max are commonly used in their examples. This should be treated as source context, not a universal law. [LB, Ch. 5]
Work duration and recovery
If the first interval is shorter than the athlete’s time to reach VO2max, high VO2 can still be reached later through accumulated oxygen uptake and priming. Work to relief ratios greater than 1 are highlighted by Laursen and Buchheit as useful when maximizing time at VO2max is the main goal. However, recovery must still be long enough to preserve the intended external output. [LB, Ch. 5]
Accumulated work
The source proposes approximate targets of several minutes at VO2max, with longer accumulated exposure often sought in endurance athletes than team sport athletes. These are practitioner recommendations, not hard physiological thresholds. The engine should use them as contextual reference points and stop or modify the session when pace, power, mechanics, or intended internal response deteriorate materially. [LB, Ch. 5]
Progression and overload
Start with an interval format the athlete can execute with stable output and technique.
Increase useful accumulated work or reduce unnecessary recovery only if the intended physiological target is still reached.
Increase pace or power when the same structure becomes clearly submaximal and the athlete can preserve quality.
Progress specificity as the event approaches if the target performance depends on a specific movement or race pace.
Do not increase intensity, interval duration, density, and total repetitions simultaneously.
5. Athlete modifiers
Training status: trained athletes can have faster oxygen kinetics and may tolerate greater external workloads, yet they can also need a stronger stimulus to improve VO2max.
Mode: running often reaches oxygen uptake faster than cycling in the source discussion; external speeds and powers cannot be transferred directly between modalities.
Anaerobic speed or power reserve: athletes with the same v/pVO2max can experience different relative neuromuscular and anaerobic stress at the same supramaximal percentage.
Concurrent strength or sport: hard intervals may compromise later strength, power, or technical work, so the desired adaptation must justify the recovery cost.
Skill demand: game based HIIT may be useful when technical and tactical specificity is wanted, but a controlled ergometer may be preferable when precise metabolic targeting is needed.
6. Failure modes
Treating 4 x 4 min, 30/30, Tabata, or any other format as the best protocol for everyone.
Using heart rate alone to prescribe short work intervals.
Chasing the highest possible intensity and losing accumulated quality work.
Using all out sprint intervals for an athlete whose main need is high aerobic power with limited neuromuscular cost.
Adding interval volume after output or mechanics have already deteriorated substantially.
Ignoring the hard interval exposure already contained in sport practice.
7. Decision rules
IF the goal is VO2max development and the athlete tolerates several minute efforts well, THEN consider long intervals that allow sustained high oxygen uptake and repeatable external output
IF the athlete cannot maintain high quality long intervals but can repeat shorter bouts while keeping oxygen uptake elevated, THEN use short intervals with recovery short enough to preserve the aerobic carryover but long enough to maintain mechanics
IF the athlete’s sport already supplies repeated sprint and glycolytic stress, THEN prefer a controlled aerobic power interval format unless repeated sprint qualities are independently deficient
IF the athlete reaches the target physiological stress but power or technique collapses early, THEN reduce interval duration, total work, or session density rather than adding more repetitions
IF the same interval session becomes easier while pace or power and quality are stable, THEN progress one variable at a time, usually accumulated work or external intensity, and recheck the response
Source traceability
Direct source support: [LB] Chs. 3 to 5 for physiological targets, work and recovery manipulation, time at VO2max, and interval formats; sport chapters for examples. [K7] Chs. 9 and 11 for HIIT adaptation and the need for high intensity in trained athletes. [M8] Ch. 21 for the four basic interval prescription variables.
Source synthesis: The selection algorithm prioritizes target, accumulated quality work, mode, and recovery cost rather than a named protocol.
Unresolved source gap: The books do not provide a validated universal rule for the exact minutes at VO2max required for adaptation in every athlete. Source ranges should be treated as practitioner targets, not biological thresholds.

ARTICLE 6
Anaerobic Conditioning and Repeated High Intensity Performance
Peak sprint ability, glycolytic capacity, and repeated effort recovery are different problems. A session should be designed around which one is limiting rather than using “anaerobic conditioning” as a single category.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
Repeated high intensity performance depends on the interaction of ATP PCr availability, glycolytic ATP production, oxidative metabolism, neuromuscular output, and recovery between efforts. Repeated sprint training and sprint interval training deliberately manipulate this interaction. Laursen and Buchheit define repeated sprint sequences as more than two short all out efforts, typically 10 seconds or less, separated by short recovery, and distinguish these from longer all out sprint interval work commonly lasting tens of seconds. [LB, Chs. 2 and 5]
Peak sprint ability versus repeatability
If the goal is peak sprint speed or power, the athlete needs enough recovery to preserve maximal output. If the goal is repeatability, recovery is intentionally limited so the athlete must restore PCr, maintain oxidative contribution, and tolerate accumulating metabolic and neuromuscular stress. These are not the same session even when the work interval duration is identical. [LB, Ch. 5]
2. Mechanisms
PCr depletion and resynthesis
High power efforts rapidly use phosphocreatine. Recovery between efforts allows PCr to be restored through oxidative processes. Therefore, aerobic fitness contributes to repeated sprint performance even when each individual sprint is heavily phosphagen dependent. [K7, Chs. 2 and 5; LB, Ch. 5]
Glycolytic contribution
As sprint duration increases or recovery becomes shorter, glycolytic contribution and blood lactate commonly rise. Laursen and Buchheit show that repeated sprint format, sprint duration, recovery duration, active versus passive recovery, and change of direction can substantially alter lactate accumulation. This means “6 x sprint” is not a sufficient prescription. The entire work relief structure determines the metabolic target. [LB, Ch. 5]
Fatigue is not one variable
Performance decrement across repeated sprints reflects multiple mechanisms, including local metabolic disturbance, impaired force production, altered movement mechanics, and neural adjustments. Laursen and Buchheit caution that percentage speed decrement has poor reliability and does not fully represent musculoskeletal strain. Kenney likewise describes fatigue as task specific and multifactorial. [K7, Ch. 5; LB, Ch. 5]
3. Programming implications
Peak sprint development: maximize repetition quality, use long enough recovery to prevent large output decline, and stop before the session turns into metabolic conditioning.
Repeated sprint ability: use short all out efforts with incomplete recovery when the sport requires repeated explosive actions and the athlete’s repeatability is a real limiter.
Glycolytic capacity / tolerance: longer high intensity bouts or denser work can create larger glycolytic stress, but this is costly and should be used only when the sport and athlete profile justify it.
Recovery ability: lower intensity aerobic training and appropriate interval structures can improve the oxidative processes that restore output between hard efforts.
Sport specific conditioning: combat drills can target the same systems while adding movement specificity, but they make the exact intensity dose less controllable.
4. Prescription variables
Sprint duration: very short all out efforts bias the high energy phosphate and neuromuscular end; longer efforts increase glycolytic contribution.
Recovery duration: shorter recovery increases metabolic carryover and reduces restoration of peak output.
Recovery intensity: active recovery changes oxygen uptake and lactate dynamics compared with passive recovery.
Repetition count: sets the accumulated exposure and should be constrained by the acceptable loss of output and movement quality.
Change of direction and added tasks: increase neuromuscular and metabolic demand and make simple distance comparisons misleading.
Mode: bike sprinting can reduce impact and technical running demand, while running or sport drills may provide greater movement specificity.
5. Athlete modifiers
An athlete with high peak power but poor repeatability may need recovery focused conditioning more than more peak sprint work.
An athlete with low peak power cannot solve the problem by becoming better at repeating mediocre outputs; maximal speed or power development remains necessary.
A well conditioned athlete may show less performance decline in repeated sprint work, but the same protocol can still create different neuromuscular stress between individuals.
Combat athletes already obtain repeated high intensity exposure from sparring and drilling. Supplemental work should target a gap rather than reproduce hard rounds by default.
When joint or tendon load is high, a cycle ergometer can create severe metabolic stress without the same running impact, but this reduces movement specificity.
6. Failure modes
Using incomplete recovery for peak power development and then interpreting falling output as useful fatigue.
Using long glycolytic intervals year round because the sport feels hard.
Maximizing blood lactate instead of targeting performance adaptation.
Using percentage speed decrement as the only fatigue or quality criterion.
Adding repeated sprint training on top of multiple hard sparring sessions without accounting for total high intensity load.
Assuming a short sprint is purely alactic or that a long sprint is purely glycolytic.
7. Combat sport translation
BJJ, MMA, and boxing combine short explosive actions with longer periods of movement, positional work, and repeated exchanges. The oxidative system supports the ability to recover between bursts and across rounds, while high energy phosphate and glycolytic pathways support high impulse actions. The practical mistake is to see a hard round and conclude that supplemental conditioning should simply imitate the hardest part of the round. The coach must first determine whether the athlete lacks peak explosive output, the ability to repeat it, sustainable aerobic support, or sport specific technical efficiency. [LB, Ch. 11]
8. Decision rules
IF the goal is maximal sprint speed or power, THEN use enough recovery to preserve near maximal output and terminate the set when meaningful quality loss appears
IF the goal is repeated sprint ability, THEN use short all out efforts with deliberately incomplete recovery and monitor repeatability rather than only peak output
IF the goal is high glycolytic capacity and the sport genuinely requires sustained high intensity bursts, THEN use longer or denser high intensity work sparingly because the fatigue cost is high
IF hard sparring already supplies frequent glycolytic and neuromuscular exposure, THEN bias supplemental conditioning toward missing aerobic or repeatability qualities rather than duplicating hard rounds
IF the athlete’s repeatability improves but peak sprint output remains low, THEN shift emphasis toward speed, power, or strength rather than adding more conditioning density
Source traceability
Direct source support: [LB] Chs. 2 and 5 for RST / SIT definitions, oxygen uptake, lactate, neuromuscular responses, and manipulation of sprint and recovery duration; Ch. 11 for combat sport application. [K7] Chs. 2, 5, and 11 for phosphagen, fatigue, and anaerobic training adaptations.
Source synthesis: The separation of peak sprint ability, repeated sprint ability, and glycolytic capacity is the main coaching architecture of this article.
Unresolved source gap: The books do not provide one universal acceptable percentage drop in sprint performance for terminating all repeated sprint sessions. Quality thresholds should be sport and athlete specific.

ARTICLE 7
Programming Endurance Training
A conditioning block is an allocation problem. The coach must distribute a finite weekly dose across low intensity volume, threshold or sustainable work, high aerobic power, and sport specific demands while preserving enough recovery for adaptation and the athlete’s other priorities.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
Training adaptation depends on overload, specificity, and recovery. Intensity and volume interact: lower intensity permits more volume, while high intensity creates a large training load quickly. Kenney emphasizes that excessive high intensity volume can deplete glycogen and contribute to maladaptation, while Laursen and Buchheit show that training load must be interpreted from both external and internal perspectives. [K7, Chs. 9 and 14; LB, Ch. 8]
Intensity distribution
The supplied sources support the broad observation that endurance athletes generally perform a large proportion of training at low intensity and a smaller proportion at high intensity. Kenney describes research suggesting high low intensity volume, substantial moderate work, and less high intensity work. Laursen and Buchheit describe polarized distributions in several elite endurance sport examples, including an approximately 80 / 20 pattern in marathon running. These examples should not be converted into a universal rule for every sport or hybrid athlete. [K7, Ch. 14; LB, Ch. 14]
Unresolved source gap: The prompt asks for polarized, pyramidal, and other models. The source base supports low intensity dominant and polarized examples, but does not provide enough direct evidence in the retrieved material to encode a universal pyramidal model or declare one distribution superior. The engine should preserve this uncertainty.
2. Mechanisms and adaptation timelines
Different adaptations occur on different time courses. Blood volume can respond relatively quickly, while structural and mitochondrial adaptations accumulate over repeated weeks of training. The books also show that maintaining a fitness quality often requires less frequency or duration than developing it, while reducing intensity too far can reduce VO2max. These observations support block based programming in which one quality receives a progression dose and other qualities are maintained rather than all qualities being maximized simultaneously. [K7, Ch. 11; M8, Ch. 21]
Coaching inference: The “one quality progresses while others are maintained” rule is a conservative programming inference from the sources’ specificity, intensity volume interaction, maintenance versus development differences, and fatigue constraints. It is not stated as a universal law in the three books.
3. Building a conditioning block from scratch
Define the performance target. Specify event duration, required pace or power, repeated effort pattern, and mechanical constraints.
Audit the training already present. Count sport practice, races, hard sparring, lifting, and other conditioning before adding sessions.
Identify the most plausible limiting quality: aerobic base, upper sustainable intensity, VO2max, repeated sprint ability, peak power, economy, or sport specific execution.
Choose a primary adaptation for the block. Maintain secondary qualities with the minimum dose that preserves them.
Choose the modality according to specificity and fatigue cost.
Set the weekly hard session budget before filling in easy volume. High intensity sessions have disproportionate fatigue cost.
Add low intensity volume to the extent that it supports the goal and is recoverable.
Place sessions so priority work is performed with adequate freshness.
Progress one main variable at a time and monitor both external output and internal response.
Reassess at a meaningful block boundary or when performance and recovery trends show that the current plan is no longer producing the intended adaptation.
4. Frequency, volume, and progression
There is no single optimal weekly frequency in the source base. McArdle reports that two versus more weekly sessions can sometimes produce similar VO2max changes when total exercise volume is controlled, and notes that frequency matters less than intensity and duration in some studies. Therefore, frequency should be a way of distributing the required dose, not an isolated target. [M8, Ch. 21]
Increase frequency when it improves distribution of useful work without degrading session quality or recovery.
Increase duration when additional low or moderate intensity volume is needed and the athlete tolerates it.
Increase intensity when the current workload no longer overloads the target quality, especially in trained athletes.
Reduce volume before important competition while preserving enough intensity to maintain performance qualities. McArdle describes successful taper models that reduce volume substantially while maintaining intensity, but the exact taper should remain sport and athlete specific.
Avoid simultaneous large increases in frequency, duration, and intensity.
5. Hard / easy organization and sequencing
Hard training is expensive because it combines high internal stress with high glycogen use and often greater neuromuscular fatigue. A practical weekly plan therefore benefits from clear differentiation between hard and easy work. Sport practice must be included in that classification. A “conditioning day” is not easy if it follows or precedes a hard sparring session that taxes the same qualities.
Place the highest priority interval or race specific work when the athlete is sufficiently fresh to execute it.
Use easy aerobic work on days where additional volume is useful but another severe stress would reduce recovery.
Cluster or separate hard stresses according to the athlete’s schedule, but protect the next key performance session from predictable local fatigue.
As competition approaches, shift from general to specific exercise and reduce nonessential volume rather than trying to retain maximum training load.
6. Athlete modifiers
Endurance specialist: can allocate most of the week to sport specific aerobic volume because endurance is the primary outcome.
Combat athlete: sport practice already supplies variable high intensity work and technical fatigue, so supplemental conditioning must be smaller and more targeted.
Strength or hypertrophy athlete: conditioning volume and modality should be constrained by lower body recovery and the priority of resistance training.
Novice: a simple three zone model and broad aerobic training can be sufficient; excessive complexity adds little value.
Advanced athlete: needs more precise external load targets, event specificity, and surveillance because small programming errors can consume a large recovery budget.
7. Failure modes
Programming every physiological quality hard in the same week.
Treating an 80 / 20 distribution as a universal law instead of a sport specific example.
Increasing training load only by adding sessions.
Running multiple severe interval days while also performing hard sport practice and calling the total plan “balanced.”
Deloading by removing all intensity and therefore detraining the quality the taper is meant to express.
Changing sessions randomly before enough repeated data exist to judge adaptation.
8. Decision rules
IF one conditioning quality is the clear limiter, THEN give that quality the progression dose and hold secondary conditioning qualities closer to maintenance
IF sport practice already contains multiple hard days, THEN count them inside the hard session budget before adding formal HIIT
IF the athlete is improving and recovery remains stable, THEN progress the smallest variable that increases target exposure rather than redesigning the whole block
IF performance is stable but fatigue trends upward, THEN reduce nonessential volume first and preserve the most specific or high priority quality
IF competition approaches, THEN reduce general volume and increase specificity while maintaining the intensity needed to preserve performance
Source traceability
Direct source support: [K7] Chs. 9 and 14 for overload, specificity, intensity volume interaction, periodization, and distribution of intensity. [M8] Ch. 21 for frequency, duration, maintenance, and taper. [LB] Chs. 8 and 9 for load monitoring and sport chapters for intensity distribution examples.
Source synthesis: The block building sequence combines source training principles with the HIIT target and surveillance model.
Unresolved source gap: No universal weekly volume, number of hard sessions, polarized percentage, or deload schedule is supported for all athlete types. These must be individualized from performance and recovery data.

ARTICLE 8
Concurrent Strength + Endurance Training
Concurrent training is not automatically incompatible. The practical problem is to protect the quality that matters most from accumulated volume, local fatigue, neuromuscular strain, and poorly timed sessions.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
Laursen and Buchheit describe the concurrent training literature as conflicting. Some studies show an interference effect and others do not. When interference is observed, maximal strength, power, and hypertrophy can be more vulnerable than endurance performance. Strength training, in contrast, generally does not reduce endurance adaptation and can improve aspects of endurance performance. [LB, Ch. 6]
Source rule: Do not tell an athlete that cardio automatically “kills gains.” The source base supports a conditional interference model in which risk rises with certain combinations of volume, frequency, modality, local overlap, and insufficient recovery.
2. Mechanisms
Fatigue versus molecular interference
The practical evidence in the sources strongly supports acute fatigue as an important mechanism. Endurance work can reduce later force production, alter running mechanics, and deplete glycogen. High volume or high frequency endurance training may therefore reduce the quality of strength and power work. Molecular signaling explanations are discussed in the literature, but McArdle also presents evidence that resistance exercise after endurance exercise can amplify mitochondrial biogenesis signaling rather than simply block it. This prevents a simplistic molecular incompatibility story. [LB, Ch. 6; M8, Ch. 22]
Volume and frequency
Laursen and Buchheit summarize evidence that greater weekly endurance frequency and duration are associated with more interference, while lower endurance frequencies often show little or no interference. They explicitly state that more work is needed to define the dose relationship. The safe programming implication is therefore directional: as strength, power, or hypertrophy priority rises, unnecessary endurance volume should fall. [LB, Ch. 6]
Modality overlap
Running likely creates more lower body neuromuscular strain than cycling because of its eccentric loading component. Rowing and swimming involve substantial upper body work and can therefore interfere with upper body strength more than cycling or running. The coach should think in terms of shared tissues and movement demands, not simply “cardio minutes.” [LB, Ch. 6]
3. Programming implications by priority
Maximal strength or power priority
Keep conditioning sufficient for health, work capacity, or sport needs but remove unnecessary high volume endurance work.
Favor modalities with low local interference to the key strength lifts when possible.
Protect heavy and explosive sessions from prior hard conditioning fatigue.
Use low intensity aerobic work for extra conditioning when a severe session is not required.
Hypertrophy priority
Manage local muscle fatigue and energy demand so conditioning does not repeatedly reduce resistance training volume or load quality.
A lower impact, lower eccentric mode can be useful when lower body hypertrophy is a priority.
Avoid adding high frequency hard conditioning to the same muscle groups simply because it is metabolically different.
Combat sport priority
Sport practice is the main specific conditioning stimulus. Strength and supplemental conditioning should support rather than compete with technical and sparring quality.
Preserve enough strength and power work to maintain force qualities, but reduce gym fatigue as hard sport volume increases.
Use supplemental aerobic work to fill a base or recovery gap; add hard intervals only when sport practice does not already provide the needed stimulus.
Endurance priority
Protect key endurance sessions and event specific volume from residual lifting fatigue.
Use strength training because the source base supports benefits to endurance performance, but dose it so it does not compromise the priority endurance work.
As competition approaches, shift strength toward maintenance and preserve event specific quality.
4. Same session, order, and spacing
The source base suggests that increasing recovery between modes can reduce acute performance interference. Laursen and Buchheit describe studies where cycling affected leg press performance at 4 and 8 hours but not 24 hours, and studies in which alternating days or longer between mode recovery improved some strength or power outcomes. These examples support a direction, not a universal “six hour rule.” [LB, Ch. 6]
If both sessions must occur on the same day, perform the priority quality first unless a specific study or sport constraint justifies the opposite order.
Separate sessions longer when both stress the same muscles at high intensity.
A low intensity easy session can often coexist closer to lifting than a hard interval session because the acute fatigue cost is lower.
If the next day contains a high priority lower body lift, hard running HIIT is a higher risk choice than low impact aerobic work.
5. Nutrition and recovery considerations
The supplied source set supports the importance of glycogen availability for high intensity work and notes that chronic high intensity volume can deplete glycogen. It does not, within the three core books used here, justify a full concurrent sports nutrition protocol inside this article. The engine should at minimum flag inadequate energy and carbohydrate availability as recovery constraints when high volume conditioning and lifting are combined. [K7, Ch. 14; LB, Ch. 3]
6. Athlete modifiers
Training age: advanced lifters can be more sensitive to small reductions in high quality strength volume because further gains are difficult.
Sport load: four hard sport sessions cannot be treated as zero endurance or conditioning volume.
Mode: choose the least interfering modality that still creates the required adaptation.
Impact tolerance: running is more mechanically specific for runners but can be an unnecessary eccentric cost for a nonrunner.
Schedule: shift workers or athletes with poor sleep may need a lower total concurrent dose even if each component is reasonable in isolation.
7. Failure modes
Blaming molecular interference for a plan that is simply too fatiguing.
Adding endurance volume without reducing anything else when strength or sport volume rises.
Doing hard conditioning immediately before heavy or explosive lifting and then interpreting poor lifting as lost strength.
Choosing running HIIT for a nonrunning strength athlete when cycling could create the desired metabolic stress with less lower body eccentric cost.
Assuming rowing is “low impact” and therefore free of upper back and arm fatigue before pulling work.
Using one fixed spacing rule regardless of intensity, modality, and priority.
8. Decision rules
IF strength or power is the primary goal, THEN perform the highest priority strength or power work fresh and cap conditioning volume to the dose required for the secondary goal
IF both modes stress the same muscle groups hard, THEN increase recovery between sessions or separate them onto different days when possible
IF the conditioning goal can be achieved with either running or cycling and lower body strength is the priority, THEN consider cycling because the source suggests lower eccentric neuromuscular strain
IF combat sport volume rises sharply, THEN reduce supplemental conditioning and nonessential lifting volume before allowing sport quality to deteriorate
IF endurance is the primary competitive goal, THEN protect key endurance sessions first and use strength as a supportive quality at a recoverable dose
Source traceability
Direct source support: [LB] Ch. 6 for conflicting concurrent evidence, volume and frequency effects, modality differences, session recovery, and bidirectional acute fatigue. [M8] Ch. 22 for evidence that resistance exercise can coexist with or augment endurance signaling.
Source synthesis: The priority first and shared tissue framework is a practical translation of the source evidence.
Unresolved source gap: The source base does not justify a single universal number of hours that must separate strength and endurance sessions. Spacing should scale with intensity, local overlap, and the priority session.

ARTICLE 9
Conditioning for Combat and Intermittent Sports
Combat conditioning should not be a punishment circuit. BJJ, MMA, and boxing already provide highly specific intermittent stress. Supplemental work should target the physiological quality that sport practice does not develop efficiently enough, while preserving technical and sparring quality.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
Combat sports combine explosive high impulse actions, repeated high intensity exchanges, active movement at lower intensities, isometric or positional work, and recovery between exchanges and rounds. Laursen and Buchheit describe oxidative metabolism as important for maintaining repetitive high intensity actions and accelerating recovery, while ATP PCr and glycolytic systems support high power striking, takedowns, wrestling exchanges, and rapid transitions. [LB, Ch. 11]
Sport practice is already conditioning
Sparring, pad work, wrestling rounds, drilling, and live grappling are not merely skill sessions. They produce real internal and external training load. The exact load varies enormously with round format, opponent, tactical style, coaching constraints, and athlete behavior. Therefore, the first programming task is to classify the existing sport week before prescribing supplemental conditioning. [LB, Chs. 8, 9, and 11]
2. Demand analysis by sport
Boxing and striking
Strikes are brief, fast, high impulse actions. The high energy phosphate and glycolytic systems support the bursts, while oxidative metabolism helps maintain repeated actions over the bout and recover between exchanges and rounds. Supplemental work should distinguish between punch or kick power, repeatability, and whole bout aerobic support. [LB, Ch. 11]
BJJ and grappling
Grappling alternates positional movement, isometric tension, explosive sweeps or takedowns, scrambles, grip work, and variable recovery. The metabolic demand is therefore highly intermittent and opponent dependent. General aerobic development can support recovery, but only grappling develops grappling specific economy, technical efficiency, and local muscular coordination. [LB, Ch. 11; K7, Ch. 14 on specificity]
MMA
MMA combines striking, wrestling, clinch, and grappling demands. The session plan must account for the fact that technical practice can already hit multiple physiological targets. A separate conditioning session may be useful, but it should solve a defined deficit rather than duplicate every energy system again. [LB, Ch. 11]
3. Programming implications
Map the sport week. Separate technical low stress practice, moderate rounds, and hard sparring.
Identify the performance failure. Does the athlete lose overall pace, fail to recover between bursts, lose peak explosiveness, or become technically inefficient under fatigue?
Determine whether the missing quality is general or sport specific. General aerobic capacity can be trained on a bike; grip specific efficiency cannot.
Choose the lowest cost method that develops the missing quality. Use general conditioning when precision and recovery matter; use sport specific intervals when transfer and technical execution under the target stress are the goal.
Place supplemental high intensity work away from the most important sparring and technical sessions. Easy aerobic work is easier to integrate.
Reduce supplemental work as specific fight or competition practice becomes more intense and complete.
4. Prescription variables
Effort duration: match the physiological target, not just the official round length.
Recovery: short recovery develops repeatability but also raises metabolic fatigue. Long recovery preserves explosive quality.
Modality: bike, run, row, bag, pads, drilling, takedown sequences, and grappling can create similar cardiovascular strain but very different local and technical stress.
Round density: repeated hard sport rounds count as high intensity training and should be included in weekly load decisions.
Specificity: increase sport specific conditioning as competition approaches, while reducing redundant generic work.
5. Athlete modifiers
Highly skilled athlete with poor aerobic support: additional low intensity work may improve recovery without adding more chaotic sport fatigue.
Athlete with strong aerobic base but poor explosive output: more Zone 2 is unlikely to solve the main problem; strength, power, and high quality sport actions become more relevant.
Athlete who fades because technique deteriorates under pressure: technical pacing and sport specific efficiency may be more limiting than general cardiovascular capacity.
Heavy athlete or athlete with joint irritation: cycling can add aerobic work with lower impact than running, but cannot replace sport practice.
Athlete in fight camp: as hard sparring and specificity rise, generic conditioning should become more selective.
6. Failure modes
Adding brutal circuits after hard sparring because the athlete “needs cardio.”
Using only round duration to design intervals and ignoring the burst recovery pattern within the round.
Duplicating glycolytic stress while neglecting low intensity aerobic development.
Using sport drills for every conditioning target when a controlled ergometer would allow more precise dosing and less technical fatigue.
Using general conditioning to replace technical sparring and expecting equal transfer.
Treating every BJJ, boxing, or MMA athlete as having the same metabolic profile.
7. Decision rules
IF hard sparring already provides repeated severe efforts, THEN count that as high intensity conditioning and add more hard intervals only when a specific deficit remains
IF the athlete is strong for the first exchanges but fades across rounds, THEN assess aerobic support, pacing, and repeat effort recovery before adding more peak power work
IF the athlete maintains pace but lacks decisive explosive actions, THEN prioritize strength, power, and high quality short explosive exposures rather than simply increasing conditioning volume
IF a general conditioning modality can develop the missing aerobic quality with less orthopedic or technical fatigue, THEN use the general modality and preserve sport practice for specificity
IF competition approaches and sport practice becomes more specific and intense, THEN reduce redundant general high intensity conditioning and preserve freshness for technical and sparring quality
Source traceability
Direct source support: [LB] Ch. 11 for combat sport demands and examples of long and short interval targets; Chs. 8 and 9 for load surveillance. [K7] Ch. 14 for specificity and periodization principles.
Source synthesis: The “sport practice first, supplemental work fills a gap” architecture is the central practical synthesis.
Unresolved source gap: The books do not provide sufficiently precise sport wide work to rest ratios for every BJJ ruleset, boxing bout, MMA round structure, or tactical style. Event and athlete data must drive those details.

ARTICLE 10
Modality Translation: Running, Cycling, Rowing, Swimming, and Multisport
The same cardiovascular system can be stressed through many modalities, but the local muscular, mechanical, technical, and measurement demands are not interchangeable. Transfer is therefore strongest for central capacity and weakest where event economy and tissue specific adaptation dominate.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Comparative principles
Kenney and McArdle emphasize specificity of testing and training. Highly trained athletes often produce modality specific VO2max values, and the muscles actually recruited must develop the local oxidative machinery for performance. Laursen and Buchheit add a practical layer: mode changes neuromuscular strain, pacing tools, and how accurately HIIT can be prescribed. [K7, Chs. 1 and 11; M8, Ch. 11; LB, Chs. 6 and 8]
Running
Mechanical profile: weight bearing with substantial eccentric loading, so the neuromuscular and musculoskeletal recovery cost is usually greater than cycling for the lower body. [LB, Ch. 6]
Measurement: pace or speed is the main external anchor; GPS or measured distances can be used, while RPE can help on variable terrain. [LB, Chs. 2 and 8]
Economy: strongly performance relevant. Similar VO2max does not imply similar running speed if oxygen cost differs. [K7, Ch. 5]
Transfer: cycling can support aerobic capacity but does not fully reproduce running economy or impact tolerance.
Cycling
Mechanical profile: lower eccentric impact than running, making it attractive when additional aerobic or HIIT volume is needed with lower running related muscle damage. [LB, Ch. 6]
Measurement: power meters provide a direct external workload in watts and high resolution monitoring. [LB, Ch. 8]
Local fatigue: high power cycling can still create substantial quadriceps fatigue and therefore interfere with squat or lower body strength work.
Transfer: excellent for general aerobic loading and precise interval work, but not a complete replacement for running or other event specific mechanics.
Rowing
Mechanical profile: large muscle mass with substantial lower and upper body involvement, so “low impact” does not mean low systemic or back / pulling fatigue.
Measurement: rowing speed, pace, power, stroke rate, distance, HR, and RPE can all be used. Laursen and Buchheit note that HR lag makes it a poor primary prescription tool for short HIIT; rowing speed and stroke rate are more useful for those efforts. [LB, Ch. 16]
Technical dependency: stroke rate changes both metabolic and neuromuscular demands. The same interval duration at different stroke rates is not the same session.
Transfer: indoor ergometers allow strong individual control but on water performance also depends on boat skill and crew context.
Swimming
Mechanical profile: non weight bearing and low impact, but with high upper body technical demand.
Economy and technique: drag, body position, stroke mechanics, and skill have unusually large effects on oxygen cost and speed. [M8, Ch. 10]
Measurement: pace over measured distances, stroke rate, and critical swim speed can be useful; heart rate behaves differently from land modes and should not be transferred blindly.
Transfer: swimming can add cardiovascular work with low impact, but a weak swimmer may be technique limited before a precise aerobic target is reached.
Multisport / triathlon
The three modes share central aerobic physiology but impose different local muscular and technical demands.
Laursen and Buchheit describe shorter triathlon formats as placing greater importance on high speed or power associated with VO2max and VT2, while longer formats place more emphasis on sustainable output around VT1 and pacing. [LB, Ch. 19]
Intensity must be calibrated separately for swim, bike, and run. One heart rate or pace anchor cannot simply be copied across modes.
Training allocation should reflect event duration, transition effects, and which discipline is limiting performance.
2. Event translation
Shorter running events and 5K / 10K
The sources support a combination of VO2max, high fractional utilization, economy, and event specific speed. As event duration shortens, work at higher fractions of vVO2max and higher speed becomes more important; as duration lengthens, sustainable threshold and economy contribute more. Exact race programs require specialist running sources beyond the three core books. [K7, Ch. 5; LB, Ch. 14]
Marathon
Laursen and Buchheit describe elite marathon programs as dominated by low intensity volume with a smaller amount of marathon pace and faster work, including HIIT. This is a sport specific example, not a template for hybrid athletes. Marathon preparation also requires running specific economy, impact resilience, fueling, and large event specific volume that cycling cannot fully replace. [LB, Ch. 14]
Rowing distance / time goals
Rowing prescription should use rowing specific pace or power and stroke rate for high intensity work. Laursen and Buchheit provide rowing examples in which early season training contains more aerobic and threshold work, with long, short, and sprint intervals increasing as key races approach. This supports a progression from general aerobic capacity toward race specific intensity, without implying that the sample elite weeks are universal. [LB, Ch. 16]
Long cycling events
Cycling allows precise power based pacing and high aerobic volume with less impact than running. Long event preparation should therefore emphasize sustainable power, economy, fueling, and sufficient cycling specific duration, while using higher intensity work to develop aerobic power where useful. The core sources support these principles but are not a full specialist road cycling periodization manual.
Triathlon / Ironman style endurance
Long duration multisport performance requires very high sustainable aerobic output across three specific modes, with transitions and cumulative fatigue. Laursen and Buchheit emphasize that long distance triathlon outcomes depend more on sustained, well paced effort and high speed or power around the lower threshold region than short format racing. Specialist sources would be needed to prescribe detailed discipline volume, fueling, open water skill, and race specific periodization. [LB, Ch. 19]
3. Programming implications
Use event specific training for economy, technique, and tissue adaptation. Use cross training to add central aerobic stimulus or reduce mechanical cost.
Choose the modality that best balances transfer and recovery cost for the athlete’s current block.
Calibrate intensity separately in each mode. A percentage of one modality’s threshold should not be copied directly to another.
Use external metrics that fit the mode: speed / pace for running and swimming, power for cycling, pace / power and stroke rate for rowing.
As competition approaches, move the conditioning mix toward the event specific modality and race demands.
4. Failure modes
Calling cycling and running interchangeable because both raise heart rate.
Using swimming as low impact cardio for a poor swimmer and assuming the athlete is receiving the intended metabolic dose.
Prescribing short rowing HIIT from heart rate alone despite response lag.
Using an indoor rower result as the only measure of on water rowing performance.
Replacing too much event specific running with cross training before a running race.
Copying elite marathon or triathlon volume distributions into a strength or combat athlete’s week.
5. Decision rules
IF the athlete needs more aerobic volume but running impact is the limiting cost, THEN add cycling or swimming while retaining enough running for event specific economy and tissue preparation
IF the athlete is a cyclist and precise interval intensity matters, THEN prefer direct power output as the main external prescription variable and use HR / RPE as response markers
IF the athlete is rowing short HIIT, THEN prescribe from rowing speed / power and stroke rate rather than relying on HR to control each effort
IF the swimmer cannot hold efficient technique at the prescribed metabolic intensity, THEN treat technique as a limiter and avoid interpreting the session as pure aerobic conditioning
IF the athlete is preparing for a multisport event, THEN set separate intensity anchors and sufficient specific exposure for each discipline rather than one universal zone system
Source traceability
Direct source support: [LB] Ch. 6 for modality related neuromuscular strain; Ch. 8 for external and internal load measurement; Chs. 14, 16, and 19 for running, rowing, and triathlon examples. [M8] Chs. 10 and 11 for swimming economy and modality specific VO2 testing. [K7] Ch. 5 for economy and Ch. 11 for specificity of aerobic adaptations.
Source synthesis: Cross training is treated as a tool for central load and fatigue management, while event specificity is retained for economy, local adaptation, and skill.
Unresolved source gap: Detailed event plans for 3K, 5K, 10K, marathon, rowing race preparation, cycling events, and Ironman require specialist sport sources if the engine is expected to prescribe high resolution race specific systems.

ARTICLE 11
Testing, Monitoring, and the Conditioning Decision System
Testing is useful only when it changes a decision. The engine should combine sport specific performance, external workload, internal response, and recovery trends, then use reassessment to update the prescription rather than collect data for its own sake.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to train, how hard, how much, how often, and what to monitor. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Key concepts
No single test measures all conditioning qualities. VO2max measures maximal oxygen uptake, threshold tests estimate sustainable metabolic boundaries, time trials measure integrated performance, critical power or speed models describe the power duration relationship, and repeated sprint tests examine repeatability. Monitoring then tracks whether the athlete is adapting to the prescribed workload and whether fatigue is obscuring performance. [K7, Chs. 5 and 11; M8, Ch. 11; LB, Chs. 8 and 9]
2. Laboratory versus field testing
VO2max testing
Laboratory tests can directly measure oxygen uptake and control workload precisely. Treadmill testing commonly produces high peak values in healthy people, while trained specialists can show strong modality specificity. A cyclist or swimmer should therefore be tested in a relevant mode when the purpose is sport specific prescription. [M8, Ch. 11; K7, introductory testing sections]
Field tests and time trials
Field tests trade some physiological precision for accessibility and specificity. Running time trials, cycling power tests, rowing ergometer tests, and sport specific incremental tests can provide practical external performance anchors. Laursen and Buchheit emphasize that field based maximal or critical speed and power tools can be highly useful for individualizing intervals. [LB, Ch. 2]
Critical power / speed
A series of maximal efforts across different durations can be used to characterize the power duration relationship and estimate a sustainable asymptote plus finite work capacity above it. Laursen and Buchheit provide rowing examples using a “power continuum” across multiple durations. The exact testing protocol must be mode specific and should not be improvised beyond source support. [K7, Ch. 5; LB, Chs. 4 and 16]
3. Monitoring tools
External load
External load describes the work performed: power, speed, pace, distance, accelerations, or sport specific actions. For cycling, power meters provide a particularly direct measure. For running, GPS or measured distance can track speed. For rowing, pace, power, and stroke rate are useful. External load tells the coach what the athlete did. [LB, Ch. 8]
Internal load
Heart rate, lactate, oxygen uptake where available, and RPE describe aspects of the physiological response to external work. Laursen and Buchheit favor session RPE as a highly practical global indicator while acknowledging moderate reliability. Heart rate is useful for steady aerobic work but has lag and limited value for supramaximal intermittent work. Blood lactate can inform glycolytic participation but is often impractical in the field. [LB, Ch. 8]
Performance trends
The most actionable signal is often the relationship between external output and internal cost. Examples include a faster pace at the same HR and RPE, the same power at a lower internal response, improved repeatability across intervals, or stable output despite a reduced training load during taper. Performance should be interpreted with training context because acute fatigue can temporarily reduce output without indicating loss of fitness. [LB, Ch. 9]
Recovery and fatigue monitoring
Laursen and Buchheit discuss resting heart rate and HRV, but also emphasize that these measures depend on training phase and intensity distribution and can be misleading without context. The engine should therefore combine subjective recovery, session RPE, performance quality, sport load, and any physiological marker rather than using one readiness number as an automatic decision. [LB, Ch. 9]
Monitoring rule: External performance is what the athlete produced. Internal response is what that work cost. Recovery data help explain why. The coaching decision should integrate all three.
4. Reassessment frequency
The books support periodic reevaluation of threshold and sport specific performance as fitness changes, but they do not establish one universal retest interval for all athletes. Kenney recommends testing after training cycles to evaluate performance and transfer. McArdle recommends periodic reevaluation of the lactate intensity relationship. Therefore, reassessment should occur when enough training has accumulated for a meaningful change to be plausible and when the result would alter prescription. [K7, Ch. 14; M8, Ch. 21]
Unresolved source gap: A fixed rule such as retest every 4 weeks is not supported across all populations and modalities by the supplied books. The engine should schedule reassessment from block length, test fatigue, competition calendar, and the need to recalibrate intensity.
5. The conditioning decision system
Identify the goal or event. Define duration, pace or power demands, burst pattern, technical demand, and mechanical constraints.
Define the physiological demands. Estimate the relative importance of aerobic capacity, sustainable threshold, economy, repeated high intensity recovery, peak power, and glycolytic contribution.
Assess current performance. Use the most specific practical test, plus available physiological markers.
Identify the likely limiter. Use multiple indicators rather than one test score.
Identify recovery constraints. Count sport practice, strength training, sleep, schedule, impact tolerance, and recent fatigue.
Select modality. Balance specificity with the mechanical and local fatigue cost.
Select intensity domain. Anchor to individual threshold, critical power / speed, v/pVO2max, race pace, RPE, or another source supported marker.
Select session type. Low intensity continuous, threshold / sustainable work, long intervals, short intervals, repeated sprints, sprint intervals, or sport specific intervals.
Prescribe dose. Set intensity, work duration, recovery, repetitions, and weekly frequency. Avoid unsupported universal volume rules.
Place the session in the week. Protect priority sport, strength, power, and technical work from predictable fatigue.
Monitor execution. Compare prescribed versus actual external output and internal response.
Progress or regress. Change one main variable according to adaptation and recovery.
Reassess. Retest when the result is likely to be meaningful and actionable.
6. Failure modes
Collecting VO2max, HRV, lactate, pace, and RPE without a predefined decision attached to each measure.
Using a treadmill VO2max to prescribe precise cycling or swimming intensity in a specialist athlete.
Treating HR as an instantaneous external workload marker during short intervals.
Interpreting one bad time trial during a high fatigue week as loss of fitness.
Retesting so often that testing itself becomes a fatigue source.
Changing the program because of one noisy readiness metric while performance remains stable.
Using a fixed threshold from months earlier after external performance has clearly changed.
7. Decision rules
IF a test result will not change training prescription, THEN do not test merely to collect data
IF the athlete is highly trained in a specific modality, THEN prefer testing and intensity calibration in that modality
IF short interval HR appears below the target despite correct speed or power and high RPE, THEN recognize HR lag and do not increase workload solely to chase the HR number
IF external performance improves at the same or lower internal cost, THEN treat this as positive adaptation and recalibrate training targets when appropriate
IF performance drops while load, soreness, sleep disruption, and subjective fatigue rise together, THEN reduce or redistribute training stress before concluding that fitness has declined
IF the athlete completes a meaningful block or their current zones no longer match performance, THEN reassess the relevant field or laboratory marker and update prescription

Appendix: Engine Wide Decision Hierarchy
The following rules govern the entire cluster. They are intentionally higher order than any single workout prescription.
IF the goal and event demands are not defined, THEN do not prescribe conditioning yet
IF sport practice already provides a large amount of a target stress, THEN count it before adding supplemental conditioning
IF two methods can produce the desired physiological adaptation, THEN choose the one with the better combination of specificity, controllability, and recovery cost for the athlete
IF a numerical range comes from an elite sport example, THEN do not generalize it automatically to another sport or athlete population
IF a threshold term is used, THEN state the operational definition or test used rather than assuming equivalence with every other threshold term
IF an athlete is improving while recovering well, THEN avoid unnecessary changes and progress the smallest useful variable
IF fatigue rises and priority performance falls, THEN reduce redundant volume or local overlap before adding more intensity
IF a recommendation is not adequately supported by the three core sources, THEN flag the gap rather than inventing a precise rule
