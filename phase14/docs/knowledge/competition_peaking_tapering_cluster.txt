How to Use This Cluster
This document is not a collection of fixed fight-week or meet-week templates. It is a reasoning system for moving an athlete from productive training into competition readiness. The central problem is not simply how to reduce work. It is how to remove enough fatigue to reveal fitness while preserving the physical, technical, tactical, and psychological qualities that competition requires.
Source rule: The current version uses the supplied project sources as its authority base. It does not silently invent universal timing rules for combat sports, strength sports, or mock meets where the sources do not support them. Practical templates are labeled as source synthesis, coaching inference, or project heuristic rather than being presented as scientific laws.
Evidence and traceability labels
Direct source support: a statement or recommendation explicitly present in one or more supplied books.
Source synthesis: a practical framework produced by combining compatible statements from the supplied sources.
Coaching inference: a conservative practical implication that follows from the evidence but is not stated as a universal rule by the books.
Project heuristic: an internal coaching rule from the Raz Strength Program Coaching Engine. It can be operationally useful but is not treated as a validated physiological measurement.
Unresolved source gap: an area that needs a firmer sport-specific source before the engine should encode a universal rule.

ARTICLE 1
Peaking, Tapering, Fitness, Fatigue, and Readiness
A taper is not a rest week and a peak is not a last-minute trick. Competition preparation works when accumulated fitness is retained while residual fatigue falls enough for the athlete to express what training has already built.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.

Mujika describes tapering as a specialized precompetition reduction in training load designed to reduce accumulated physiological and psychological stress while preserving or enhancing performance. The key model is that training has both positive and negative effects: adaptation raises future performance potential, while fatigue temporarily suppresses the athlete's ability to express it. When training load falls, fatigue can dissipate more quickly than adaptation, so performance can improve even though the athlete is doing less training. [MJ, Chs. 1 and 7]
Direct source principle: A taper should be judged by competition readiness, not by whether the athlete is still accumulating a large training load. The athlete is allowed to feel unusually good. That is often the intended outcome, not unused training capacity.
2. Fitness is not the same as current performance
A poor session late in a heavy block can reflect accumulated fatigue rather than a true loss of fitness. Conversely, a good session during a taper can reflect successful fatigue dissipation rather than a sudden new adaptation. This distinction prevents two common errors: panicking after a fatigue-suppressed performance and adding workload when the athlete begins to feel fresh.
A useful coaching abstraction is: readiness equals accumulated fitness minus residual fatigue, then modified by technical, tactical, nutritional, environmental, and psychological state. This is not a literal biological equation and should not be calculated as one. It is a decision model that keeps the coach from confusing what the athlete has built with what the athlete can express today. [MJ, Ch. 7]
3. Why tapering can improve performance without building much new fitness
Muscle damage and soreness can decline as high-volume and damaging work is removed.
Neuromuscular force and power can rebound when chronic training stress is reduced.
Muscle glycogen can rise as training expenditure falls and carbohydrate availability is maintained.
Mood, perceived effort, sleep quality, and subjective recovery can improve.
Technical execution can feel sharper when fatigue no longer degrades coordination, timing, and force expression.
Some delayed adaptation to previous training may continue to appear during the taper, so the process is not purely passive.
Mujika reports that taper-associated performance gains can occur with or without large changes in VO2max, cardiac function, hormones, or other isolated laboratory markers. The practical implication is important: the coach should not demand that a single biomarker prove the taper is working. Performance readiness is the integrated outcome. [MJ, Chs. 2, 3, 6, and 7]
4. Taper versus deload
A deload is organized around recovery from training so the next training phase can be productive. A taper is organized backward from a fixed performance date. The same reduction in volume can therefore be correct in one context and wrong in another. During a deload the coach may deliberately reduce specificity and psychological arousal. During a taper the coach often preserves competition-relevant intensity, skill frequency, speed, and routine because these qualities must still be expressed at the end of the week.
5. Peaking versus tapering
Peaking is the larger architecture. It decides which qualities are developed early, which become more specific later, which are maintained, and which are allowed to decay because they no longer justify their fatigue cost. The taper is the terminal portion of that architecture. A good taper cannot rescue a badly designed peak, and a good peak can be obscured by a poor taper.
6. Practical decision rules
IF the competition date is not fixed and the athlete only needs recovery, THEN use deload logic rather than competition taper logic.
IF the athlete is objectively improving and feels increasingly fresh during the taper, THEN do not add training merely because extra capacity appears available.
IF a performance test is poor after a heavy training period but recovery signals are also poor, THEN first consider fatigue suppression before concluding that fitness was lost.
IF the athlete entered the final period undertrained rather than overreached, THEN a large taper may remove too much useful stimulus; use a smaller readiness-prime approach.
IF competition requires skill, speed, and decision making as well as physical output, THEN readiness must include all of those domains rather than a gym-only score.
Source traceability
Direct source support: [MJ] Chs. 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, and 7 for taper aims, physiological and psychological changes, and fitness-fatigue modeling.
Source synthesis: The distinction between training fitness, current expression, peak, taper, deload, and primer combines Mujika's taper model with the project's state-based coaching logic.
Unresolved source gap: No source provides a single validated readiness equation that converts all physical, technical, and psychological states into one number. The engine should use converging signals rather than false precision.
ARTICLE 2
Designing the Taper: Intensity, Volume, Frequency, Duration, and Taper Shape
The strongest source-grounded taper rule is not simply "do less." Reduce training load mainly through volume, preserve enough relevant intensity to retain performance qualities, and individualize frequency and duration according to training status, sport, and the fatigue accumulated before the taper.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. The four variables are independent
Training load is created by the interaction of intensity, volume, frequency, and the stress produced by the exercise mode. A taper can therefore reduce total load without reducing every variable equally. Mujika treats intensity, volume, frequency, and taper duration separately because they have different effects on retention, fatigue, and the athlete's feel for the event. [MJ, Ch. 5]

Important evidence boundary: The 41 to 60% volume reduction and 8 to 14 day duration are population-level findings from sports such as swimming, running, and cycling. They are starting priors, not universal laws for MMA, BJJ, powerlifting, or every individual.
2. Intensity: preserve the signal, not the fatigue
Mujika's chapter summary is unusually clear: maintaining training intensity is necessary to retain and enhance training-induced adaptations during tapering, while reductions in other variables provide recovery. McArdle similarly emphasizes that maintenance of aerobic capacity is especially sensitive to intensity and describes successful tapers that reduce volume while retaining intensity. [MJ, Ch. 5; M8, Ch. 21]
For strength and power work, this means the athlete can still touch meaningful loads or perform explosive actions, but with very little total work and no need to approach failure. External intensity and effort must therefore be stored separately in the engine. A heavy single that is technically clean and far from a grinder may be less fatiguing than multiple moderate-load sets taken close to failure.
For combat sports, the word intensity needs to be decomposed. Competition-speed movement, tactical urgency, and technical intent may be preserved. Heavy contact, exhaustive live rounds, and repeated glycolytic suffering are different stressors and may need to fall. Blindly carrying the endurance rule "maintain intensity" into hard sparring is a category error.
3. Volume: the primary fatigue-reduction lever
The meta-analysis summarized by Mujika found the largest overall effect when pretaper training volume was reduced by 41 to 60%, with benefits also possible outside this band. The practical lesson is not to force every athlete to exactly 50%. It is that meaningful volume reduction is usually required; cutting only trivial accessory work while leaving the rest of the week unchanged may not dissipate enough fatigue. [MJ, Ch. 5]
In multi-quality sports, remove volume selectively. General conditioning, hypertrophy-only accessories, redundant strength patterns, extra drilling, and high-damage work usually disappear before competition-specific skill and brief high-quality exposures. A 50% reduction in total minutes that accidentally removes the most specific practice while preserving junk volume is poor taper design.
4. Frequency: protect rhythm and technical feel
Mujika reports that moderately trained individuals can maintain adaptations with frequencies around 30 to 50% of pretaper values, whereas highly trained athletes are generally advised to retain much higher frequencies, often above 80%, particularly in technique-dependent sports. A key concern is "loss of feel." [MJ, Ch. 5]
This maps well to combat and strength skill. The athlete may still enter the gym, mat, ring, or platform area frequently, but sessions become shorter, more predictable, and less costly. Frequency can preserve routine, timing, confidence, and technical fluency without requiring normal training volume.
5. Duration: start from prior fatigue, not from event length
The taper duration literature spans roughly 4 to 28 days, with the cited meta-analysis showing the largest average effect in the 8 to 14 day category. Mujika also emphasizes large interindividual variation and notes that pretaper overload can extend the recovery time required. The athlete who accumulated more fatigue often needs a longer taper, not necessarily a more extreme single-day reduction. [MJ, Chs. 5 and 7]
A practical starting hierarchy is therefore: low prior fatigue or undertraining may require only a short readiness prime; normal competition preparation may fit roughly one to two weeks; deliberate overload or unusually high camp fatigue may justify a longer taper. The engine should learn from the athlete's previous competition response and progressively replace generic defaults with individual history.
6. Taper shape
Linear taper: training load decreases systematically in a roughly linear fashion.
Exponential taper: training load decreases rapidly at first and then the rate of reduction slows. Mujika describes fast-decay and slow-decay versions.
Step taper: training load is reduced abruptly and then held at the lower level.
Two-phase or alternative taper: an early reduction may be followed by a small later increase or primer, but the evidence for these strategies is less complete.
Progressive tapering generally performed better than step tapering in the meta-analysis summarized by Mujika, especially in swimming and running, while sport-specific uncertainty remained. A progressive taper is therefore a reasonable default when there is no personal taper history, but not an absolute rule. [MJ, Ch. 1]
7. Practical construction sequence
1. Set Day 0: the competition, fight, test, or high-fidelity mock meet.
2. Estimate the athlete's fatigue entering the taper from the previous 1 to 3 weeks, not from one readiness score.
3. Choose a provisional taper duration.
4. Identify the competition qualities that must remain sharp: force, speed, skill, pacing, tactical timing, repeatability, or a combination.
5. Reduce volume first, preferentially from non-specific and high-cost work.
6. Retain enough intensity and frequency to preserve those competition qualities.
7. Track whether the athlete becomes fresher without becoming flat or technically disconnected.
8. Modify the taper from the smallest effective variable rather than redesigning the entire week after one noisy signal.
Source traceability
Direct source support: [MJ] Chs. 1 and 5 for taper models, intensity, volume, frequency, duration, and the cited Bosquet meta-analysis; [M8] Ch. 21 for maintenance and taper concepts.
Source synthesis: The table converts population evidence into a default hierarchy: volume is the main lever, relevant intensity is preserved, frequency is retained more strongly in highly trained skill-dependent athletes, and duration is adjusted to prior fatigue.
Unresolved source gap: Exact percentage reductions for combat sport training minutes, hard sparring, strength sets, and mixed-modality sessions are not established by the current source base. A future combat-specific source should refine those allocations.
ARTICLE 3
Residual Fatigue and Exercise Cost: Damage, Eccentrics, Novelty, and Interference
Competition-week exercise selection should be based on the ratio of useful performance signal to residual fatigue cost. Load percentage alone cannot tell the coach how expensive an exercise will be.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Training intensity is not training cost
Two exercises at the same RPE or percentage of maximum can produce very different residual fatigue. Cost depends on total repetitions, eccentric strain, local tissue loading, novelty, skill demand, metabolic stress, collision or contact, exercise mode, and the tissues that must perform in the upcoming event. This is why a taper needs an exercise-cost layer rather than a single intensity score.
2. Muscle damage and eccentric loading
Kenney describes delayed-onset muscle soreness and loss of force after unaccustomed or intense eccentric exercise. Structural disruption, excitation-contraction coupling failure, inflammation, edema, and contractile changes can reduce maximal force for several days; the source specifically notes that excitation-contraction coupling failure is important during the first several days after damaging work. Muscle glycogen restoration can also be impaired in damaged muscle. [K7, Ch. 5]
The taper implication is straightforward: there is little reason to create a large muscle-damage signal immediately before a competition where the adaptation cannot mature in time but the performance cost can still be present.
Direct source principle: DOMS is not merely discomfort. When it reflects exercise-induced muscle damage, force generation and range of motion can be impaired. Therefore "I can tolerate soreness" is not the same as "soreness is performance neutral." [K7, Ch. 5]
3. Novelty and the repeated-bout effect
Unaccustomed eccentric work generally produces more damage and soreness than familiar repeated exposure. The repeated-bout effect means a trained athlete often tolerates familiar work better than a novel variation. This makes novelty a distinct taper cost. A new exercise can be mechanically moderate and still be a poor competition-week choice because the athlete has no reliable history of soreness, coordination demand, or recovery time. [K7, Ch. 5; M8, Ch. 22]

6. Project stress-cost layer: useful but not validated physiology
The existing Advanced Layer scores spinal, CNS, local, grip, skill, soreness, sport interference, and other costs. This is useful for organizing decisions, especially in combat athletes, but its numerical thresholds and recovery windows are internal heuristics rather than validated biological measurements. The new competition cluster should preserve the dimensions while removing false certainty. [ADV, Cluster F]
The engine should therefore store costs as structured descriptors and athlete-specific history. For example, "heavy belt squat, familiar, low soreness history, 36 hours until sparring" is more informative than a single total cost number. A high score can flag review, but should not overrule actual performance and recovery data.
7. Practical decision rules
IF an exercise is novel and competition is close, THEN substitute a familiar pattern unless the new movement is essential to the event itself.
IF an exercise produces DOMS or measurable force loss that regularly persists into the next key session, THEN remove it early enough that its residual cost is gone before Day 0.
IF a heavy exposure is familiar, technically clean, very low volume, and does not create soreness, THEN do not reject it solely because the load is heavy.
IF a moderate-load session requires many hard sets or near-failure work, THEN treat it as potentially high cost despite the lower percentage.
IF sport practice already taxes grip, trunk, adductors, neck, or another region heavily, THEN count that local cost before adding gym work to the same tissues.

ARTICLE 4
What Recovers During a Taper: Neuromuscular, Metabolic, and Psychological Readiness
The taper can improve performance through several overlapping pathways. The coach does not need every pathway to improve, but should understand what can change and why a single biomarker cannot define readiness.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Neuromuscular recovery
Mujika summarizes repeated observations of improved strength and power after reduced training. Examples include increased swim power after two-week tapers, improved voluntary strength in strength-trained subjects after a ten-day taper, and modest 1RM improvements after a longer taper. These are protocol-specific studies, not guarantees, but they support the general principle that chronic intensive training can suppress force and power expression and that reduced load can reveal it. [MJ, Ch. 3]
Potential mechanisms include recovery of contractile function, reduced muscle damage, changes in neural activation, and improved force or power characteristics of muscle. The practical lesson is not to chase a specific mechanism during fight week. It is to avoid doing enough work to recreate the fatigue that the taper is trying to remove.
2. Metabolic and cardiorespiratory readiness
VO2max may increase, remain stable, or be unrelated to the final performance gain. Economy can improve in some athletes. Muscle glycogen commonly increases during tapering when training volume falls and carbohydrate availability remains adequate. Higher glycogen availability is particularly relevant to events requiring repeated or sustained high-intensity work. [MJ, Ch. 2]
The endurance cluster adds an important integration rule: maintaining a conditioning quality usually requires less work than developing it, while severe sport practice must be counted as conditioning load. Therefore the final phase should usually protect useful aerobic and event-specific intensity with much less total conditioning volume rather than attempting to create a new aerobic base in the last week. [M8, Ch. 21; LB, Chs. 8 and 9]
3. Muscle damage and biochemical recovery
Creatine kinase and other markers of training stress often move toward baseline during tapering, but the relationship between any one marker and performance is inconsistent. The same is true for testosterone, cortisol, and testosterone-to-cortisol ratios. Performance can improve with stable hormones, and hormonal changes can occur without directly tracking performance. [MJ, Ch. 3]
Monitoring rule: Hormones, CK, HRV, resting heart rate, or any other single marker can support a decision, but should not be treated as a truth machine. Competition performance is multifactorial and readiness signals are noisy.
4. Psychological recovery
Mujika reports that intensive training can worsen mood and increase fatigue, confusion, and stress, while tapering can improve mood, relaxation, recovery, perceived effort, and sleep quality. Psychological readiness is therefore part of the taper outcome rather than decoration around the physiology. [MJ, Ch. 4]
A combat athlete can be physically fresh but mentally flat or over-aroused. A strength athlete can be physically ready but anxious about attempt selection. The engine should separate physical readiness from arousal, confidence, and tactical clarity rather than averaging them into one score.
5. Freshness is an active training objective
Physical freshness: low residual soreness, low local fatigue, normal movement comfort, stable body mass and hydration for the event context.
Neuromuscular freshness: force and speed are available without repeated warm-up sets, grinding, or excessive arousal.
Technical freshness: timing, coordination, and competition patterns feel automatic rather than consciously forced.
Metabolic freshness: repeated efforts recover normally and the athlete is not carrying unnecessary conditioning fatigue or depleted glycogen.
Psychological freshness: the athlete is motivated and confident without excessive tension, panic, or the urge to prove fitness in training.
6. The "flat" athlete is not one problem
Feeling flat late in a taper can mean several different things: residual fatigue has not cleared, training frequency fell too far and the athlete lost rhythm, carbohydrate availability is poor, sleep or travel disrupted arousal, a weight cut is suppressing output, or the athlete is simply psychologically under-stimulated. The correct response depends on the cause. Adding a hard workout is only one possible intervention and often the wrong one.
7. Practical decision rules
IF strength or speed improves while total training falls, THEN treat this as a normal taper response rather than evidence that the athlete needs more volume.
IF the athlete feels flat but is not sore, sleep is good, body mass and fueling are stable, and brief warm-up efforts are fast, THEN consider a small familiar primer rather than a full workout.
IF the athlete feels flat and performance, sleep, mood, and soreness are all poor, THEN treat residual fatigue or recovery failure as more likely than under-stimulation.
IF a biomarker conflicts with good sport performance, normal mood, and normal recovery, THEN monitor rather than automatically changing the taper.
IF a weight cut or travel disruption is present, THEN interpret every readiness signal in that context before changing training.
Source traceability
Direct source support: [MJ] Chs. 2, 3, and 4 for cardiorespiratory, metabolic, neuromuscular, biochemical, hormonal, mood, RPE, and sleep responses.

ARTICLE 5
Building the Peak: Development, Specificity, Realization, and Expression
The taper only works well when the preceding weeks place the right fitness in the athlete. A peak progressively shifts the training question from "what can we build?" to "what must we preserve and express on Day 0?"
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. The competition clock changes training value
Far from competition, the athlete can tolerate training that produces soreness, large volume, technical experimentation, and delayed adaptation because there is time for the benefits to mature. As competition approaches, the value of new general adaptation falls and the cost of residual fatigue rises. Specificity therefore increases while nonessential volume falls. This is consistent with the periodization principles in Kenney and the competition-preparation logic in Mujika. [K7, Ch. 14; MJ, Chs. 5 and 6]
2. Four functional phases

These are functional phases, not mandatory calendar blocks. An athlete may move through them quickly or slowly depending on training age, event date, prior preparation, injury, and whether the sport itself supplies the specific exposure. The engine should not force a 12-week peak when only six weeks exist, or invent a peak when the event is merely a low-priority mock meet.
3. Specificity must be sport-specific, not just "harder"
For a strength meet, specificity usually means the competition lifts, competition standards, heavy but technically clean singles or low-rep work, commands, warm-up sequencing, and attempt strategy. For combat sport, specificity means tactical and technical execution against realistic resistance, pace changes, round structure, decision making, and maintaining strength or power as support qualities. The closer the event, the less reason there is for nonessential gym complexity.
4. Development and maintenance are different doses
The athlete generally needs more volume and repeated exposure to build a quality than to retain it over a short taper or competition period. McArdle describes maintenance of aerobic adaptations with lower frequency or duration when intensity is retained; the Advanced Layer makes the same practical assumption for strength. This allows the program to create space for sport practice without pretending that every quality must keep progressing simultaneously. [M8, Ch. 21; ADV, Clusters F and G]
5. Overload before taper: useful but not mandatory
Mujika's modeling work shows that heavier pretaper loading can create more fatigue and may require a longer taper. This does not mean every athlete should deliberately overreach before competition. Overload is only useful if the athlete can absorb it, if the event date permits recovery, and if the added adaptation is worth the risk. A poorly recovered athlete does not need a harder final block simply because a taper is coming. [MJ, Chs. 5 and 7]
6. Competition priority determines how aggressive the peak should be
A-priority competition: build the macrocycle around the event and use the athlete's best-known taper strategy.
B-priority competition: reduce fatigue enough to perform well but do not destroy a larger training block for a minor event.
C-priority event or technical mock meet: use the event as training information; only a small reduction may be needed.
High-fidelity mock meet or formal testing day: treat the day as a performance event if the result is intended to estimate true current capacity.
7. Practical decision rules
IF a quality will not meaningfully adapt before competition, THEN stop trying to develop it and decide whether to maintain it or remove it.
IF sport-specific work increases, THEN reduce general work before allowing total load to rise automatically.
IF the athlete has no history of successful precompetition overload, THEN do not manufacture overreaching simply because taper research includes overload models.
IF the competition is low priority, THEN preserve the long-term block and use a smaller taper rather than peaking aggressively for every event.
IF an athlete competes frequently, THEN repeated full peaks are usually impossible; alternate build periods, maintenance, and smaller tapers according to event priority.
Source traceability
Direct source support: [K7] Ch. 14 for periodization and specificity; [MJ] Chs. 5 to 7 for taper design, individual response, and pretaper load.
Source synthesis: The development to specificity to realization to expression sequence is a source synthesis that translates periodization and taper principles into one competition clock.
Unresolved source gap: The sources do not prescribe one universal peak length for strength meets or combat camps. The existing project ranges are useful planning heuristics but should not be encoded as physiological laws.
ARTICLE 6
Combat Sport Competition Camp: Integrating Skill, Sparring, Strength, and Conditioning
In combat sport, the gym is support work. As the fight or tournament approaches, technical and tactical specificity should occupy the cleanest recovery budget while strength and conditioning move from development toward maintenance and expression.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. The priority hierarchy
Combat performance is not a gym total. It requires technical execution, tactical decisions, repeated high-intensity actions, aerobic recovery between actions, force and power, emotional regulation, and the ability to perform under an opponent-imposed workload. The endurance cluster therefore treats sport practice as the primary specific conditioning stimulus and supplemental work as a way to fill identifiable gaps. [LB, Ch. 11; K7, Ch. 14]
Combat camp rule: As hard and specific sport practice rises, the gym and generic conditioning contribution should usually fall. The coach should not keep off-season strength volume flat while adding fight-camp sparring on top.
2. Count the mat, ring, and cage before writing the gym
Hard sparring counts as high-intensity conditioning, technical fatigue, local tissue loading, and psychological stress.
Positional or scenario rounds can be metabolically hard even when total session duration is short.
Gi grappling adds grip and forearm load; wrestling and takedown work add trunk, neck, hip, and eccentric demands; striking adds impact and high-speed repetition.
A strength session that is reasonable in isolation can become excessive when it loads the same tissues shortly before or after hard sport practice.
The existing Advanced Layer captures this with a multi-channel stress-cost system. The dimensions are useful, but the numerical scores and fixed recovery windows are project heuristics. The engine should use them as prompts for reasoning, then verify them against the athlete's actual sparring quality, soreness, force or speed, and recovery history. [ADV, Cluster F]

4. Strength training in camp
Strength work should preserve force qualities with less volume as sport stress rises. Familiar movements, low set counts, high technical quality, and low soreness are favored. High-repetition hypertrophy work, new exercises, large eccentric doses, and grinders offer little late-camp return. This does not require replacing every free-weight exercise with a machine; it requires choosing the exercise that gives the needed force signal at acceptable cost for that athlete.
A combat athlete does not need a strength-sport realization phase. Near-maximal gym performance is not the competition demand. If heavy exposure is retained, it should be because it preserves force familiarity efficiently, not because the coach wants proof of strength.
5. Sparring progression and withdrawal
The sport-specific source gap is greatest here. The current books do not justify one universal date for the final hard sparring session. A practical rule is to schedule the last truly damaging or high-risk live exposure early enough that soreness, local tissue irritation, cognitive fatigue, and sleep disruption have resolved before competition, then retain shorter technical and tactical exposures closer to the event. The exact timing should be learned from sport type, contact severity, athlete history, and the weight-management plan.
For striking sports, heavy head contact has additional health risk that should not be treated as merely another fatigue variable. Medical or governing-body guidance takes priority over performance programming.
6. Conditioning in camp
The endurance cluster is explicit that hard sparring already supplies repeated severe efforts. Adding brutal circuits after hard live work because the athlete "needs cardio" can duplicate the same stress while degrading technical quality. Supplemental conditioning should target the missing quality: aerobic base, repeat-effort recovery, high aerobic power, or a specific pacing limitation. [LB, Chs. 8, 9, and 11]
7. Weight-class interaction
As energy availability and body mass are manipulated, the recovery margin narrows. A cut can alter glycogen, mood, sleep, hydration, and training quality. Therefore the final strength and conditioning dose should become more conservative as the athlete approaches a difficult cut. The taper and weight plan must be designed together rather than allowing one to sabotage the other. [MJ, Ch. 2; NUT; ADV, Art. 76]
8. Practical decision rules
IF hard sparring quality is dropping while gym performance is still good, THEN reduce gym or supplemental conditioning stress before concluding that the athlete needs more sport work.
IF sport practice already supplies repeated severe metabolic exposure, THEN do not add hard intervals unless a specific conditioning deficit remains.
IF a strength exercise regularly compromises the next key mat session, THEN reduce its volume, change its placement, or substitute a lower-cost pattern.
IF competition-specific practice rises, THEN remove generic conditioning and hypertrophy work before reducing valuable technical frequency.
IF a weight cut becomes more demanding, THEN lower nonessential training cost and interpret poor readiness through the nutrition and hydration context.
IF the athlete is fresh late in camp, THEN protect sharpness rather than converting freshness into another hard sparring session.

ARTICLE 7
Fight Week and Combat Competition Week
Fight week is a distinct microcycle. The goal is not to gain fitness. It is to preserve technical feel, speed, confidence, and enough physiological readiness while eliminating avoidable fatigue and risk.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Build backward from Day 0
Competition day is Day 0. Every session in the final week should have a clear answer to one question: what useful state will this create on Day 0, and what residual cost might remain? This prevents the common habit of programming a normal week and simply deleting the final session.
2. What usually stays
Frequent but short technical touches when they help the athlete retain feel and confidence.
Competition-specific movement speed and tactical rehearsal without exhaustive rounds.
A familiar warm-up and arousal routine.
Very low-volume strength or power priming if the athlete has a known positive response.
Easy aerobic movement when it helps routine, circulation, recovery, or mood without creating local fatigue.
Normal familiar nutrition and hydration behavior unless a planned weight-class procedure requires specialist adjustment.
3. What usually leaves
Hypertrophy-only volume and failure training.
Novel lifts, novel drills, or major technical reconstructions.
High-damage eccentric work and large volumes of loaded lower-body work.
Redundant high-intensity conditioning and hard circuits.
Unnecessary heavy contact and hard sparring close enough to threaten recovery or injury risk.
Unplanned recreational activity, testing, or "confidence workouts" that are actually fitness tests.
4. Example fight-week structure
This is a practical starting template, not a universal combat-sport rule. Shift sessions earlier for athletes who recover slowly, after a very hard camp, during a difficult weight cut, or when contact risk is high. Shift slightly later only when the athlete has a demonstrated history of responding well.

5. Strength priming in fight week
A primer should create readiness, not adaptation. If used, it should employ familiar movements, very low set and rep counts, generous rest, fast concentric intent, and clear termination before velocity or technique deteriorates. The exact load is athlete-specific. A primer can be moderate or relatively heavy; its defining feature is low residual fatigue, not a fixed percentage.
Athletes who reliably feel better with complete rest should not be forced into a primer because a template says so. Likewise, an athlete who becomes flat after several days of inactivity may benefit from preserving frequency with very short sessions. Mujika's frequency findings support this individualization, especially in highly trained, technique-dependent athletes. [MJ, Ch. 5]
6. Technical work and contact
Maintain skill frequency more readily than hard contact. Technical drilling, pad work, controlled positional work, or competition-speed rehearsal can preserve timing with much less residual cost than unrestricted hard sparring. The engine should store "technical intensity" and "contact intensity" separately so that the instruction to preserve intensity does not accidentally preserve damage.
7. Psychological week
Reduce unnecessary uncertainty. Rehearse warm-up, corner communication, equipment, arrival time, rules, first-minute tactical priorities, and a simple arousal routine. Mujika's psychological chapter supports monitoring mood, perceived effort, recovery-stress, and sleep rather than assuming that physical rest automatically produces mental readiness. [MJ, Ch. 4]
8. Fight-week failure modes
The athlete feels amazing on Day -4 and adds hard rounds.
The coach removes all training and the athlete loses technical rhythm or becomes psychologically flat.
A final brutal conditioning session is used to "top up cardio."
The final strength session becomes a confidence test or PR attempt.
Weight-cut fatigue is mistaken for lack of fitness and answered with more training.
A new recovery method, supplement, food, sleep intervention, or warm-up is introduced in the final days.
9. Decision rules
IF the athlete finishes a fight-week session more fatigued, sore, or technically worse than before it, THEN the dose was too high for the purpose.
IF technical feel deteriorates after excessive rest but physical recovery is good, THEN restore a short familiar technical touch rather than a hard session.
IF contact or sparring has a meaningful chance of leaving soreness, injury, headache, or sleep disruption into Day 0, THEN move it earlier or remove it.
IF a difficult cut is underway, THEN prioritize safe weight management and technical freshness over maintaining normal gym volume.
IF the athlete has a proven personal fight-week routine that repeatedly produces good performance, THEN individual history should outrank generic templates unless a clear risk or new constraint appears.

ARTICLE 8
Strength Meets and Mock Meets: Peaking, Testing, and Attempt Planning
A strength meet is a performance event. A mock meet can be a performance event, a calibration test, or a technical rehearsal. The taper should match the purpose rather than automatically treating every heavy test day like a championship.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Strength meet priorities
For a maximal-strength event, the performance target is high force expression under specific rules. Late preparation therefore becomes more specific: competition movements and standards dominate, volume falls, meaningful load is retained, and unnecessary accessory or damaging work is removed. The new cluster does not need a snatch-by-snatch or deadlift-by-deadlift peak; it needs this general logic.
2. External intensity versus effort
Near competition, relatively heavy loads can remain useful while total work and proximity to failure fall. A technically clean single at a high percentage is not the same stress as repeated grinders. The engine should store load, reps, velocity or technical quality, and effort separately. "Heavy" is a property of external load; "fatiguing" is an outcome of the whole dose.

4. Attempt planning
The current source set does not establish one universal attempt percentage. The Advanced Layer contains specific opener percentages, but those should be treated as project heuristics rather than scientific rules. A safer engine principle is that the opener should have very high expected success based on recent specific training and competition standards; the second attempt should secure the primary performance objective; and the third should be selected from the first two attempts and the day's actual readiness rather than precompetition emotion. [ADV, Art. 94]
Attempt selection should be written before the event with clear adjustment rules. The athlete should know what changes after an unexpectedly easy opener, unexpectedly hard opener, technical error, or rule issue. This reduces emotional loading decisions when arousal is high.
5. Last heavy exposure
There is no universal last-heavy-day rule in the supplied taper evidence. Timing depends on the athlete's recovery from heavy singles, competition movement, total volume, body size, training age, and whether the exposure causes local soreness or psychological fatigue. The correct question is not "what day does every lifter lift heavy?" but "how long does this athlete need for a heavy, low-volume exposure to improve readiness rather than suppress it?"
The same logic governs mock meets. If the goal is to measure true capacity, reduce fatigue enough to make the test valid. If the goal is only technical practice, do not waste two weeks of productive training to manufacture a test-day PR.
6. Strength competition taper structure
Reduce total lifting volume substantially as the meet approaches.
Keep the competition lifts or highly specific variants as the main patterns.
Preserve meaningful load exposure while reducing repetitions, accessory work, and failure risk.
Remove high-soreness eccentric accessories and novel variations.
Maintain enough lift frequency for technical feel when the athlete benefits from it.
Rehearse equipment, commands, warm-up jumps, timing, and attempt-decision rules before meet day.
7. Mock meet data quality
A fatigued mock meet underestimates fitness; a heavily tapered mock meet may overestimate what the athlete can repeat during normal training. Both results can be useful if the context is recorded. The engine should therefore store the taper applied before the test, sleep, body mass, recent heavy volume, warm-up, RPE or bar-speed notes, and rule standard. A number without the testing context is weak programming data.
8. Practical decision rules
IF the mock meet is intended to estimate true competition performance, THEN taper enough to remove major training fatigue and standardize the test day.
IF the mock meet is primarily technical, THEN preserve the training block and keep attempts submaximal enough that the day does not require a major recovery period.
IF a heavy single becomes a grinder in the final phase, THEN reduce subsequent volume or load rather than forcing the planned progression.
IF the athlete misses training before the meet, THEN do not compress the missed volume into the final week.
IF the athlete feels exceptionally strong mid-taper, THEN keep the planned peak exposures rather than testing the goal early.
IF an attempt plan changes, THEN change it because of objective execution and competition context, not because adrenaline increased.

ARTICLE 9
Conditioning During the Taper: Aerobic Work, Intervals, and Repeated High-Intensity Readiness
Conditioning late in competition preparation should maintain the performance qualities already built and preserve recovery between high-intensity actions. It should not become a last chance to create fitness.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Maintenance is cheaper than development
McArdle describes maintenance of aerobic adaptation with lower training frequency or duration when intensity is retained, while reducing intensity too much can reduce aerobic capacity. Mujika likewise supports preserving training intensity as volume falls. This allows a taper to keep the relevant conditioning signal with far less total work. [M8, Ch. 21; MJ, Ch. 5]
2. Combat athletes already perform conditioning
The endurance cluster treats hard sparring and sport practice as part of the high-intensity conditioning budget. In combat sports, short explosive exchanges use rapid ATP pathways, while oxidative metabolism contributes to recovery between repeated actions. Therefore the athlete needs both action quality and the aerobic support that restores repeatability, but supplementary intervals are only justified when they address a specific deficit. [K7, Ch. 5; LB, Chs. 3 to 5 and 11]

4. Low-intensity aerobic work
Easy aerobic work is often a useful taper tool because it can preserve routine and oxidative activity with relatively low mechanical and metabolic cost. But "easy" is not automatically free. A long bike session can fatigue the quadriceps; running adds eccentric impact; rowing can tax the back and grip. The mode and duration must be selected according to the competition tissues and the athlete's response. [LB, Ch. 6]
5. High-intensity intervals
The closer competition gets, the stronger the justification required for a hard interval session. If the athlete already receives competition-specific severe exposure through sparring or event rehearsal, another hard interval session is often redundant. When some high-intensity work remains, reduce the repetitions and preserve output quality rather than chasing exhaustion or lactate.
6. Repeated sprint and alactic work
Brief explosive work can preserve speed or power when volume is tiny and recovery is long enough that each effort remains high quality. Do not convert a primer into conditioning by shortening rest. A session intended to retain peak speed should end before speed drops materially.
7. Practical decision rules
IF hard sport practice already supplies the severe-domain stimulus, THEN remove redundant formal HIIT before cutting valuable skill work.
IF easy aerobic work causes local leg, back, shoulder, or grip fatigue that affects the event, THEN reduce duration or change mode.
IF the athlete is one week from competition, THEN the burden of proof is on any hard conditioning session: it must clearly improve event readiness more than it threatens recovery.
IF the athlete feels flat after several low-load days, THEN a brief familiar pace or speed primer may be preferable to a long hard workout.
IF conditioning quality was not built earlier in the camp, THEN accept the limitation; do not attempt to repair weeks of undertraining in the final days.

ARTICLE 10
Nutrition, Weight Class, Glycogen, Hydration, and Travel During Competition Preparation
A taper changes energy expenditure, glycogen restoration, body-mass management, and travel demands. Nutrition and weight strategy must support freshness rather than creating a second fatigue problem while training stress is being reduced.
Coaching objective: Convert this topic into decisions about what to preserve, what to reduce, when to reduce it, and what to monitor as competition approaches. The article is written for both human coaches and an AI coaching engine.
1. Reduced training changes energy demand
Mujika notes that reduced training load lowers daily energy expenditure and that athletes should pay attention to energy intake so undesirable energy imbalance and body-composition changes do not occur. This does not mean automatically cutting calories aggressively during the taper. The appropriate intake depends on event demands, body mass goals, and whether the athlete is making weight. [MJ, Ch. 2]
2. Glycogen is part of readiness
Muscle glycogen has been shown to increase during tapering, especially when training volume falls and carbohydrate intake is adequate. High-intensity and repeated-effort performance relies heavily on carbohydrate. Therefore an athlete who is not constrained by a weight class should generally avoid creating unnecessary carbohydrate depletion during the final days. [MJ, Ch. 2; NUT]
3. Weight-class athletes
Weight making changes the taper problem. The athlete is trying to reduce fatigue while potentially reducing body mass, energy availability, gut content, or body water. These goals can conflict. The more aggressive the cut, the less tolerance there is for nonessential training stress. The existing Advanced Layer correctly flags aggressive acute dehydration as a higher-risk process that should not be casually generated by a training engine. [ADV, Art. 76]
Safety rule: The cluster can organize training around a planned weight-class strategy, but should not generate aggressive dehydration, diuretic, or medically risky weight-cut protocols. Significant acute weight manipulation should be managed with appropriately qualified professional oversight and the rules of the sport.
4. Weigh-in timing changes the performance problem
A same-day weigh-in leaves little recovery time for fluid, electrolyte, carbohydrate, and gastrointestinal restoration. A longer interval between weigh-in and competition permits more recovery but does not guarantee complete restoration after a large cut. The engine should therefore collect weigh-in timing before prescribing competition-week training or nutrition. The current source base does not justify one universal safe percentage of acute body-mass loss for all athletes and contexts.
5. Final-week food strategy
Favor familiar foods and routines. Do not introduce major dietary experiments in the final days.
Maintain protein intake and adequate total energy unless a defined weight strategy requires a controlled deficit.
Preserve carbohydrate availability for high-intensity sport and competition, especially once training volume has fallen enough to allow glycogen restoration.
Match fiber, meal size, and gastrointestinal load to the athlete's event timing and known tolerance rather than making abrupt changes without a reason.
Hydration should be normal and deliberate unless an expert-supervised weight-class plan temporarily changes it.
6. Supplements and stimulants
Competition week is a poor time to test a new supplement, pre-workout, sleep aid, or recovery protocol. In tested sport, contamination and banned-substance risk must also be considered. The Advanced Layer recommends third-party certification and conservative supplement use; this is a sensible project rule even though supplementation is not the core of the taper. [ADV, Art. 75]
7. Travel and jet lag
Mujika distinguishes short-lived travel fatigue from jet lag after crossing multiple time zones. Jet lag can impair strength, reaction time, and arousal. The source advises against training hard immediately before departure merely because the athlete will rest on the plane and recommends integrating reduced training load with the taper rather than allowing travel to become an extra stressor. [MJ, Ch. 5]
The engine should collect destination, travel duration, time-zone change, arrival date, event date, sleep opportunity, and equipment access. If travel creates a major circadian disruption, the taper should protect recovery and sport-specific feel rather than chasing normal training volume.
8. Practical decision rules
IF training volume falls and the athlete is not making weight, THEN do not automatically slash carbohydrate; allow glycogen restoration to support competition readiness.
IF a weight cut is suppressing mood, power, sleep, or technical quality, THEN reduce nonessential training before adding more work to compensate.
IF weigh-in is close to competition, THEN be more conservative with any acute body-mass strategy because recovery time is limited.
IF travel crosses multiple time zones, THEN build arrival and acclimation into the taper rather than treating travel as a neutral rest day.
IF a food, supplement, stimulant, recovery method, or sleep aid is unfamiliar, THEN do not introduce it merely because competition is important.

Competition day should use rehearsed systems. The warm-up should raise temperature, mobility, technical readiness, and arousal without becoming another workout. Competition is not the day to solve every uncertainty that training failed to solve.
5. If the taper seems to have failed
Check whether fatigue was actually reduced enough. The athlete may simply have needed a longer taper after a harder pretaper block.
Check whether intensity or frequency was reduced too far and the athlete lost feel, rhythm, or high-output readiness.
Check whether weight cutting, travel, illness, sleep, or psychological stress masked the taper response.
Check whether the competition task was poorly simulated or the wrong physical quality was prioritized during the peak.
Do not conclude from one event that the entire taper model is invalid. Record the response and adjust one major variable next time.
6. Post-competition transition
Competition can leave local tissue stress, sleep disruption, emotional fatigue, travel fatigue, and in combat sport substantial contact damage. The next phase should begin with assessment, not an automatic return to full training. The Advanced Layer proposes a restoration and rebuild phase; the exact day counts are useful planning heuristics but should be individualized rather than treated as fixed physiology. [ADV, Art. 96]
First assess physical status, soreness, pain or injury flags, sleep, body mass and refeeding if applicable, and psychological state.
Use light movement or rest until normal function and motivation begin to return. Medical assessment takes priority when injury is suspected.
Reintroduce training volume progressively rather than jumping immediately to pretaper workload.
Debrief the event while details are fresh: what failed, what worked, whether the limiter was physical, technical, tactical, pacing, nutrition, or decision making.
Select the next block from what the competition revealed and the date and priority of the next event.
7. Learning the athlete's taper fingerprint
After each meaningful competition, save: pretaper training load, taper duration, volume reduction, intensity exposure, frequency, final hard sessions, weight status, travel, sleep, subjective freshness, competition performance, and the athlete's comments about feeling sharp or flat. Over several events this becomes far more valuable than generic percentage tables because it estimates the individual balance between fatigue dissipation and loss of feel.
8. Final decision rules
IF multiple recovery signals improve and competition-specific output improves, THEN do not change the taper merely to satisfy a predetermined workload target.
IF the athlete feels fresh earlier than expected, THEN preserve freshness; do not treat it as a problem unless technical feel or arousal clearly deteriorates.
IF the athlete remains fatigued close to Day 0, THEN remove nonessential load immediately and accept that late fitness cannot be rescued by more training.
IF one metric is abnormal but sport-specific performance and the broader context are good, THEN monitor rather than automatically intervening.
IF the competition reveals a clear limiter, THEN the next training block should address that limiter rather than repeating the same peak with more effort.

Appendix: Engine-Wide Competition Preparation Decision System
The engine should construct the correct competition-preparation shape from the athlete and event rather than generate a normal training plan and then patch it with last-week prohibitions.
A. Core reasoning pipeline
1. Define the event and Day 0: fight, BJJ tournament, strength meet, high-fidelity mock meet, technical mock meet, hybrid event, or other competition.
2. Set event priority: A, B, or C priority. The importance of the event determines how much long-term training is sacrificed for short-term readiness.
3. Define performance demands: maximal force, power, technical skill, tactical decision making, repeated high-intensity work, aerobic support, pacing, weight class, rules, and event duration.
4. Audit current fitness and likely limiter. Do not peak every quality equally.
5. Audit the previous 1 to 3 weeks of fatigue: volume, hard sport sessions, local soreness, sleep, travel, weight manipulation, and performance trends.
6. Choose a peak state: development, specificity, realization, taper, competition week, or postcompetition transition.
7. Choose a provisional taper duration from athlete history. If history is absent, use source-grounded population defaults conservatively.
8. Reduce training load mainly by cutting nonessential volume and high-cost work while preserving relevant intensity, frequency, and technical feel.
9. Protect against residual fatigue: novelty, eccentric damage, contact, near-failure work, redundant conditioning, and unplanned tests.
10. Integrate nutrition, weight-class strategy, travel, sleep, and psychological readiness.
11. Monitor performance plus internal cost and recovery. Make the smallest change that solves the actual problem.
12. Compete using rehearsed warm-up, tactical or attempt logic, and fueling.
13. Record the taper response and use it to improve the next competition cycle.

D. Constructive program-generation brief
Example engine instruction: "The athlete is 9 days from an A-priority BJJ tournament after a high-load camp. Shift the goal from adaptation toward freshness and expression. Preserve technical frequency and competition-speed movement, remove redundant severe conditioning, cut gym volume substantially while retaining only familiar low-cost strength exposures, avoid novel and high-soreness work, integrate the weight-class plan, and reassess sport sharpness, soreness, sleep, and body mass each day. Do not add workload because the athlete begins to feel fresh."

F. Unresolved source gaps for the next revision
Dedicated combat S&C source: exact camp sequencing, sparring withdrawal, strength maintenance dose, and fight-week practices by combat sport.
Combat weight-cut source: evidence-based acute weight management and rehydration by weigh-in window, with explicit medical and safety boundaries.
Strength-sport peaking source: if future versions need lift-specific realization, attempt selection, and fatigue timelines. This is optional for the current conceptual scope.
More current taper meta-analysis: Mujika is foundational but the book was published in 2009. A later update could refine effect sizes and sport-specific conclusions.
Individual taper-response model: the engine should eventually learn from repeated athlete competitions rather than relying mainly on population priors.
G. Cluster-wide summary
The final coaching principle is simple but demanding: build fitness early, increase specificity as competition approaches, reduce training load mainly by removing volume and high residual-fatigue work, preserve the intensity and frequency needed to retain force, speed, skill, and feel, integrate nutrition and travel, monitor the athlete rather than one metric, and arrive at Day 0 with nothing important left to recover from.
