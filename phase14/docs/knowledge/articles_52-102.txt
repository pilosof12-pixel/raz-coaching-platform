Article 52: GPP Decision Rules — Choosing the Right Support Work for the Athlete, Goal, and Fatigue Budget

How This Article Sits Inside the Cluster

This is the routing piece. Articles 45 through 51 cover the tools. This one orders them and writes the rules.

The Five-Question Filter

Before any GPP or conditioning piece is added, the coach answers:

- What is the priority quality for this block?
- What is the limiting structure right now?
- What is the fatigue budget left after priority work?
- Which equipment and modality is pain tolerant?
- What does the calendar require in the next four weeks?

Quality and Tool Matrix

- Aerobic base. Bike, rower, ski erg, easy swim, sled walks, hills at easy pace.
- Tempo and extensive intervals. Bike, rower, ski erg, runs for tolerant athletes.
- Alactic power. Short hill sprints, bike or ski erg sprints, broad jumps, throws.
- Alactic capacity. Short bike intervals, repeated short hills, battle ropes, sandbag bursts.
- Sport-specific intervals. Round timed work for combat, event mimic for strongman.
- Loaded GPP. Sled, prowler, yoke, carries, medleys.
- Plyometrics. Pogos, skips, broad jumps, box jumps, bounds, depth jumps, throws.
- Restoration. Walks, easy bike, mobility, pool flush.

Selection by Limiting Structure

- Trunk stiffness limited. Bias suitcase carries, sled push, slow yoke if tolerated.
- Scapular control limited. Bias unilateral carries, controlled rowing, throw progressions.
- Hip capacity limited. Bias sled push, low box step ups, A and B skips.
- Knee tolerance limited. Bias backward sled drag, bike, pool, low box work.
- Foot and ankle limited. Bias pool, bike, controlled pogos only when tolerated.
- Neck and grip limited for grapplers. Bias bike or rower over rope and carry, reduce grip GPP.
- Aerobic base limited. Bias Zone 2 work across multiple modalities.
- Repeated sprint ability limited. Bias short interval work matched to sport demand.

Decision Rules

- Rule one. The priority quality gets the cleanest day. GPP supports it, never crowds it.
- Rule two. If two pieces compete for the same tissue, drop one.
- Rule three. Loaded GPP and heavy lower work do not share a day at full dose.
- Rule four. Hard intervals and heavy lower work do not share a day at full dose.
- Rule five. Skill and sport sessions come before GPP in the day order.
- Rule six. When in doubt, do less. Capacity comes from repeatable weeks, not from a hero session.

If, Then Routing Across Categories

- If athlete is an advanced powerlifter, then GPP is two Zone 2 sessions, one short loaded carry tail, optional plyo priming, restoration as needed.
- If athlete is a strongman, then GPP includes event mimic medleys, one Zone 2, one alactic capacity, restoration.
- If athlete is a BJJ competitor, then GPP includes two Zone 2, one sport-specific interval co-scheduled with mat, light plyo and throw, careful grip and neck management.
- If athlete is MMA, then GPP includes sport-specific round intervals, two Zone 2, light alactic, careful sparring count.
- If athlete is calisthenics or gymnastics focused, then GPP includes scapular and trunk biased carries, low-impact aerobic base, restorative pool work, careful plyo dosing.
- If athlete is hybrid, then priority quality drives the calendar. The other quality holds at maintenance.

Fatigue Triggers Across Tools

- Bar speed drop on planned submax. Pull GPP volume before SPP volume.
- Resting heart rate climbing. Cut intensity, keep easy aerobic.
- Sleep disruption after late conditioning. Move sessions earlier.
- Recurrent local pain at one site. Pause that tool. Switch modality. Coach review. Clinician review if not resolving.

Pre-Competition Tapering

- Two to three weeks out. Cut interval volume. Keep alactic taps and easy aerobic.
- One week out. Walks, very easy spins, sharp short throws or jumps, technique only.
- Day before. Movement and rest.

Prerequisites and Substitutions Summary

- Loaded carries need a clean brace and hinge.
- Plyometrics need clean landing mechanics. Depth jumps need a strength baseline.
- Intervals need an aerobic base.
- Pool work needs basic water competence.
- Breath restricted work is not recommended without specialist supervision. The safer path is paced nasal breathing on a bike or rower.

Conservative Injury Language for the Generator

When the article assigns work near a sensitive site, output should use phrases like:

- May support tolerance.
- Often a more tolerable pathway.
- Pause and review.
- Coach review. Clinician review if symptoms persist.

The generator should never claim to prevent injury, diagnose pain, or guarantee a result.

Program Generator Notes

- Tags: gpprouting, decisionrules, fatiguebudget, calendaraware.
- Routing: this article supplies the priority filter and modality selector for every other article in the cluster.
- When to include: every athlete. Treat as the rulebook layer.
- When to exclude: never. The rules still apply during peak and deload, only the dose changes.
- What to avoid: a flat schedule that treats every tool as equal, hard intervals stacked on heavy lower days, prescribing GPP that maps onto the limiter rather than supporting it.

Module 0 — The Exercise Selection / Stress-Cost Scoring Layer (Shared Engine)

This is the connective tissue across Articles 53–60. Every article references these constructs. The generator should treat this section as the canonical definition and never redefine the formulas inconsistently elsewhere.

The core idea: a combat athlete has a finite, multi-channel recovery budget, and the sport itself already spends most of it. Strength work is the last claimant on that budget, not the first. So we do not select exercises by "what builds the most strength" — we select by strength return per unit of total systemic cost, given what the mat already took this week.

0.1 The Cost Channels

We track five recovery channels independently because they recover at different rates and the sport loads them unevenly:

Channel  What it is  Recovers in  Primary sport drain
Spinal  Cumulative compressive/shear/flexion-extension load on the spine and surrounding tissue  48–96 h (slow)  Scrambles, takedown defense, guard, bridging, stacking
CNS  High-threshold motor output, maximal/near-maximal intent, speed-strength  24–72 h (intent-dependent)  Hard sparring, explosive exchanges, max-effort scrambles
Peripheral (local)  Muscle/connective tissue fatigue and soreness in a specific region  24–96 h (region-dependent)  Grip, neck, forearms, hips, lower back
Skill/coordination  Motor-learning bandwidth and freshness for technique work  12–48 h  Technical drilling, new positions
Tissue tolerance  A constraint, not a fuel tank — current capacity of a region to accept contact/load without symptom flare  Variable  Contact, repeated impact, end-range loading

0.2 Spinal Debt

The spine is the single most over-drafted channel in grappling, so it gets its own running ledger.

Spinal Debt (SD) formula — weekly running score:

SDweek = Σ (SpinalLoadi × Densityi × FatigueStatei)

where for each session i:
  SpinalLoadi   = sum of exercise spinal-compression/shear scores (0–3 each)
                    + mat spinal load estimate
  Density_i       = 1.0 normal, 1.25 if two spinal-heavy sessions <24 h apart
  FatigueStatei = 1.0 fresh, 1.3 if entering session already sore/stiff in low back


Mat spinal-load estimate (per session):

Session type  Mat spinal load
Technical drilling, flow  1
Positional rounds, moderate  3
Hard sparring / live wrestling  5
Competition / hard takedown battles  7

SD interpretation (rolling 7-day):

SD_week  State  Programming response
0–10  Green  Normal spinal loading allowed (deadlift variants, loaded carries, heavy hinge)
11–18  Amber  Cap axial loading; prefer supported/neutral-spine variants; no max hinge
19–26  Red  Remove spinal-compressive lifting; substitute to low-SD options
27+  Black  Strength session becomes restorative only; reassess weekly mat load

Coaching note: SD is additive across mat and gym. The most common programming error is scoring the gym in isolation. A "light" 70% deadlift day on top of a hard wrestling week is not light — it is a Red-zone decision.

0.3 CNS Fatigue Score

CNS cost is driven by intent and proximity to maximum, not just load. A fast, maximal-intent movement at 60% can cost more CNS than a grindy 85% single.

CNS Fatigue Score (CFS) — per session, then summed weekly:

CFSsession = Σ (Intenti × Proximityi × Volumefactor_i)

  Intent_i      = 1 (controlled), 2 (forceful), 3 (maximal/ballistic)
  Proximity_i   = 1 (≤70% / RIR≥3), 2 (70–85% / RIR 1–2), 3 (>85% / RIR 0)
  Volume_factor = 1 (≤3 hard sets), 1.5 (4–6), 2 (7+)

Add mat CNS load:
  +2 hard sparring,  +3 competition,  +1 hard positional


CFS interpretation (per microcycle / rolling 7-day):

CFS_week  State  Response
≤14  Recoverable  Plan a true high day this week
15–24  Loaded  One high day max; keep speed work crisp and low-volume
25–32  Saturated  No max-effort gym work; convert to medium; protect sparring quality
33+  Overdrawn  Strength = maintenance dose only; flag deload candidate

Rule of thumb: sparring quality is the canary. If bar speed and sparring sharpness both drop, CNS is overdrawn regardless of what the number says — the number serves the eye, not the reverse.

0.4 Peripheral (Local) Fatigue Table

Region  Typical recovery after hard load  Sport reload demand  Reload caution
Grip / forearms  24–48 h, but cumulative across a week  Every session (gi especially)  High-grip lifting day before gi sparring degrades grip endurance on the mat
Neck  48–72 h (slow, tendon/connective heavy)  Constant in grappling  Never train neck to true failure within 72 h of hard sparring
Lower back (erectors/QL)  48–96 h  Constant  Most over-drafted; see Spinal Debt
Hips/adductors/glutes  24–72 h  High (guard, shots)  Heavy adductor eccentrics can degrade shot speed for 48 h
Shoulders (cuff/scapular)  24–72 h  High (framing, posting, pressing)  Pressing volume + heavy posting in sparring stacks local cost
Lats/upper back  24–48 h  Moderate  Recovers fast; good "safe" volume sink
Quads  24–48 h  Moderate  Heavy eccentric squats sore for shots/sprawls
Hamstrings  48–72 h  Moderate–high  Eccentric-biased work lingers; respect before live

0.5 Exercise Stress-Cost Scoring Dimensions

Every exercise the generator considers is scored 0–3 on each dimension (0 = negligible, 3 = severe). The sum is the exercise's Total Stress Cost (TSC); individual dimensions also gate placement.

Dimension  What it captures
Spinal compression/shear  Axial load + shear on spine
Eccentric cost  Magnitude/duration of lengthening tension
Grip/forearm cost  Demand on grip and forearm flexors
Local tissue cost  Concentrated load on one region
Skill cost  Coordination/learning bandwidth used
CNS cost  Intent × proximity to max
Soreness risk  Likelihood of next-day soreness affecting mat
Sport interference  Direct conflict with imminent mat quality
Injury-constraint cost  How much it loads a currently sensitive/limited region

TSC = sum of all nine (0–27). But TSC alone never decides placement — the highest individual dimension governs day-type and timing. A TSC-9 exercise that scores 3 on Spinal in a Red SD week is disallowed even though its total looks moderate.

0.6 Low-Cost Substitution Library

When a channel is over-budget, swap to same-pattern, lower-cost options:

Pattern  Standard (higher cost)  Low-spinal sub  Low-CNS sub  Low-grip sub
Hinge  Conventional DL  Hip thrust, back-extension  RDL at tempo light  Machine ham, hip thrust
Squat  Back squat  Belt squat, hack  Tempo goblet  Belt squat, leg press
Pull  Heavy bent row  Chest-supported row  Light cable row  Straps / chest-supported
Press  Barbell OHP  Landmine, machine press  DB press at RIR 3  Machine press
Carry  Heavy farmer  Front-rack carry (lighter)  Short distance  Yoke/zercher (less grip)

0.7 How Scoring Drives Programming

1. Read the week first. Estimate mat spinal/CNS load before touching the gym plan. The gym fills the remaining budget.
2. Assign day types — High / Medium / Low — based on CFS and SD state, then place high days away from the hardest mat sessions.
3. Filter exercises by the governing dimension for that day and that athlete's current constraints.
4. Substitute, don't cancel. A channel being over-budget changes the exercise, rarely the session. Maintenance trumps zero.
5. Deload triggers when two or more channels are simultaneously in Red, OR when sparring quality drops for 2+ sessions, OR a constraint flag escalates.

Universal day-type definitions used across the cluster:

- High day: highest intent/load gym session of the week; only when CFS ≤14 and SD ≤10; placed 24–48 h before a rest or light mat day, never before hard sparring.
- Medium day: moderate load, RIR 1–3, no max intent; the default during camps.
- Low/Restorative day: pumps, blood flow, positional isometrics, mobility-with-load; CNS ≈ 0; usable even in Red weeks.

Article 53 — Strength Training Around Hard Sparring Weeks

Coaching Problem

Hard sparring weeks are the highest-value, highest-cost training the athlete does. The mistake coaches make is treating the gym as an independent variable — programming a normal hypertrophy or strength block on top of weeks where the mat is already drawing maximally on the CNS and spinal channels. The result is degraded sparring quality, accumulating Spinal Debt, and the illusion that the athlete is "lazy" when they are simply overdrawn. The coach who fixes this stops asking "what should the gym do this week?" and starts asking "what budget did the mat leave me?"

The deeper trap is that hard sparring feels like conditioning, so coaches under-count its strength-channel cost. A live wrestling round is a series of near-maximal isometric and explosive efforts under fatigue — it is, in CNS and spinal terms, indistinguishable from a heavy lifting session the body has to recover from. Two of those in a week, plus a "normal" lower-body day, is three heavy CNS/spinal exposures stacked on a recovery budget built for maybe two.

Operating Principles

- The mat is the priority stimulus during a hard sparring week. The gym protects and maintains; it does not compete.
- Strength qualities are retained for 2–3 weeks even at maintenance dosing. There is no urgency to "make progress" during a hard sparring block — only urgency to not erode it.
- The two channels most at risk are CNS and Spinal. Peripheral grip is a close third in gi weeks.
- Place the gym in the troughs, not on the peaks. The best lifting day of a hard sparring week is the one with the most recovery buffer before the next live session.

Stress-Cost Implications

A hard sparring session adds +2 CFS and +5 mat spinal load by the Module 0 estimates. Two hard sparring sessions in a week place an athlete near Amber CFS and Amber/Red SD before any lifting. Therefore:

- Heavy hinge (Spinal 3) is effectively disallowed mid-week.
- Max-effort gym work (CNS 3) competes directly with sparring sharpness and is converted to medium.
- Grip-cost ≥2 the day before gi sparring degrades on-mat grip endurance — a direct, observable interference cost.

Worked SD/CFS example — a typical 2-hard-spar week (Tue + Thu live):

Mat-only baseline before any lifting:
- Mon drilling: spinal 1, CFS 0
- Tue hard spar: spinal 5, CFS +2
- Wed technical: spinal 1, CFS 0
- Thu hard spar: spinal 5, CFS +2
- Fri positional: spinal 3, CFS +1
- Mat-only SD ≈ 15 (Amber), mat-only CFS ≈ 5.

Adding a naive "normal" leg day Wednesday: back squat 4×5 @ 82% (Spinal 2, CNS 6) and conventional deadlift 3×3 @ 85% (Spinal 3, CNS 6) pushes SD into Red and dumps fresh CNS fatigue directly into Thursday's sparring. The Density 1.25 multiplier applies when the Wednesday lift sits less than 24 h after Tuesday's spar and is spinal-heavy.

Programming Framework

A repaired 2-hard-spar week:

Day  Mat  Gym day-type  Focus  Notes
Mon  Drilling  Medium  Belt squat 4×6 @ RIR 2, chest-supported row 3×10, front-rack carry  Spinal ≤1, CNS modest, full buffer to Tue
Tue  Hard spar  None / AM restorative  Neck isometric maintenance, light grip flush  Protect the spar
Wed  Technical  Low  Hip thrust 3×8, back-extension 3×12, sled, lat volume  Spinal 0–1, CNS ≈ 0
Thu  Hard spar  None  —  Protect the spar
Fri  Positional  Medium  Landmine press 4×6, pull volume, hip/adductor low-cost  Upper bias, low spinal
Sat  Optional roll  High (only if CFS≤14)  One heavy main lift, low-spinal  Most buffer to next hard spar
Sun  Off  —  —  —

Exercise Selection and Tradeoffs

- Belt squat vs back squat: belt squat keeps the quad/leg-drive stimulus at Spinal 0–1 instead of Spinal 2–3. Large drop in spinal cost for a trade of slightly less trunk/bracing stimulus — which the mat already over-supplies.
- Hip thrust vs deadlift: the hip thrust delivers hip-extension strength at Spinal 0–1 and near-zero shear, versus Spinal 3 for conventional pulls. Acceptable mid-week.
- Chest-supported row vs heavy bent row: removes both the spinal cost and the grip-as-limiter problem.
- Sled vs any eccentric leg work: sled is concentric-only (Eccentric 0, Soreness 0–1), so it builds work capacity and leg drive without next-day soreness that would slow shots.

If/Then Rules

- If two hard spars are scheduled and CFS ≥15 → no High gym day this week; cap at Medium.
- If SD reaches Red → replace all hinge/squat axial work with belt squat + back-extension at low load.
- If sparring sharpness drops two sessions running → cut gym to restorative regardless of the calendar.
- If a heavy lift is desired → place it the day after the last hard spar with ≥36 h before the next, never the day before.
- If gi sparring is next-day → grip-cost ≤1 in the gym today.
- If two spinal-heavy exposures fall <24 h apart → apply Density 1.25 and re-check the SD tier before finalizing.

Common Mistakes

- Programming a "leg day" (squat + deadlift) into a hard sparring week and blaming the athlete for flat rounds.
- Chasing PRs because the block "called for it" while the real priority was the sparring.
- Treating gym soreness as harmless — DOMS in quads/hams directly degrades shot and sprawl speed.
- Scoring the gym in isolation and missing the Density multiplier when a lift sits next to a spar.

Generator-Use Rules

- Detect "hard sparring week" flag → cap gym at Medium by default; unlock one High day only if CFS ≤14 and SD ≤10 after adding mat estimates.
- Compute Density 1.25 automatically whenever a spinal-scored lift is within 24 h of a mat session of spinal load ≥5.
- Auto-substitute any Spinal-3 or CNS-3 exercise to the 0.6 library equivalent when SD/CFS thresholds are crossed, and log the substitution reason.
- Never schedule grip-cost ≥2 within 24 h before a gi session; route to a low-grip variant.
- Place the highest-load gym session at the calendar slot with maximal buffer to the next live session; never the day before.
- Output conservative language only; never claim the plan "prevents injury."

Article 54 — Strength Training During MMA/BJJ Competition Camp

Coaching Problem

Camp compresses everything: more sparring, more conditioning, weight management, and rising psychological stress, all while the temptation to "get stronger for the fight" peaks. Adding training stress in camp usually subtracts from fight readiness. The job of strength work in camp is fidelity — keep the qualities the athlete already built, stay fresh, and get out of the sport's way.

Most blown camps share a signature: the gym volume that worked in the off-season was carried into camp unchanged, so total systemic load climbed week over week until sparring quality, then mood, then bodyweight management all degraded together. The strength coach's contribution to a good camp is almost entirely subtractive — knowing precisely how little is enough and removing everything else.

Operating Principles

- Camp is a taper of strength stress and a peak of sport stress. Gym volume should decrease across camp while intensity is kept just high enough to maintain.
- Maintenance requires surprisingly little: approximately 2 hard sets per major pattern at a meaningful load (≥80% or RIR ≤2), 1–2×/week, retains strength for a standard 6–8 week camp.
- Weight cuts compress every recovery channel. As the cut tightens, all gym cost dimensions must drop.
- Novelty is the enemy in camp. A new exercise carries skill cost and soreness risk — two dimensions that buy nothing and can cost a sparring session.

Programming Framework — Camp Phasing

Camp phase  Weeks out  Gym dose  Day types  CNS ceiling
Early  8–6  Highest gym tolerance of camp  2 Medium, occasional 1 High early-week  CNS ≤2
Mid  5–3  Maintenance, volume trimmed  2 Medium  CNS ≤2
Late  2  Minimal dose  1 Medium + 1 Low  CNS ≤1
Fight week  1  Movement only  1–2 Low/restorative, sharp and brief  CNS ≤1, no soreness

Maintenance template (mid-camp, 2×/week):

- Day A (Medium): belt squat 2×3 @ RIR 2 (Spinal ≤1), horizontal pull 3×8, front-rack carry, neck isometric maintenance. Placed ≥36 h before hard sparring.
- Day B (Medium): hip thrust 3×6, landmine press 3×6 @ RIR 2, low-cost hip/adductor, grip maintenance only. Placed on a lighter mat day.

The gym's job is to fall as the mat rises. The gym contribution line descends (12 → 8 → 5 → 2 in CFS units) while the mat line climbs across the camp. If the gym line is flat or rising across a camp, the plan is wrong regardless of how good each individual session looks.

Exercise Selection and Tradeoffs

- Machines/belt squat vs free-bar maxes: machine and belt-supported variants deliver near-equal maintenance stimulus at lower spinal/skill/CNS cost.
- Short crisp jumps vs heavy lower-body: 2–3 low-volume jumps keep speed-strength "switched on" at a fraction of the CNS cost of heavy squats. Cut entirely in late camp.
- Sled vs eccentric leg work: sled keeps conditioning and leg drive alive with Eccentric 0 — no soreness to compromise sparring. Eccentric-biased work is a late-camp liability.
- During the cut: drop grip-cost and eccentric-cost to ≤1; a glycogen- and fluid-depleted athlete bruises and sores more easily and recovers slower.

If/Then Rules

- If weeks-out ≤2 → no exercise with Eccentric cost ≥2.
- If in an active weight cut → all gym TSC ceilings drop one tier; convert High→Medium, Medium→Low.
- If CFS Saturated for 2 consecutive weeks → drop to one gym session, maintenance only, and flag for the head coach.
- If the athlete reports a flagged sensitive region → reduce injury-constraint-cost loading to 0 on that region; substitute the pattern.
- If fight week → only crisp, sub-maximal, confidence-building movement; nothing that can create soreness.
- If a new exercise is requested in camp → deny by default (skill + soreness cost); keep the patterns the athlete already owns.

Generator-Use Rules

- Require a "weeks_out" input; auto-select the phase template and clamp CNS/eccentric/spinal ceilings accordingly.
- If a "weightcutactive" flag is set, drop every cost ceiling one additional tier and disable jumps/throws.
- In fight week, hard-block any Eccentric ≥2, CNS ≥2, Spinal ≥2 exercise, and any exercise not already used earlier in camp.
- Auto-trim volume_factor across phases so gym CFS contribution falls each phase even if the athlete asks for more.
- Never present strength changes as fight-outcome guarantees; keep language about maintenance and freshness.

Article 55 — Lower-Back Management for Guard Players and Wrestlers

Coaching Problem

Guard players and wrestlers live in repeated flexion, extension, rotation, and axial load on the mat. The lower back is the single most over-drafted region in grappling. When coaches then program heavy axial lifting "for a strong back," they double-dip the most fatigued, slowest-recovering region. The goal is building tolerant, capable trunk strength while keeping the spinal channel out of chronic debt — framed strictly as load management, never as treating a condition.

The conceptual error is treating the deadlift as the definition of a "strong back." For a grappler whose mat life is already a relentless spinal load, the deadlift is often the least cost-efficient way to build trunk capacity. Carries, anti-movement work, and hip-dominant patterns build a robust, capable trunk at a fraction of the spinal cost.

Operating Principles

- The lower back's gym budget is a remainder, computed after mat spinal load. Spinal Debt is the governing number here.
- Build trunk capacity primarily through lower-spinal-cost tools: anti-movement bracing, supported extension, controlled loaded carries, and hip-dominant work that spares the axial spine.
- Heavy axial loading (conventional deadlifts, heavy good mornings) is a periodized minority of the work, used only in Green SD windows and away from hard mat days.
- A stiff-back day is a signal to downgrade, not to "work through it." The Fatigue_State multiplier exists precisely for this.

Programming Framework

Trunk capacity menu, organized by spinal cost:

Spinal cost  Tools  Weekly role
0–1  Loaded carries, suitcase/front-rack, dead-bug progressions, Pallof, reverse hyper light, back-extension (bodyweight→light)  Staple — most of the volume
2  RDL moderate, Zercher carry, moderate good morning, trap-bar (neutral)  Periodic, Green SD only
3  Heavy conventional/sumo, heavy good morning  Rare, Green SD, away from hard mat

Sample week (wrestler, 2 hard mat sessions):

- Early week (Medium): front-rack carries + suitcase carries + Pallof anti-rotation + hip thrust 3×8. SD contribution ≈ 1–2.
- Mid week (Low): back-extension (controlled) + reverse hyper light + dead-bug. SD ≈ 0–1.
- One moderate hinge (trap-bar or RDL, Spinal 2) only if SD ≤10 on a buffered day.

If/Then Rules

- If SD Amber → all hinging capped at Spinal ≤1 (hip thrust, back-extension); no barbell deadlift.
- If SD Red → trunk work becomes carries + bracing only; remove all hinge load.
- If the athlete reports low-back stiffness entering a session → apply Fatigue_State 1.3, downgrade the plan one tier, and avoid end-range loading (conservative, non-diagnostic).
- If a Green SD window appears (light mat week) → that is when to schedule the rare heavy hinge.
- If a wrestler has heavy takedown sparring next day → no Spinal ≥2 today.
- If Density and Fatigue_State multipliers both apply to one session → re-check the SD tier before finalizing; they compound.

Generator-Use Rules

- Compute SD every week including mat estimates; gate hinge cost by the SD tier.
- Apply FatigueState 1.3 automatically when a "lowback_stiff" flag is set for the day, and Density 1.25 when a spinal lift is <24 h from a mat session of spinal load ≥5.
- Default the hinge slot to hip thrust / back-extension; only offer barbell deadlift when SD ≤10 and ≥48 h buffer to hard mat.
- Use load-management language only — never "fix," "heal," "rehab," or "prevent injury."

Article 56 — Neck Training for Grapplers: Isometrics, Harness Work, and Contact Tolerance

Coaching Problem

The neck is loaded constantly in grappling — framing, posting, bridging, defending guillotines and front headlocks, scrambles. It is a slow-recovering, connective-tissue-heavy region (48–72 h). Coaches either ignore it or train it aggressively to failure, both errors. The objective is building neck strength and contact tolerance with conservative, sub-maximal, well-spaced dosing — described as capacity work, never as injury prevention.

The neck punishes impatience more than almost any region. It adapts slowly, sores deeply, and the sport never gives it a day off. The coach's job is to find the small, repeatable dose that builds capacity over months without ever colliding with a hard sparring session.

Operating Principles

- Neck work is almost never trained to failure and almost never within 72 h of hard sparring.
- Isometrics first. They build strength with minimal soreness and minimal local tissue cost.
- Progress time, then position, then gentle load, in that order. Range and contact tolerance build slowly.
- Consistency beats intensity here: 2 small doses a week for months outperforms occasional hard sessions.

Programming Framework

Progression ladder (conservative):

1. Isometric holds — flexion, extension, both lateral sides. Start 3×10–20 s sub-maximal against hand/band.
2. Longer/loaded isometrics — light head-harness held statically, or banded, longer durations.
3. Slow dynamic — controlled harness flexion/extension through a comfortable range, light load, high control.
4. Contact-tolerance integration — bridging / wrestler's-bridge variations progressed gradually, only when prior tiers are comfortable.

Dosing: 2×/week, sub-maximal, placed away from hard sparring (ideally a clean window before the next live session). 3–4 directions, low volume each. Progress one variable at a time.

Exercise Selection and Tradeoffs

- Manual/banded isometrics vs harness: isometrics give the most strength per unit of soreness and require no equipment — the right default and the right re-entry point after any layoff.
- Isometric vs dynamic: isometrics let you train an exact angle with near-zero soreness; dynamic harness work trains through range but carries more soreness risk.
- Bridging progression: supported/partial bridges build contact tolerance at lower cost than full bridges; rushing to full bridging is the classic over-reach.

If/Then Rules

- If hard sparring within 72 h → neck work is isometric maintenance only, sub-maximal, low volume — or skipped.
- If the athlete reports any neck stiffness or sensitivity → reduce to gentle isometrics in a comfortable range only; do not load into discomfort (conservative, non-diagnostic).
- If new to neck training → start with isometrics for 2–3 weeks before any harness load.
- If competition week → maintenance isometrics only; no novel load.
- If bridging is introduced → progress from supported/partial before full; never rush range.
- If the weekly calendar offers no clean 72 h window → default to maintenance isometrics, never harness.

Generator-Use Rules

- Hard-block neck training within 72 h of a hard sparring flag (allow only sub-maximal isometric maintenance).
- Default new athletes to the isometric tier; gate harness/dynamic work behind 2–3 weeks of logged isometrics.
- Set CNS cost for neck work to 0; never schedule it as a "hard" element or count it toward a High day.
- Score any harness/dynamic neck work the day before a spar as Sport-interference 3 and block it.
- Use capacity/tolerance language; never claim it prevents injury.

Article 57 — Shoulder Durability for BJJ: Framing, Posting, and Pressing Selection

Coaching Problem

BJJ loads the shoulder in vulnerable, end-range, often-fatigued positions: framing under pressure, posting on scrambles, defending kimuras/americanas, stiff-arming. Many lifters bring a bench-heavy pressing diet that over-loads the front of the shoulder and under-builds the cuff, scapular control, and overhead/end-range capacity the sport actually demands. The goal is balanced shoulder strength biased toward control, scapular stability, and tolerable end-range positions — framed as building capacity, not as injury prevention.

Operating Principles

- Pull volume ≥ push volume for grapplers; the sport and most lifting programs already over-bias the front.
- Build the cuff and scapular stabilizers directly and frequently — low cost, high value.
- Choose pressing variations that load shoulder-friendly angles and let the scapula move freely (landmine, neutral-grip DB, incline) over high-volume flat barbell bench.
- End-range capacity is built patiently and in comfortable ranges first.

Programming Framework

Weekly shoulder structure:

Slot  Tool  Sets  Cost notes
Primary pull  Row / pull-up variants, 8–15 reps  6–10  Low spinal if chest-supported
Primary press  Landmine / neutral DB / incline, RIR 1–2  4–6  Moderate local
Cuff/scapular  External rotation, prone/wall scap, face pulls  6–10 across week, 2–3×/wk  Very low cost
End-range capacity  Controlled overhead carries, light bottoms-up, gradual overhead loading  3–6  Low–moderate, progress slowly

Pull:push volume ratio target ≈ 1.5–2 : 1.

If/Then Rules

- If the athlete reports shoulder sensitivity in a position → reduce injury-constraint-cost on that range to 0; keep pressing/posting work in comfortable ranges, bias cuff/scapular work (non-diagnostic).
- If heavy posting / scramble sparring next day → cap pressing local cost ≤1 today.
- If introducing overhead pressing → progress landmine → neutral DB → barbell over weeks, not in one jump.
- If push volume has crept above pull volume → rebalance before adding any pressing intensity.
- If pull:push falls below 1.3:1 → the generator rebalances by trimming pressing sets and adding pulling before anything else.

Generator-Use Rules

- Enforce a pull:push volume ratio ≥1.3:1 for grappling profiles; auto-add pulling / trim pressing to hit it.
- Default the press slot to landmine / neutral-grip; gate barbell overhead behind a logged progression flag.
- Always include low-cost cuff/scapular work 2–3×/week regardless of phase.
- Cap pressing local cost ≤1 within 24 h of a heavy-posting sparring flag.
- Capacity language only; no injury-prevention or guarantee claims.

Article 58 — Hip Strength for Guard Retention, Passing, and Takedowns

Coaching Problem

The hips power nearly every grappling action: guard retention and recovery, passing pressure, shots, sprawls, bridging. Yet hip training is often reduced to back squats, which over-load the spine and under-train the hip's true demands — abduction/adduction strength, hip extension power, end-range control, and unilateral capacity. The goal is comprehensive, multi-vector hip strength delivered at a manageable spinal and soreness cost, so it complements the mat instead of taxing shots and scrambles.

The reductive "hip strength = back squat" model fails twice: it over-spends the spinal channel and it neglects the lateral and rotational vectors that decide guard retention and passing battles.

Operating Principles

- Train the hip in all its sport vectors: extension (thrust/hinge), flexion, abduction/adduction, rotation, and unilateral stability — not just the bilateral squat.
- Bias toward low-spinal, low-eccentric-soreness tools so hip work doesn't degrade shot/sprawl speed.
- Power expression (jumps, throws, sled) is valuable but CNS-costed; ration it.
- Match the vector emphasis to the athlete's game — a guard player and a wrestler weight the menu differently.

Programming Framework

Hip vector menu:

Vector  Lower-cost staples  Higher-cost / rationed
Extension  Hip thrust, back-extension, sled  Heavy RDL / deadlift (spinal)
Squat pattern  Belt squat, split squat, hack  Heavy back squat (spinal)
Abduction/adduction  Cable/band ab-/adduction, Copenhagen progressions (controlled)  Heavy adductor eccentrics (soreness)
Flexion/control  Loaded hip flexion, knee-drive, dead-bug  —
Power  Sled, low-volume jumps, KB swing  Max jumps / throws (CNS)

Vector emphasis by game:

- Guard player: weight abduction, hip flexion control, and unilateral end-range work.
- Wrestler: weight extension power and squat-pattern leg drive, while respecting the adductor-soreness rule before live.

If/Then Rules

- If shooting-heavy sparring within 48 h → adductor/hamstring Eccentric cost ≤1; keep hip power crisp and low-volume.
- If SD Amber/Red → squat/hinge slot to belt squat / hip thrust / split squat; no heavy back squat.
- If introducing Copenhagen/adductor work → start short-lever, controlled; soreness risk is high early.
- If CFS Saturated → cut jump/throw volume; keep extension strength via thrust/sled.
- If guard-retention is the priority quality → emphasize abduction, hip flexion control, and unilateral end-range work.
- If a heavy eccentric hip exercise falls before takedown sparring → move it after the session or substitute.

Generator-Use Rules

- Ensure weekly coverage across extension, squat, abduction/adduction, and unilateral vectors.
- Gate heavy squat/hinge by SD tier; default to belt squat / hip thrust when SD ≥ Amber.
- Cap adductor/hamstring eccentric cost ≤1 within 48 h of a shooting-heavy sparring flag; offer to reschedule heavy eccentrics to after the session.
- Ration jump/throw volume by CFS tier; cut entirely when Saturated.
- Bias the vector menu by a "game_style" input (guard vs wrestler). Performance/capacity language only.

Article 59 — Isometric Strength for Grappling: Pins, Frames, Holds, and Scrambles

Coaching Problem

Grappling is full of positions where the athlete must produce force without movement — holding a pin, maintaining a frame under pressure, gripping in a stalemate, surviving a scramble in an awkward joint angle. Dynamic-only training leaves these specific demands under-developed. Isometrics directly build position-specific strength and joint-angle robustness, and crucially they do it at very low spinal and soreness cost, making them ideal fillers in over-budget weeks.

Isometrics are the most under-used precision tool in combat strength work. Because they let a coach apply load to an exact joint angle with almost no eccentric component, they are uniquely suited to a population that is constantly spinal- and soreness-limited.

Operating Principles

- Two isometric flavors with different jobs:
  - Yielding isometrics (resisting a load, holding a position) → build position-holding endurance and tissue tolerance at length; low CNS cost.
  - Overcoming isometrics (pushing/pulling against immovable resistance) → high force expression and joint-angle strength; CNS-costed (covered in Article 60).
- Isometrics are angle-specific — train the angles the sport demands (framing, posting, pinning, gripping). Strength carries roughly ±15° around the trained angle.
- They are the best low-cost tool for maintaining strength when SD/CFS are high.

Programming Framework

Position-matched isometric menu:

Grappling demand  Isometric tool  Type  Cost profile
Pin / top pressure  Loaded position holds, prone press holds  Yielding  Low
Framing / posting  Bottoms-up holds, wall/rack press holds  Yielding  Low–moderate
Grip stalemate  Bar/towel hangs, plate pinches, gi grip holds  Yielding  High grip cost
Scramble joint angles  Split-stance holds, mid-range squat holds  Yielding  Low spinal
Force production  Overcoming pushes/pulls vs pins (see Article 60)  Overcoming  CNS

Dosing: yielding holds 20–45 s, 2–4 sets; grip holds spaced away from gi sparring; overcoming work brief and crisp (Article 60).

If/Then Rules

- If SD/CFS are in Amber/Red → yielding isometrics become primary gym content (low cost, maintains qualities).
- If gi sparring within 24 h → grip isometric cost ≤1.
- If building position-specific strength (e.g., framing) → pick the exact joint angle the position uses.
- If the athlete is fresh and a strength stimulus is wanted in a tight week → short yielding holds give stimulus with minimal cost.
- If overcoming isometrics are programmed → treat as a High/Medium CNS element, not a filler (Article 60).

Generator-Use Rules

- Tag isometrics as yielding (low cost — usable in Red weeks) vs overcoming (CNS-costed).
- Default Low/restorative days to yielding position holds when SD/CFS are high.
- Gate grip isometric cost by proximity to gi sparring (≤1 within 24 h).
- Pull yielding-isometric angles from a "problem_positions" input where available.
- Capacity language only; never claim isometrics prevent injury.

Article 60 — Using Overcoming Isometrics for Combat Athletes

Coaching Problem

Overcoming isometrics — maximal or near-maximal pushes/pulls against an immovable resistance (pins in a rack, against a wall, against a loaded bar that won't move) — are one of the most cost-efficient ways to express and build high force in combat athletes. They produce a strong CNS/strength stimulus with near-zero spinal compression at the chosen setup, no eccentric loading, and almost no soreness, which makes them uniquely valuable when the spinal and peripheral channels are over-budget but the athlete can still tolerate CNS output.

The single most useful thing to understand about overcoming isometrics is where their cost lands. They concentrate stress almost entirely in the CNS channel while sparing the spinal, eccentric, and soreness channels — the exact opposite of the deadlift. That makes them the precision instrument for the combat athlete's most common bind: a body deep in Spinal Debt that still has CNS headroom and wants to keep expressing high force.

Operating Principles

- Overcoming isometrics are a high-intent, CNS-costed tool — program them like heavy lifting, not like filler.
- They are angle-specific: train 1–3 joint angles per pattern per session; strength carries roughly ±15° around the trained angle.
- They allow maximal force with minimal joint travel and minimal soreness — ideal when you want a strength stimulus without adding spinal or eccentric cost.
- Set up to minimize spinal cost: pin heights and stances that keep the spine neutral and unloaded are the whole point.

Stress-Cost Implications

By the Module 0.5 dimensions, a well-set overcoming isometric typically scores: Spinal ≈0–1, Eccentric 0, Soreness 0–1, but CNS 2–3. That profile is the entire reason to use them — they concentrate cost in the one channel (CNS) that may still have room when Spinal is in debt.

The flagship use case: overcoming isometrics convert spare CNS capacity into a strength stimulus when the spine cannot pay for traditional lifting.

Programming Framework

Prescription guidelines:

- Effort: ramp to approximately 90–100% perceived effort over the set; early sets sub-maximal to groove the position.
- Duration: 3–6 s per maximal push (true overcoming intent), 3–5 hard efforts per angle.
- Angles: 1–2 angles per pattern per session, chosen for sport relevance.
- Frequency: 1–2×/week as a CNS-costed element; not daily.

Sport-matched overcoming menu:

Pattern  Setup  Sport relevance
Press  Pins in rack at framing / lockout angle  Framing, posting, stiff-arm
Pull  Bar/handle pinned, pull into immovable  Grip fights, breaking posture, pulling guard
Hinge/extension  Pins low, push into bar (low-spinal setup)  Bridging, hip-extension power
Squat / leg drive  Pins mid-range, drive up  Scramble leg drive, stand-ups

If/Then Rules

- If SD is Amber/Red but CFS ≤14 → overcoming isometrics are an ideal strength stimulus (low spinal, real strength). Promote them as the High-day tool.
- If CFS Saturated/Overdrawn → treat them as a max-effort element and pull them, like any heavy work.
- If hard sparring within 24 h → no maximal overcoming work (CNS interference).
- If building a specific position's strength → set the pin at that exact joint angle.
- If the athlete reports sensitivity at a joint angle → do not load maximally into that angle; choose a comfortable angle (conservative, non-diagnostic).
- If a weight cut is active → reduce to sub-maximal; maximal CNS efforts are poorly tolerated depleted.

Generator-Use Rules

- Classify overcoming isometrics as a CNS-costed (CFS-gated) element; never schedule them the day before hard sparring.
- Promote them automatically as the preferred strength stimulus when SD is Amber/Red but CFS ≤14.
- Enforce brief maximal-intent prescriptions (3–6 s, 3–5 efforts), not long holds; reject long-duration prescriptions as mislabeled yielding work.
- Pull target angles from a "problempositions" or "gamestyle" input where available.
- Use capacity / force-production language; no injury-prevention, no guarantees, no medical claims.

1. Score the week before the gym. Always estimate mat Spinal Debt and CNS load first; the gym fills the remainder.

Cluster G — Volume Thresholds and the Adaptation-Scoring Layer

Knowledge vault module for the Raz Pilosof strength program generator. Audience: advanced coaches and athletes — calisthenics/skill athletes, strength and powerlifting-adjacent lifters, hypertrophy-focused trainees, and combat-sport athletes who lift. Scope: Articles 61–68.

Relationship to Cluster F. Cluster F answered "what is this session costing across recovery channels, given what the sport already took?" Cluster G answers the adjacent question: "how much of each adaptation is worth doing in the first place, and where is the ceiling?" The two layers compose: volume thresholds set the target dose; the stress-cost layer decides whether the week can afford it and how to place it. The generator runs volume thresholds first (what to aim for), then filters through the stress-cost layer (what the week allows). Stress-cost scoring stays internal — it does not appear in client-facing programs unless the user asks.

---
Module 0 — The Volume-Threshold and Adaptation-Scoring Layer (Shared Engine)

The core idea: adaptation is dose-dependent and bounded on both ends. Too little volume and the stimulus is below threshold — nothing changes. Too much and the dose exceeds what the athlete can recover from and consolidate — quality erodes, fatigue masks fitness, and progress reverses. Between those bounds is a moving window that depends on the adaptation targeted, the athlete's training age, what else is being trained concurrently, and what the sport already spends. The job is to find the smallest dose that drives the desired adaptation, then add only as much as the athlete can recover from and still improve.

0.1 The Volume Landmarks

We use five landmarks. They apply, with different units, to every adaptation in the cluster (skill, strength, hypertrophy, endurance/density, GPP, tissue tolerance). The unit changes; the logic does not.

Landmark  Name  Definition
MEV  Minimum Effective Volume  Smallest weekly dose that produces measurable progress in the target adaptation
MAD  Minimum Adaptive Dose  The per-exposure threshold — smallest single-session dose that counts as a real stimulus
MAV  Maximum Adaptive Volume  The dose that produces the most adaptation per unit of fatigue — the efficient sweet spot
MRV  Maximum Recoverable Volume  The most the athlete can recover from across a microcycle and still adapt
AR  Adaptive Reserve  The unused gap between current working volume and MRV

MEV ≤ MAV ≤ MRV always. The window between MEV and MRV narrows the more advanced the athlete, the more concurrent the training, and the more the sport already loads the same channel. For a combat athlete in camp, MEV and MRV can sit almost on top of each other — meaning maintenance is the ceiling that week.

0.2 Why Thresholds Are Individual and Autoregulated

These landmarks are estimates that move week to week. They shift with sleep, nutrition, life stress, training age, exercise selection, proximity to failure, and concurrent sport load. The generator must:

- Treat any printed number as a starting anchor, not a prescription.
- Adjust off performance and recovery feedback (bar speed, rep quality, soreness, skill freshness, sport sharpness), not off the calendar alone.
- Never present a threshold as a guarantee — present it as an expected range under stated conditions.

0.3 The Adaptation Threshold Table (master reference)

Typical starting ranges for an advanced athlete, per adaptation, per muscle/quality/region, per week, assuming this quality is a genuine priority and not merely maintained. Ranges are anchors to autoregulate from, not universals.

Adaptation  Unit  MEV  MAV (sweet spot)  MRV (ceiling)  Warning signs of exceeding capacity
Skill acquisition (planche, HSPU, OAP)  quality exposures/week  2 exposures  3–5 exposures  5–6 exposures  form decays within a session; failed attempts rise
Maximal strength  hard sets/week per main lift  4–6  8–12  12–16  bar speed drops across weeks; top-set grind with no PR
Hypertrophy  hard sets/week per muscle/region  8–10  12–18  18–22 (region-dependent)  pump/connection fades; soreness lingers >72 h
Endurance / density (pull-up family)  total submax reps/week relative to max  ~150–200% of max-rep set  ~250–400%  ~450–550%  rep quality/ROM degrades; elbow/forearm nags
GPP / conditioning  hard work-intervals or minutes/week  1–2 sessions  2–3 sessions  3–4 sessions  flat sessions; resting HR creep; appetite/sleep disruption
Tissue tolerance / prehab  loaded exposures/week to a region  2 exposures  3–4 exposures  4–5 exposures  target region stays irritated rather than settling between exposures

Combat-athlete correction: When the sport already loads a quality (grip, posterior chain, conditioning), the sport counts toward the threshold. The gym fills the remainder between sport-supplied volume and MAV — and never pushes the combined total past MRV.

0.4 Effective Volume Units (EVU)

Not all sets are equal stimulus. The EVU normalizes a raw set into an effective-stimulus value.

EVU(set) = Proximity × Loadzone × ROMquality × (1 − Junk_penalty)

  Proximity   = 0.5 (RIR ≥4),  0.8 (RIR 3),  1.0 (RIR 1–2),  1.1 (RIR 0, true failure)
  Load_zone   = 1.0 in the adaptation's effective zone; 0.6 if clearly off-zone
  ROM_quality = 1.0 full quality ROM; 0.7 partial/cut ROM (unless partials are the intent)
  Junk_penalty= 0.2 if the set is fatigue-only (no progression, no quality)


Weekly Effective Volume = Σ EVU across qualifying sets. Compare this — not raw set count — to the MEV/MAV/MRV anchors.

0.5 Volume Cost Score (VCS)

EVU measures stimulus; VCS measures the recovery price of that stimulus.

VCS(block) = Σ [ EVUi × Failuremulti × Eccentricmulti × Noveltymulti ] × Channelweight

  Failure_mult   = 1.0 (RIR ≥2), 1.3 (RIR 1), 1.6 (RIR 0)
  Eccentric_mult = 1.0 normal, 1.4 slow/loaded eccentric, 1.6 supramaximal eccentric
  Novelty_mult   = 1.0 familiar, 1.3 new exercise/first exposure in a block
  Channel_weight = 1.0 local hypertrophy; 1.3 spinal/axial; 1.4 CNS-heavy; 1.2 connective-heavy


VCS is the explicit handshake with Cluster F: it converts planned volume into the same "cost" language the stress-cost layer uses.

0.6 Skill Quality Score (SQS)

Skill volume must be counted in quality reps/holds, not attempts. SQS decides when a skill exposure should stop today and whether weekly skill volume is real or junk.

SQS(exposure) = Qualityreps / (Qualityreps + Failedordegraded_reps)

  A "quality" rep/hold = full position, full intended duration, clean form, no grinding.
  A "failed/degraded" rep = missed, collapsed, broke position early, or visibly fought.

Stop rule: end the skill exposure when SQS drops below ~0.7, OR at the first
           true form failure on a high-skill static (planche, OAP).


Only quality reps count toward weekly skill MEV/MAV. Failed attempts count toward CNS/connective cost (VCS) but contribute zero stimulus and actively teach the wrong motor pattern.

0.7 Local Tissue Overlap Score (LTOS)

The most common volume-audit failure is counting exercises, not the regions they load. LTOS sums the real weekly load on a region across all sources (multiple exercises + sport).

LTOS(region) = Σ [ EVUexercise × Regionshareexercise ] + Sportregion_load

  Region_share = fraction of an exercise's effective volume that lands on the region
  Sportregionload = region's weekly load from the sport

Flag when LTOS(region) approaches that region's hypertrophy/tolerance MRV from 0.3.


LTOS is why a program with weighted pull-ups, barbell rows, curls, and gi sparring can overload elbow-flexors and grip even though no single line item looks high.

0.8 Adaptive Reserve (AR) Estimate

AR is the practical decision variable: it estimates how much headroom remains before MRV on the channel that is closest to its ceiling.

AR(channel) = MRV(channel) − [ current gym volume on channel + sport-supplied volume on channel ]

Express as a tier:
  AR ≥ 30% of MRV   → Green:  room to add stimulus / progress this quality
  AR 10–30%         → Amber:  hold volume; progress via intensity or quality, not added sets
  AR 0–10%          → Red:    at ceiling; maintain only; any add risks overshoot
  AR < 0            → Black:  already over MRV; cut volume now


Governing-channel rule: Plan to the channel with the lowest AR, not the average.

0.9 How the Layer Drives Programming

1. Pick the adaptation and its unit. Strength = hard sets; skill = quality exposures/reps; density = submax reps vs max; etc.
2. Read the landmarks (0.3) as starting anchors, then adjust for training age and concurrency.
3. Count stimulus in EVU (0.4), not raw sets, and count skill in SQS quality reps (0.6).
4. Add sport-supplied volume to the ledger and compute LTOS per shared region (0.7).
5. Compute AR per channel (0.8); plan to the lowest-AR channel.
6. Price the plan in VCS (0.5) and check it against the week's stress-cost budget (Cluster F). If over budget, cut volume or substitute.
7. Autoregulate: if quality (bar speed, SQS, ROM, sport sharpness) degrades, you have found MRV early — back off regardless of the printed number.
8. Deload triggers: AR < 0 on any priority channel for the week, OR quality down 2+ sessions, OR two channels simultaneously Red, OR a constraint flag escalates.

Universal volume-day language used across the cluster:

- Progression dose: working volume at/above MEV with AR Green — the dose that drives the adaptation.
- Maintenance dose: roughly MEV (often one-third to one-half of MAV) — holds the quality with minimal cost.
- Overreach dose: brief planned excursion toward/above MRV for 1–2 weeks, only when followed by a deload and only on a non-sport-priority channel.

Article 61 — Volume Landmarks for Advanced Athletes: MEV, MAV, MRV, and Adaptive Reserve

Coaching Problem

Advanced athletes have already harvested the easy adaptations. The lever that worked in their first three years — "add volume, get bigger and stronger" — now produces fatigue faster than fitness. The recurring mistake is treating volume as a dial that only turns up. The advanced reality is that the productive window between too little and too much has narrowed to a band that must be found, defended, and re-found every block.

The deeper trap is conflating the two failure modes. Stalling because you are below MEV looks almost identical, day to day, to stalling because you are above MRV: flat performance, no PRs, frustration. The fix is opposite in each case — add in the first, cut in the second — so guessing wrong doubles the damage. Landmarks exist precisely to tell these two states apart by reading quality and recovery, not just outcomes.

Operating Principles

- The five landmarks (Module 0.1) are the planning language for every adaptation. MEV is the floor, MAV the efficient target, MRV the ceiling, MAD the per-session minimum, AR the room left.
- Count EVU, not sets (0.4). Advanced programs are full of nominal volume that doesn't earn its keep — RIR-4 "working" sets, cut-ROM reps, junk warm-ups logged as sets.
- The window narrows with training age and concurrency. A novice's MEV–MRV band is wide and forgiving; an advanced concurrent athlete's is tight and unforgiving.
- Plan to the lowest-AR channel (0.8), not the average. A program is only as progressable as its most-constrained region or system.
- Maintenance is a legitimate, valuable dose. Most qualities hold for 2–3 weeks at roughly MEV, which is what lets a program prioritize one thing without losing the others.

Threshold Table by Adaptation

Adaptation  Unit  MEV  MAV  MRV  Warning signs
Skill (high-static)  quality exposures/wk  2  3–5  5–6  form decays in-session, failed attempts climb
Max strength  hard sets/wk per lift  4–6  8–12  12–16  bar speed falls week-over-week, no PR
Hypertrophy  hard sets/wk per muscle  8–10  12–18  18–22  connection fades, soreness >72 h
Endurance/density  submax reps/wk vs max  150–200%  250–400%  450–550%  ROM degrades, joints nag
GPP/conditioning  hard sessions/wk  1–2  2–3  3–4  flat sessions, HR/sleep creep
Tissue tolerance  loaded exposures/wk  2  3–4  4–5  region stays irritated between exposures

If/Then Rules

- If a quality has stalled 2–3 weeks and EVU sits below MAV with quality intact → you are below productive volume; add EVU.
- If a quality has stalled and quality (bar speed, ROM, SQS) is degrading → you are at/over MRV; cut, don't add.
- If AR is Amber on the priority channel → progress via load/quality, not added sets.
- If AR is Red/Black on any priority channel → maintenance dose only; plan a deload.
- If the athlete is concurrent (sport + lifting) → subtract sport-supplied volume from the channel's MRV before computing gym room.
- If logged volume is high but progress is flat → recompute in EVU before changing anything; nominal volume lies.

Generator-Use Rules

- Store landmarks per adaptation, per region/lift/quality, in the unit from 0.3 — never one global volume number.
- Compute working volume in EVU, not raw sets, before comparing to any landmark.
- Subtract sport-supplied volume from MRV for concurrent athletes; compute LTOS for shared regions.
- Plan to the lowest-AR channel; gate "add volume" suggestions behind AR Green.
- Surface landmarks as ranges-under-conditions, never as guarantees.
- Use capacity/recovery/quality language only; no injury-prevention, no medical claims, no guarantees.

Article 62 — Skill Acquisition Volume: How Much Practice Builds Planche, HSPU, OAP, and Gymnastics Strength

Coaching Problem

High-skill statics — planche, freestanding handstand push-up, one-arm pull-up, front lever progressions — fail under the volume logic that works for hypertrophy. The athlete who "trains planche to failure five days a week" is not accumulating skill; they are accumulating failed reps, which teach the nervous system the wrong pattern and load connective tissue that recovers slowly. The mistake is counting attempts as volume. The correct unit is quality reps/holds (Module 0.6), and the ceiling is set by coordination freshness and connective recovery — both low.

Operating Principles

- Quality reps are the only reps that count (0.6). Stop the exposure when SQS drops below approximately 0.7 or at the first true form failure on a high static.
- Frequency beats density for motor learning. 3–4 short, fresh exposures/week build a skill faster than 2 long, fatiguing ones.
- Failed attempts are pure cost, zero stimulus — they add to VCS (CNS/connective) and teach error. Minimize them.
- The connective tissue is the slow channel. Wrists, elbows, biceps tendon (OAP), and anterior shoulder (planche) set the real ceiling, not the muscles.
- Deload skill on a separate clock from strength — coordination and connective tissue accumulate fatigue you won't feel as soreness.

Skill Volume Thresholds

Skill  Frequency (exposures/wk)  Quality reps or holds per exposure  Failed-attempt cap  Deload rule
Planche (straddle→full)  3–5  4–6 quality holds  ≤1–2 per exposure  every 4–6 wk drop to MEV (2 exposures)
HSPU (freestanding/deficit)  2–4  5–10 quality reps total  ≤2  deload when balance/ROM decays
One-arm pull-up (OAP)  2–3  3–6 quality reps/side  ≤1 grind/side  every 3–4 wk; elbow is the governor
Front/back lever  3–4  4–8 quality holds  ≤2  every 4–6 wk to MEV
Press-to-handstand  3–5  5–8 quality reps  ≤2  balance-decay triggered

MEV is the floor (≈2 exposures), MAV the productive target (3–5), MRV the ceiling (5–6). Beyond MRV, quality reps fall and failed reps rise — the SQS itself signals the overshoot.

Formulas in Action — SQS Gating a Planche Session

An athlete plans 8 planche holds. Holds 1–5 are clean (quality), hold 6 breaks early, hold 7 is fought and shaky, hold 8 collapses.

- Quality reps = 5; degraded/failed = 3 → SQS = 5 / (5+3) = 0.625.
- SQS dropped below 0.7 after rep 5 → the stop rule says the productive exposure ended at rep 5.
- Reps 6–8 added CNS/connective cost (VCS) with zero skill stimulus and rehearsed failure.

If/Then Rules

- If SQS < 0.7 in a session → end the skill exposure; log only quality reps.
- If failed attempts exceed the cap two sessions running → reduce difficulty or frequency; the dose is past MRV.
- If elbow/wrist/anterior-shoulder reports lingering irritation between exposures → reduce loaded exposures to that region (LTOS), choose a lower-leverage progression (conservative, non-diagnostic).
- If the athlete also trains heavy pulling/pressing → count that region's load toward the skill's connective LTOS before adding skill frequency.
- If a hard sport/CNS week is scheduled → drop skill to MEV (2 exposures); skill quality needs CNS freshness.

Generator-Use Rules

- Count skill volume in quality reps/holds via SQS, never attempts; enforce the SQS < 0.7 stop rule.
- Hold skill frequency to the anchors (MEV 2, MAV 3–5, MRV 5–6 exposures) and cap failed attempts.
- Maintain a separate connective-tissue tolerance ledger (LTOS) for wrist/elbow/biceps/anterior shoulder; gate added skill frequency behind it.
- Deduct skill exposures to MEV automatically on flagged hard-CNS or hard-sport weeks.
- Use capacity/quality/coordination language; no injury-prevention claims, no guarantees, no diagnoses.

Article 63 — Strength Volume Thresholds: Heavy Sets, Top Sets, Back-Offs, and Neural Readiness

Coaching Problem

Maximal strength is the adaptation most easily over-dosed because it feels productive while it fails. Heavy work is satisfying, and the advanced athlete who loves it will keep adding top sets, singles, and "just one more triple" until bar speed quietly collapses and the lift stalls. The mistake is treating strength like hypertrophy — more hard sets, more better. Strength's productive volume band is narrow (approximately 4–12 hard sets/week per lift) and its ceiling is set by CNS recovery and neural readiness, not by how much the muscle can tolerate. Past MRV, the athlete is training fatigue management, not strength.

Operating Principles

- Strength MRV is CNS-gated. Neural readiness — bar speed at a fixed load, readiness to express force — is the limiter and the early-warning signal.
- Top sets are the stimulus; back-offs are the practice; speed work is the cheap deposit. Dose each separately.
- Effective set in the strength zone = approximately 80%+ and RIR 0–2 (0.4). A 70% "strength" set is off-zone.
- Max-effort and repeated-effort are different volume currencies. ME (heavy singles/doubles) buys neural drive at high VCS; RE (back-offs at RIR 1–2) buys technique and some hypertrophy at lower CNS cost.
- For concurrent/sport athletes, strength volume is the first thing the sport's CNS draw eats into.

Strength Volume Thresholds

Element  Unit  MEV  MAV  MRV  Warning signs
Top sets  hard sets/wk  1–2  2–4  4–5  grind with no PR, bar speed down
Back-off sets  hard sets/wk  2–3  4–8  8–10  form drifts late in sets
Heavy singles  reps/wk  1–2  3–6  6–8  misses, slow lockout
Heavy triples  sets/wk  1–2  2–4  4–5  rep 3 grinds, speed falls
Speed/dynamic  sets/wk  3–4  6–10  10–12  bar speed not actually fast

High-Concurrent Sport Modification

When the sport supplies heavy CNS load (hard sparring, live wrestling — Cluster F), the strength CNS budget shrinks. Drop ME first; keep a small top-set "primer" (1–2 crisp reps, RIR 1) to maintain neural drive, and move accumulation to RE at submax. Strength is retained 2–3 weeks at maintenance — there is no urgency to push it during a hard sport week.

If/Then Rules

- If bar speed at a fixed load falls 2+ sessions → over MRV on CNS; cut heavy exposures, not back-offs first.
- If top sets grind with no PR but speed work is crisp → too much ME volume, not too little; reduce ME, keep speed.
- If AR Green on CNS and quality intact → add a back-off set or raise top-set load, not both.
- If the athlete trains a sport with high CNS draw → 1 heavy exposure/lift/week max; bias to RE and speed.
- If a weight cut is active → reduce ME; near-maximal work is poorly tolerated depleted.
- If two main lifts both want a heavy day → they share the CNS MRV; alternate which lift gets the true ME exposure.

Generator-Use Rules

- Separate strength volume into top sets / back-offs / singles / triples / speed, each with its own anchor; never collapse to one set count.
- Cap ME exposures at 1/lift/week for advanced athletes; auto-reduce on flagged hard-CNS or sport weeks.
- Gate strength CNS volume on a shared CNS budget across lifts + sport (Cluster F handshake via VCS).
- Trigger a cut when neural-readiness (bar-speed/RPE-at-load) degrades 2+ sessions, regardless of the printed set count.
- Use force-production/readiness language; no medical claims, no injury-prevention, no guarantees.

Article 64 — Hypertrophy Volume Thresholds: Effective Sets, Local Recovery, and Interference Control

Coaching Problem

Hypertrophy is the adaptation with the widest volume band — it rewards and tolerates more sets than strength or skill — which is exactly why it gets mismanaged. Coaches either under-stimulate by counting RIR-4 cut-ROM sets as if they were hard sets, or over-stimulate a region by stacking exercises that all hit the same muscle and blowing past its local MRV while each lift looks reasonable on paper.

For combat athletes and other concurrent trainees there is a second layer: the sport already loads several muscles hard (forearms/grip, posterior chain, neck), so naive added hypertrophy volume to those regions stacks on top of sport volume and overshoots local MRV invisibly. The Local Tissue Overlap Score (0.7) exists precisely to make this stacking visible before it becomes a stall or a lingering soreness problem.

Operating Principles

- Count effective sets per muscle, in EVU, not nominal sets (0.4). RIR 4 and cut ROM are heavily discounted.
- MRV is local and region-specific (approximately 18–22 effective sets/wk, region-dependent). Fast-recovering muscles (delts, lats, calves, forearms-as-trained) tolerate more frequency; slow-recovering, eccentric-loaded, or large muscles (hamstrings, lower back, large multi-joint movers) recover slower and cap lower.
- Eccentric load is the soreness/recovery multiplier (VCS 1.4–1.6). A few slow-eccentric sets can cost more local recovery than many concentric-biased sets.
- Sport-supplied volume counts toward the muscle's MRV. Add it via LTOS before prescribing gym volume to that region.
- Interference is bidirectional: soreness from hypertrophy work degrades skill/sport quality, and sport soreness shrinks the recoverable hypertrophy dose.

Hypertrophy Volume Thresholds by Region Group

Region group  Recovery speed  MEV  MAV  MRV  Notes for combat/concurrent athletes
Lats / upper back  fast  8–10  14–18  20–22  safe volume sink; minimal sport overlap
Delts (all heads)  fast  6–8  12–16  18–20  tolerates frequency; watch posting overlap in grapplers
Chest  moderate  8–10  12–16  16–18  pressing overlaps posting volume
Quads  moderate  8–10  12–16  16–18  heavy eccentrics sore shots/sprawls 48 h
Hamstrings  slow  6–8  10–14  14–16  eccentric work lingers; respect before live
Lower back/erectors  slow  4–6  8–10  10–12  most over-drafted by sport; often zero gym room in camp
Elbow flexors (biceps)  moderate  6–8  12–16  16–18  high gi-grip overlap → LTOS critical
Forearms/grip  slow (cumulative)  4–6  8–12  12–14  sport supplies most; gym often only fills the gap

Adjusting Hypertrophy Volume for Combat Athletes

- Subtract sport load first (LTOS). Forearms/grip, posterior chain, and neck often need little or no added gym hypertrophy in-season — the sport fills MEV already.
- Bias gym volume to low-overlap regions (lats, delts, chest) where AR is largest.
- Cap eccentric cost near hard sport sessions — eccentric soreness in quads/hams degrades shots and scrambles for 48 h.
- Use maintenance dosing in camp. Most muscle is retained 2–3 weeks at approximately MEV, so hypertrophy yields to sport without loss.

Generator-Use Rules

- Count hypertrophy volume in effective sets per muscle (EVU); never per exercise or nominal set.
- Compute LTOS per region including sport load; gate added volume behind the region's AR.
- Apply region-specific MRV (fast vs slow recoverers) and an eccentric VCS multiplier near sport days.
- Default to maintenance dosing for sport-overlapping regions in-season; bias progression to low-overlap regions.
- Use capacity/recovery/soreness-management language; no medical claims, no "prevents injury," no guarantees.

Article 65 — Pull-Up Endurance and Density Volume: Ladders, EMOMs, Clusters, and High-Rep Progression

Coaching Problem

Endurance and density work — high-rep pull-ups, ladders, EMOMs, cluster sets — is where volume math goes wrong most often, because the numbers get big fast and the connective tissue pays the bill slowly. The mistake is dosing density off enthusiasm ("100 pull-ups today") instead of off the athlete's max-rep capacity, which is the correct denominator. The right unit is total submax reps/week expressed as a percentage of a max-rep set (Module 0.3), and the limiter is local/connective recovery (elbow, forearm, biceps tendon) plus rep quality, not the lungs.

Operating Principles

- Dose density off max-rep capacity, not off a round number. Weekly submax volume is a multiple of the athlete's clean max set (0.3).
- Stay submax per set. Most density reps live at 40–70% of max-rep capacity, stopping ≥2–3 reps shy of failure.
- Connective tissue is the ceiling. Elbows, forearm flexors, and biceps tendon set MRV here, and they recover slowly and cumulatively.
- Quality reps only (EVU ROM_quality). Stop a density set when ROM or tempo visibly degrades.
- Density is a low-CNS, moderate-local cost (VCS). It's a good volume sink when CNS/spinal channels are taxed — but it competes for the same elbow/grip tissue as pulling strength and gi sport.

Density Methods and Volume Profiles

Method  Per-set load vs max  Typical weekly dose  Best use
Straight sets  35–50% of max set  200–400% of max  base endurance, pain-free high-rep capacity
Ladders  ramps to ~60%  250–450% of max  volume accumulation with built-in submax cap
EMOMs  30–50% of max, lots of sets  250–400% of max  density/work-capacity, easy autoregulation
Cluster sets  50–70% of max  200–350% of max  quality reps at higher per-rep load without failure

All weekly doses are starting anchors relative to a clean max-rep set. MEV ≈ 150–200% of max; MAV ≈ 250–400%; MRV ≈ 450–550%.

Scaling Rule

Express every density prescription as a percentage of the athlete's current clean max set, not as an absolute. A fixed "4×12" is light for a 30-rep athlete and brutal for a 12-rep athlete. Re-anchor whenever the max set changes by more than approximately 15%.

A 28–30 rep athlete using 4×10–12 at roughly 33–40% of max set, pain-free, is a moderate density dose — not a hard one. This is appropriate and unremarkable for this athlete in a MAV band.

If/Then Rules

- If the athlete's max set is high and elbows/forearms are pain-free → moderate fixed schemes like 4×10–12 are an appropriate submax density dose; progress reps or sets within the MAV band.
- If ROM or tempo degrades within a set → stop the set; only full-ROM reps count (EVU).
- If elbow/forearm/biceps-tendon irritation lingers between sessions → reduce density volume or shift to a lower-overlap pattern (conservative, non-diagnostic).
- If the athlete also does heavy weighted pulling or gi sport → run LTOS on elbow flexors/grip before adding density.
- If density times/rep counts slow week over week → at/over MRV on the connective channel; deload the density block.
- If CNS/spinal channels are taxed by sport → density is a good low-CNS volume sink, provided the local channel has reserve.

Generator-Use Rules

- Prescribe density as a percentage of the athlete's current max-rep set, mapped to MEV/MAV/MRV multiples; re-anchor when the max set shifts >15%.
- Count quality full-ROM reps only (EVU ROM_quality); auto-stop sets on ROM degradation.
- Run LTOS on elbow flexors/grip across density + pulling strength + gi sport before adding density.
- Treat density as a low-CNS volume sink usable when CNS/spinal are taxed, if the local connective channel has reserve.
- Use capacity/tolerance/quality language; no injury-prevention, no medical claims, no guarantees.

Article 66 — Combat-Sport Volume Management: Strength Training Dose Around Sparring, Wrestling, and BJJ

Coaching Problem

For a combat athlete, the gym is not the main volume source — the mat is. The recurring failure is programming gym volume as if the athlete arrived fresh, when in reality hard sparring, live wrestling, and rolling have already spent most of the week's recoverable volume on the same channels the gym wants to load. The volume question for a fighter is never "how much should we lift?" — it is "what volume budget did the mat leave, and how do we fill the remainder without crossing MRV?"

The second problem is that mat volume is spiky and unpredictable: a hard sparring week, a competition camp, a deload week, and a technical week have radically different sport-supplied volumes. A fixed gym template ignores this and either overshoots MRV (hard weeks) or wastes reserve (light weeks). Gym volume must flex inversely to sport volume.

Weekly Sport-Context Volume Map

Sport week type  Sport-supplied volume  Gym strength dose  Gym hypertrophy dose  Governing channel to protect
Hard sparring week (2+ live)  very high CNS + spinal  maintenance (~MEV; 1 primer top set)  maintenance, low-overlap regions only  CNS + spinal
Competition camp (peak)  maximal, sport-specific  maintenance only; no ME  maintenance, low-eccentric  freshness/sharpness
Technical / skill week  low-moderate  progression allowed (MAV)  progression on low-overlap regions  none acute → use the reserve
Deload / recovery week  low  low (restorative) or 1 quality top set  low  systemic recovery

Hard / Medium / Low Day Placement Within a Week

- Low sport day → gym High day (the one real progression session, placed for maximum buffer before the next live session).
- Medium sport day → gym Medium day (RIR 1–3, no max intent).
- Hard sport day → gym Low/none (restorative, blood flow, low-overlap maintenance only; protect the mat).

If/Then Rules

- If 2+ hard live sessions this week → gym strength at maintenance (1 primer), hypertrophy on low-overlap regions only.
- If competition camp / peak week → no ME gym work; maintenance dose; protect sharpness.
- If technical/light sport week → use the reserve: run a gym progression dose (MAV).
- If combined sport+gym AR < 10% (Red) on any channel → cut gym volume; substitute, don't push.
- If gi grip/elbow LTOS is high → no added grip/elbow gym volume; the gi supplied it.
- If sparring sharpness drops 2 sessions → treat as MRV reached regardless of the printed gym numbers; deload.

Generator-Use Rules

- Require a sport-week-type input (hard spar / camp / technical / deload) and scale the gym volume fraction from it.
- Add sport-supplied volume to every shared-channel ledger before prescribing; compute combined AR and LTOS.
- Default in-season strength to maintenance + 1 primer, hypertrophy to low-overlap regions, unless the week is technical/light.
- Place the gym High day on the lowest sport day; never adjacent to hard sparring (Cluster F handshake).
- Use load-management/capacity language; no injury-prevention, no medical claims, no guarantees.

Article 67 — Volume Allocation in Concurrent Training: Balancing Strength, Skill, Hypertrophy, GPP, and Conditioning

Coaching Problem

The hardest volume problem is not setting one quality's threshold — it is dividing a finite, shared recovery budget across five qualities that all want volume at once: strength, skill, hypertrophy, GPP/conditioning, and sport. The recurring failure is additive planning — building each quality up to its own MAV in isolation, summing them, and discovering the total is far past the athlete's global MRV. Concurrent training requires subtractive planning: start from the global budget, fund the priority quality to MAV, then fund the rest only to MEV (maintenance) with whatever reserve remains.

Operating Principles

- One quality gets a progression dose at a time; the rest get maintenance. This is the core concurrent rule. You can hold 4 qualities at MEV while pushing 1 to MAV; you cannot push all 5.
- Allocate by channel, not just by total (governing-channel rule, 0.8). Plan to the lowest-AR channel; don't let two CNS qualities both run high.
- Maintenance is cheap and protective. Approximately MEV holds a quality for 2–3 weeks, which is what makes prioritization possible without loss.
- Interference is channel-specific. Heavy CNS conditioning blunts strength and skill; high local hypertrophy soreness blunts skill quality and sport. Separate colliding qualities in time.
- Re-rank priorities by block, not by week. Rotate which quality gets the progression dose across a training phase.

Concurrent Allocation Matrix

Priority this block  Strength  Skill  Hypertrophy  GPP/Cond.  Shared-channel watch
Strength focus  MAV  MEV (CNS-fresh, pre-strength)  MEV  MEV (low-CNS only)  CNS: skill + strength collide → skill before strength
Skill focus  MEV (1 primer)  MAV  MEV  MEV  CNS + connective: keep strength low so skill stays fresh
Hypertrophy focus  MEV (heavy primer)  MEV  MAV  MEV  local/connective: density + hypertrophy collide → LTOS
Conditioning/GPP focus  MEV  MEV  MEV  MAV  systemic: protect skill freshness; keep strength as a primer
In-season (sport priority)  MEV+primer  MEV  MEV low-overlap  sport supplies  every channel — plan to lowest AR

Interference Control

- CNS collisions (strength × skill × max conditioning): sequence skill first (fresh), strength second; keep conditioning low-CNS in a strength block.
- Local collisions (hypertrophy × density): apply LTOS (0.7); alternate which shares a region; don't run both to MAV on the same tissue.
- Systemic collisions (hard conditioning × everything): place hard conditioning on its own day or after the day's priority quality, never before skill/strength.

If/Then Rules

- If more than one quality is at a progression dose → reduce all but the priority to maintenance; the budget can't fund two.
- If two qualities share a channel (CNS: strength+skill; local: hypertrophy+density) → only one runs high; the other is MEV.
- If global AR (lowest channel) is Red → no quality progresses this week; hold and recover.
- If skill quality (SQS) drops during a strength or conditioning block → CNS collision; pull strength/conditioning, protect skill freshness.
- If conditioning is blunting strength → switch conditioning to low-CNS modalities or move it off strength days.
- If rotating into a new block → re-rank the priority quality and re-allocate from the channel budgets again.

Sequencing Within the Day

Skill → Strength → Hypertrophy → Conditioning. Hard conditioning off priority-quality days.

Generator-Use Rules

- Enforce one progression-dose quality per block; auto-set all others to maintenance (MEV).
- Allocate volume against per-channel budgets (CNS / local-connective / systemic), not a single total; plan to lowest AR.
- Block two CNS-max qualities (or two same-region qualities) at MAV simultaneously; force one to MEV.
- Sequence the day skill → strength → hypertrophy → conditioning; move hard conditioning off priority-quality days.
- Rotate the priority quality across blocks; re-allocate budgets each block.
- Use capacity/recovery language; no medical claims, no injury-prevention, no guarantees.

Article 68 — Using Volume Audits to Fix Overloaded Programs: From Exercise Lists to Recoverable Microcycles

Coaching Problem

Most "I'm overtrained / stalled" programs are not mysteries — they are un-audited exercise lists. The athlete (or a previous coach) added a good idea here and a good idea there until the week became a pile of exercises with no volume accounting. Each line looks justified; the aggregate is unrecoverable. The fix is a volume audit: a systematic pass that converts the exercise list into channel-and-region ledgers, finds where the program exceeds MRV, and prunes back to a recoverable microcycle. This article is the operational synthesis of the whole cluster.

Operating Principles

- Audit in EVU and per-region/channel, never in exercise count. The exercise list is the input; channel/region ledgers are the analysis.
- Find the lowest-AR channel and the highest-LTOS region first — that's where the overload lives.
- Cut redundancy before you cut priorities. Three exercises hitting one region at MRV is redundancy; the priority quality is not the thing to cut.
- Substitute and redistribute before deleting. Often the fix is moving volume to a higher-AR region/day, not removing it.
- Re-test the audited week against global MRV before shipping it.

The Volume-Audit Procedure (Step by Step)

Step  Action  Tool
1  List every gym exercise + sport session with sets, RIR, ROM, eccentric, novelty  raw input
2  Convert each set to EVU (discount RIR-high, cut-ROM, junk)  0.4
3  Map EVU onto muscles/regions via region-share; add sport region load  LTOS, 0.7
4  Map EVU onto channels (CNS, local/connective, spinal, systemic)  0.5/0.8
5  Compute LTOS per region and AR per channel; flag any near/over MRV  0.7, 0.8
6  Price the week in VCS; check against the stress-cost budget  0.5 + Cluster F
7  Prune: cut redundancy on over-MRV regions, redistribute to high-AR regions/days  allocation logic
8  Re-test: recompute AR/LTOS; confirm all priority channels ≤ MRV with reserve  0.8

The key insight from auditing: the problem is usually un-audited overlap on one channel while another sits idle. The audit finds it; the prune fixes it without touching the priorities.

If/Then Rules

- If an athlete reports stalled + a nagging region → audit that region's LTOS first; it's usually over MRV from stacked sources.
- If a region is over MRV → cut the lowest-priority direct contributor (often isolation work), keep the priority pattern.
- If a channel is over MRV but a region within it has reserve → redistribute, don't just delete.
- If the audited week still exceeds global MRV → cut a whole secondary quality to maintenance (Article 67), don't shave everything evenly.
- If sport supplies a region's MEV already → the gym adds little/nothing there; remove redundant gym volume to that region.
- If the athlete resists cutting → frame it as maintenance (qualities hold 2–3 weeks), and show the LTOS/AR numbers.

Generator-Use Rules

- Provide a volume-audit mode: ingest an exercise list + sport, output EVU, per-region LTOS, per-channel AR, and VCS, with over-MRV flags.
- Prune by redundancy and lowest priority on over-MRV channels/regions; redistribute to high-AR regions before deleting.
- Always re-test the pruned week against global MRV and the stress-cost budget before output.
- Frame cuts as maintenance, not loss; show the numbers that justify them.
- Keep all scoring internal in client-facing programs unless the user asks to see the audit; use capacity/recovery language, no medical claims, no guarantees.

Cluster G: Cross-Cluster Generator Rules Summary (Volume-Threshold Layer)

1. Pick the adaptation and use its unit. Strength = hard sets/lift; skill = quality exposures + reps (SQS); hypertrophy = effective sets/muscle; density = submax reps vs max set; GPP = sessions/week; tissue tolerance = loaded exposures/region. Never use one global volume number.
2. Count stimulus in EVU, not raw sets (0.4). Discount RIR-high, cut-ROM, and junk sets before comparing to any landmark. For skill, count only quality reps via SQS (0.6) and enforce the SQS < 0.7 stop rule.
3. Landmarks are starting anchors, not guarantees. MEV (floor), MAV (efficient target), MRV (ceiling), MAD (per-session minimum), AR (room left). They move with sleep, life stress, training age, and sport load — autoregulate off quality and recovery, never the calendar alone.
4. The combat correction is mandatory. Sport-supplied volume counts toward every shared-channel/region threshold. Add it before prescribing gym volume; the gym fills the remainder up to MAV and never past combined MRV.
5. Compute LTOS per region including sport (0.7). Catch hidden overlap before a region overshoots while each exercise looks fine.
6. Plan to the lowest-AR channel (0.8), not the average. The most-constrained channel/region governs; total volume never decides placement.
7. Price plans in VCS (0.5) and check against the Cluster F stress-cost budget. Run volume thresholds first (target dose), then filter through stress-cost (what the week affords).
8. One progression-dose quality per block; maintain the rest at MEV (Article 67). The shared budget cannot fund two qualities to MAV.
9. Strength MRV is CNS-gated; hypertrophy MRV is local; skill MRV is freshness/connective-gated; density MRV is connective-gated. Read the matching early-warning signal to find MRV before the stall.
10. Density is dosed off the athlete's max-rep capacity, not a round number. Express prescriptions as a percent of the current clean max set.
11. Volume audit before fixing overload (Article 68): exercise list → EVU → region/channel ledgers → AR/LTOS flags → prune redundancy on over-MRV channels → re-test.
12. Deload triggers: AR < 0 on any priority channel, OR quality down 2+ sessions, OR two channels simultaneously Red, OR a constraint flag escalates.
13. Scoring stays internal. VCS/EVU/LTOS/AR/SQS are coach-facing decision aids; they do not appear in client-facing programs unless the user asks.
14. Constraints are not diagnoses. A flagged sensitive region only reduces loaded exposures to that region and biases substitution. Never name conditions, never advise treatment.
15. Language discipline (hard rule): no citations, no external URLs, no medical claims, never "prevents injury," never guarantees outcomes. Use capacity, tolerance, recovery, reserve, quality, and load-management language only.

Article 69 — Nutrition Intake Triage for Strength and Combat Athletes

Purpose

Before the engine assigns any nutrition guidance, it must screen the athlete's current nutritional situation. The triage layer determines how much nutrition support to generate and when to flag the situation for professional referral.

69.1 The Nutrition Triage Inputs

The engine should collect or estimate the following before generating any nutrition output:

Body composition and weight trend: Current bodyweight and recent trend (stable, gaining, losing, cutting); whether they are in a planned gain, fat loss, recomposition, or competition-prep phase.

Training schedule: Sessions per week and approximate duration; whether sessions include strength, conditioning, BJJ/combat sports, calisthenics, or combinations; whether any same-day double sessions occur; whether the athlete trains fasted or in a fed state.

Nutrition basics: Approximate meals per day and rough meal timing; appetite level; food restrictions or preferences; GI tolerance around training.

Hydration and electrolytes: Whether the athlete actively hydrates or drinks when thirsty only; sweat rate self-assessment; history of cramping; heat exposure.

Supplement and stimulant use: Current supplements in use; caffeine use, timing, and approximate daily dose; whether the athlete is subject to anti-doping testing.

Deadlines and external pressures: Competition date or weigh-in deadline; whether a weight-class restriction exists.

69.2 Nutrition Complexity Scoring

Tier 1 — Low complexity (standard guidance appropriate): All of the following apply: stable bodyweight or gradual intentional change; three or more meals per day with reasonable protein distribution; no active weight cut within four weeks; no significant GI issues; normal appetite; adequate hydration awareness; no competition deadline within six weeks.

Engine output: standard nutrition support notes, general hydration guidance, optional supplement basics.

Tier 2 — Moderate complexity (closer monitoring, more detailed guidance): One or more of the following apply: actively losing or gaining weight with performance or recovery affected; training volume high enough that glycogen depletion is plausible; two or fewer meals per day or consistently low appetite; mild GI issues around training; approaching a competition or weigh-in within six to twelve weeks; significant caffeine dependence or erratic use.

Engine output: detailed nutrition notes, timed fueling guidance, hydration decision rules, supplement considerations, weight-class caution flags if relevant.

Tier 3 — High complexity (refer-out triggers present): One or more of the following apply: rapid unexplained weight loss or gain; signs consistent with disordered eating; severe or persistent GI distress; fainting or near-fainting episodes; persistent fatigue that does not resolve with rest or basic nutrition corrections; severe dehydration presentation; suspected supplement or drug interaction; athletes with diabetes, kidney disease, cardiac conditions, eating disorder history, or other conditions requiring medical or dietitian-level management.

Engine output: flag for professional referral, do not generate aggressive nutrition protocols, reduce training volume recommendations until situation is assessed, include a note to connect the athlete with a qualified sports dietitian or physician.

69.3 Refer-Out Triggers — Nutrition

The following situations always require professional referral regardless of athlete preference:

- Signs of disordered eating behavior
- Rapid unexplained weight change
- Persistent fainting or syncope
- Severe GI bleeding, vomiting, or malabsorption history
- Diabetes requiring medication management
- Kidney disease or a history of kidney stones
- Eating disorder diagnosis or suspected active eating disorder
- Extreme supplement stacking including hormonal products, thyroid agents, diuretics, or stimulants beyond caffeine
- Any athlete subject to WADA or USADA anti-doping testing who is asking about aggressive supplement protocols

Article 70 — Calorie and Macro Targets for Performance, Body Composition, and Recovery

70.1 Calorie Target Decision Rules

For most strength, hypertrophy, and combat athletes with moderate to high training frequency, a starting maintenance estimate of 35–45 kilocalories per kilogram of bodyweight per day is appropriate. Athletes with very high training volumes, two-a-days, or heavy conditioning loads may be at the upper end or above this range.

Goal state adjustments from maintenance estimate:

Goal State  Calorie Adjustment  Key Conditions
Performance maintenance  ±0  No change to bodyweight goal; fueling training
Gradual fat loss  Reduce by 300–500 kcal/day  Only if training performance and recovery remain intact
Aggressive fat loss (not recommended for athletes in hard training blocks)  Reduce by 500–750 kcal/day  Flag for Tier 2 or 3 monitoring; not compatible with high-intensity blocks
Muscle gain  Add 200–400 kcal/day  Use the lower end to minimize excess fat gain
Recomposition  Maintenance or slight deficit (100–200 kcal) with high protein  Best for beginners or detrained athletes returning to training
Competition performance (peaking, not cutting)  Maintenance or slight surplus  Carbohydrate timing becomes primary lever

If-then adjustment rules (apply at the one- to two-week review):

- If the athlete is losing weight faster than 0.5–1 kilogram per week and training quality is dropping: increase calories by 200–300 kilocalories, primarily from carbohydrate.
- If the athlete is gaining weight faster than 0.25–0.5 kilogram per week and fat gain appears excessive: reduce calories by 200–300 kilocalories, primarily from the surplus.
- If the athlete reports persistent fatigue, poor sleep quality, or reduced performance despite adequate rest: check calorie adequacy before reducing training. Undereating is more commonly the cause than overtraining in athletes who otherwise have good training habits.
- If training performance is stable and body composition is moving toward the stated goal: no adjustment required.
- If the athlete is cutting to a weight class and performance is dropping sharply: reassess the cut timeline and rate; a slower cut is preferable to sacrificing training quality.

70.2 Macronutrient Decision Rules

Protein: Starting ranges: 1.6–2.0 grams per kilogram/day for moderate training volume; 2.0–2.4 grams per kilogram/day for high training volume or hard dieting phase; athletes over 40 err toward the upper end; vegan athletes add 10–15% above the stated range.

Carbohydrates: Scaled to training volume and intensity, not to body composition goals alone. Starting ranges: low activity day or deload: 3–4 g/kg/day; moderate training day: 4–6 g/kg/day; hard training day (heavy lifting, BJJ, conditioning, or two-a-days): 6–8 g/kg/day; competition day or pre-competition: 7–10 g/kg/day. Low-carbohydrate approaches are contraindicated during hard conditioning blocks, high-volume hypertrophy phases, repeated same-day training sessions, and weight-class competition preparation close to competition.

Fat: Remaining calorie budget after protein and carbohydrate are established. Minimum fat intake for hormonal function is approximately 0.8–1.0 grams per kilogram per day. Fat should not be reduced below this floor even in aggressive cutting phases.

Article 71 — Carbohydrate Periodization for Strength, Hypertrophy, Conditioning, and Combat Sports

71.1 Why Carbohydrate Availability Matters

Muscle glycogen is the limiting substrate for high-intensity effort. A 45-minute heavy resistance session can deplete glycogen in type IIx fibers by 40–44%. Hard BJJ rounds, Muay Thai sparring, wrestling, or high-volume conditioning extend glycogen demands further. When glycogen is chronically low, training quality, power output, and recovery suffer.

Chronically low carbohydrate availability in high-volume athletes is one of the most common and correctable causes of performance regression. It is frequently misattributed to overtraining when underfeeding is the primary driver.

71.2 Session Type Classification and Carbohydrate Demand

High carbohydrate demand sessions: Heavy compound strength training (>75% of 1RM, multiple heavy sets); high-volume hypertrophy blocks; hard BJJ, wrestling, Muay Thai, or boxing sparring or drilling with high intensity; competitive conditioning (high-intensity intervals, circuits, AMRAP formats); two-a-day training sessions; competition or testing day.

Moderate carbohydrate demand sessions: Moderate-volume strength days (submaximal loading); technical drilling in combat sports at low to moderate intensity; accessory or hypertrophy work without heavy compound loading; single moderate-intensity conditioning session.

Low carbohydrate demand sessions: Active recovery, mobility, or light GPP work; deload sessions; rest days with low activity; low-intensity steady-state aerobic work below 60–65% of maximum heart rate.

71.3 Carbohydrate Periodization Framework

Day Type  Target Carbohydrate  Practical Examples
High demand training day  6–8 g/kg or higher  Heavy squat/deadlift day, hard BJJ, competitive conditioning, competition day
Moderate demand training day  4–6 g/kg  Moderate upper body push-pull, technical drilling, single moderate conditioning session
Low demand or rest day  2–4 g/kg  Active recovery, rest day, deload, mobility
Competition day or peak  7–10 g/kg  Prioritize high-glycemic carbohydrate availability pre-event

71.4 Pre-Session Carbohydrate Timing

Two to four hours before training: A full meal containing moderate to high carbohydrate (50–150 grams), a complete protein source, and low to moderate fat. Avoid very high fat or high fiber meals close to training.

One hour before training: A smaller carbohydrate-focused snack (25–50 grams, lower fiber) if the pre-session meal timing is not possible.

30 minutes or less before training (emergency fuel only): Fast-digesting carbohydrate only (20–30 grams). Avoid protein and fat at this window.

Fasted training: If the session is low-to-moderate intensity and under 60 minutes, fasted training is generally tolerable. If the session is high intensity, involves combat sport, or exceeds 60–75 minutes, fasted training will compromise output and recovery. Chronic fasted hard training is a performance and recovery liability.

71.5 Intra-Session Carbohydrate

Under 60 minutes, moderate intensity: Water alone is sufficient for most sessions.

Over 60 minutes OR high-intensity combat sport or conditioning: 25–60 grams of fast-digesting carbohydrate per hour.

Two-a-days or back-to-back sessions within the same day: Intra-session carbohydrate becomes more important. Begin carbohydrate intake earlier in the session and continue into the recovery window between sessions.

71.6 Post-Session Carbohydrate and Recovery

Glycogen resynthesis is fastest in the first 30–60 minutes after training. A combination of fast-digesting carbohydrate (0.8–1.2 grams per kilogram) with protein (0.3–0.4 grams per kilogram) in this window supports glycogen replenishment and initiates muscle protein synthesis simultaneously.

If two sessions occur on the same day with fewer than four hours between them: aggressive post-session carbohydrate replenishment in the recovery window becomes critical for maintaining quality in the second session.

Article 72 — Protein Rules, Meal Distribution, and Recovery Support

72.1 Protein Targets by Goal and Athlete State

Standard strength or hypertrophy athlete: 1.6–2.2 grams per kilogram of bodyweight per day.

High-volume training or dieting phase: 2.0–2.4 grams per kilogram.

Combat sport athletes (BJJ, wrestling, Muay Thai, boxing): 1.8–2.2 grams per kilogram.

Older athletes (40+): 2.0–2.4 grams per kilogram. Older athletes require more total protein per meal and benefit from distributing leucine-rich meals evenly throughout the day.

Vegetarian athletes: Add 10–15% to the target and emphasize leucine-rich plant sources (soy, legumes, edamame).

Vegan athletes: Add 15–20% to the target. Strategic pairing of complementary plant proteins helps achieve complete essential amino acid coverage.

72.2 Leucine Threshold and Meal-Level Protein

Muscle protein synthesis requires a leucine threshold of approximately 2.5–3 grams of leucine per meal. This corresponds to approximately 30–40 grams of high-quality animal protein per meal (chicken, beef, fish, eggs, dairy) or higher amounts from plant sources due to lower leucine density.

Practical implication: distribute protein across three to five meals that each contain 30–50 grams of quality protein.

72.3 Meal Distribution Decision Rules

Target: 3–5 protein-containing meals per day, each with 30–50 grams of protein.

If the athlete reports eating fewer than three meals per day: the first nutritional priority is increasing meal frequency and protein distribution, not manipulating macro ratios.

Pre-sleep protein: a protein feeding of 30–40 grams before sleep (casein, cottage cheese, or a casein-containing shake) may support overnight muscle protein synthesis, particularly in hypertrophy phases or during hard training blocks with high soreness.

72.5 Protein in Dieting Phases

During a caloric deficit, protein requirements increase. Apply the upper range of protein targets (2.2–2.4 grams per kilogram) during any fat loss phase. Higher protein in a deficit supports lean mass retention, increases satiety, and has higher thermogenic effect than carbohydrate or fat.

Article 73 — Hydration and Electrolyte Decision Rules

73.1 Hydration Physiology Basics

A fluid loss equal to 1% of bodyweight can meaningfully reduce blood volume and exercise performance. At 2% loss, strength and power output decline. At 3–4%, cognitive function, reaction time, and endurance are compromised. At 4%+, cramping and heat exhaustion risk are elevated significantly.

Thirst is a lagging indicator. By the time an athlete reports thirst during exercise, they are already mildly dehydrated. Proactive drinking is always more effective than reactive drinking.

73.2 Hydration Assessment — Practical Markers

Urine color (primary field marker): Pale straw yellow = well hydrated (target); dark yellow = mild dehydration; amber or darker = significant dehydration; clear = potentially over-hydrated (risk of dilutional hyponatremia in prolonged exercise).

73.3 Pre-Session Hydration Protocol

- 2–3 hours before training: 500–600 mL of fluid
- 20–30 minutes before training: 200–250 mL of fluid

73.4 Intra-Session Hydration

- Under 60 minutes, mild conditions: Water ad libitum.
- 60–90 minutes, moderate conditions: 400–600 mL per hour of water or a low-concentration electrolyte drink.
- Over 90 minutes or high-intensity or hot/humid conditions: 400–800 mL per hour. Add sodium at 300–700 mg per liter.

73.5 Post-Session Rehydration

Replace 1.25–1.5 liters of fluid per kilogram of bodyweight lost during the session. Include sodium (300–700 mg per liter) in recovery fluid to promote fluid retention.

73.6 Electrolyte Decision Rules

Sodium is the primary electrolyte lost in sweat. Average sweat sodium concentration is approximately 1.0–1.5 grams per liter.

Potassium is important for muscle contractility. Athletes with high sweat rates or who train in heat should ensure adequate dietary potassium from whole foods.

Magnesium is lost in sweat and commonly under-consumed in athletes with restricted diets or high training loads. Magnesium deficiency contributes to cramping, poor sleep, and impaired neuromuscular function.

Cramping decision tree: Cramping during or immediately after hard training in heat: likely sodium and fluid deficits. Cramping at rest or at night not related to heat or session: may involve magnesium, potassium, or total dietary deficiency. Cramping with severe dehydration signs: medical attention, do not train.

73.7 Hyponatremia Caution

Hyponatremia (low blood sodium) is caused by over-consumption of plain water, diluting blood sodium concentration. Risk factors include drinking very large volumes of plain water during prolonged sessions, not including sodium in fluids during prolonged events, being a salty sweater, and engaging in endurance-type events. Prevention: include sodium in hydration during prolonged events.

Article 74 — Pre-Workout, Intra-Workout, and Post-Workout Fueling

74.1 Pre-Workout Fueling by Session Context

Standard pre-training meal (2–3 hours before): 30–50 grams of protein; 50–100+ grams of carbohydrate; moderate fat (15–30 grams); moderate fiber.

One hour before (smaller, simpler): 20–30 grams of protein, lean source; 30–60 grams of carbohydrate, lower fiber; minimal fat.

30 minutes or less (emergency only): 20–30 grams of fast-digesting carbohydrate only. Avoid protein and fat at this window.

74.2 Fasted Training Protocols

Low-intensity sessions under 60 minutes: tolerable fasted for most athletes.

Moderate to high-intensity sessions: recommend at minimum 20–30 grams of fast-digesting carbohydrate 15–30 minutes pre-session if the athlete cannot tolerate a full meal. Hard BJJ, wrestling, sparring, or competitive conditioning fasted is a clear performance compromise.

Chronic fasted hard training is a compounding liability. The engine should consistently recommend against it in athletes with performance or muscle gain goals.

74.3 Intra-Workout Fueling

- Under 60 minutes, single session: Water only is sufficient for most athletes.
- 60–90 minutes, moderate to high intensity: 25–40 grams of carbohydrate per hour.
- Over 90 minutes or two sessions in one day: 40–60 grams of carbohydrate per hour.

74.4 Post-Workout Fueling Protocols

Standard post-training recovery window (within 30–60 minutes):
- Carbohydrate: 0.8–1.2 grams per kilogram of bodyweight
- Protein: 0.3–0.4 grams per kilogram of bodyweight (minimum 30–40 grams)
- Fat: minimal in the immediate recovery window

Practical post-training recovery options: Shake with whey protein, banana, and water or milk; chocolate milk; rice with chicken or fish; Greek yogurt with fruit.

74.6 Two-a-Day Fueling Requirements

Session 1 pre-training: Full pre-session meal or at minimum a carbohydrate-protein snack. Between sessions: Immediate carbohydrate and protein (0.8–1.2 g/kg carbohydrate, 30–40 g protein) as soon as possible after session 1, plus active rehydration. Session 2 pre-training: A small top-up snack 30–60 minutes before session 2 if the full recovery window has elapsed. Post-session 2: Full recovery meal.

Athletes who cannot meet these fueling requirements should reduce two-a-day frequency or reduce the intensity of one session until fueling can support both.

Article 75 — Supplements

75.1 Hierarchy Before Supplements

The engine should apply this hierarchy before discussing any supplement:

1. Total calorie adequacy
2. Protein targets consistently met
3. Carbohydrate adequacy for training demands
4. Hydration and electrolyte management
5. Sleep quantity and quality
6. Training consistency and progressive overload
7. Supplements

An athlete who is undereating, under-sleeping, and chronically dehydrated will not benefit meaningfully from any supplement. Address the hierarchy first.

75.2 Creatine Monohydrate

Well-supported, evidence-backed, widely used. Dosing: loading phase (optional) 20 grams per day in 4 divided doses for 5–7 days, then maintenance; maintenance dose 3–5 grams per day. Timing is not critical. Generally well-tolerated in healthy individuals. Not recommended for athletes with diagnosed kidney disease or renal insufficiency — refer to physician. Weight gain of 0.5–2 kg may occur in the first week due to intramuscular water retention. Choose a product with third-party verification particularly for competitive athletes.

75.3 Caffeine

Well-supported ergogenic aid. Effective dose: 3–6 mg per kilogram of bodyweight. Timing: 30–45 minutes before training. Caffeine tolerance develops with regular use. Habitual high-dose users will have attenuated performance benefits. Contraindications: individuals with anxiety disorders, panic disorder, or cardiac arrhythmias should approach cautiously. Caffeine use in the afternoon or evening may disrupt sleep quality.

75.4 Beta-Alanine

Moderate evidence support for specific use cases. Benefits most apparent in efforts of 60–240 seconds sustained high intensity. Dosing: 3.2–6.4 grams per day in divided doses. Paresthesia (tingling/flushing) is common and harmless; take doses with food or split into smaller doses. Who benefits most: athletes who regularly train or compete in sustained high-intensity efforts.

75.6 Protein Powder

Practical nutritional tool, not a required supplement. Whey protein: fast-digesting, ideal for post-training use. Casein protein: slow-digesting, suitable for pre-sleep protein. Plant-based protein (pea, rice, soy blend): appropriate for vegan athletes. Dosing: 20–40 grams per serving. Choose third-party certified products; protein powders vary significantly in quality.

75.8 Omega-3 Fatty Acids

Broad health support, moderate performance relevance. Dosing: 2–3 grams of combined EPA plus DHA per day. Consistent evidence for cardiovascular health benefits; emerging evidence for modest reductions in exercise-induced muscle soreness. High doses (above 3–4 grams EPA+DHA per day) may increase bleeding time.

75.9 Vitamin D

Condition health and performance relevance (widespread deficiency risk in athletes). Dosing: 1000–3000 IU per day for general maintenance. Recommend as a reasonable baseline supplement for strength and combat athletes, particularly if sun exposure is limited or indoor training is the norm. Pair with vitamin K2 at higher doses.

75.10 Magnesium

Widely under-consumed; practical for high-sweat athletes and athletes with cramping or sleep complaints. Dosing: 200–400 mg of elemental magnesium per day from magnesium glycinate, malate, or citrate. High-dose magnesium supplementation is contraindicated in athletes with renal disease without physician oversight.

75.11 Dietary Nitrates and Beetroot

Emerging evidence; most relevant for endurance and repeated high-intensity efforts. Dosing: 300–600 mg of dietary nitrate approximately 2–3 hours before training. Avoid mouthwash for several hours around nitrate supplementation (mouthwash kills the oral bacteria needed to convert nitrate to nitric oxide).

75.12 Collagen, Gelatin, and Vitamin C for Tissue Support

Emerging connective tissue support consideration. Early evidence suggests consuming 15–20 grams of gelatin or collagen hydrolysate with 50–200 mg of vitamin C approximately 60 minutes before a connective tissue loading activity may increase collagen synthesis markers. This is not established therapy and does not replace medical rehabilitation or physician guidance.

75.13 Pre-Workout Blends

High variability in quality and risk profile; approach with caution. The engine should not specifically recommend named pre-workout products. Individual ingredient guidance is more transparent and controllable. If an athlete uses a pre-workout blend, choose a product with third-party certification. Red flags: proprietary blends that do not list individual ingredient doses; stimulants beyond caffeine; claims that sound too extreme; no third-party certification.

75.14 Banned Substance and Third-Party Testing

Competitive athletes subject to anti-doping testing face contamination risk from supplements. Flag this risk for any athlete who competes in a tested sport. Third-party testing does not eliminate risk completely but reduces it significantly. The only fully safe approach for tested athletes is to avoid all supplements not third-party certified.

75.15 Red Flag Supplements — Do Not Recommend

Product Category  Reason
Diuretics or water pills (non-medical)  Dehydration and electrolyte risks; banned in many sports
Testosterone boosters and hormone precursors  Inadequate evidence; potential hormonal disruption; banned substance risk
Stimulant fat burners (containing DMAA, DMHA, ephedrine)  Cardiovascular risk; banned in tested sport
Growth hormone secretagogues  Banned in most tested sports; not appropriate for coaching scope
HCG, peptides, SARMs  Banned substances; outside coaching scope
Weight-loss products with undisclosed stimulants  Contamination risk; cardiovascular risk
Detox cleanses and liver flush products  No meaningful evidence
Yohimbine at high doses  Cardiovascular and psychiatric risk in sensitive individuals

Article 76 — Weight-Class, Cutting, Refeed, and Competition-Week Nutrition Cautions

76.1 Weight-Class Risk Classification

Routine weight management (low risk): The athlete is within 2–3% of their competition weight year-round. No extreme acute manipulation is planned. Gradual fat loss of 0.3–0.5 kilograms per week in the weeks before competition.

Moderate risk: The athlete is 4–6% above competition weight within 8–12 weeks of competition.

High risk (flag for referral or professional consultation): The athlete is more than 6–8% above competition weight within 6 weeks of competition; rapid weight cutting (water and food restriction in the 24–48 hours before weigh-in) is planned; the athlete is a young or developing athlete (under 18).

76.2 Gradual Cut Decision Rules

A gradual cut at a rate of 0.5–1% of bodyweight per week is the engine's conservative default. If training is hard and the competition is approaching: target the slower end (0.3–0.5 kg per week). If a larger cut is needed: add 2–4 weeks to the timeline rather than increasing the weekly deficit.

During a gradual cut: maintain protein at 2.0–2.4 g/kg; reduce carbohydrate first; fat should not fall below 0.8 g/kg.

76.3 Refeeding During a Cut

A refeed is a planned, temporary increase in caloric intake (primarily from carbohydrate) during a fat loss phase. Insert a refeed day every 7–14 days for athletes in a gradual cut lasting more than 3 weeks. On a refeed day, increase carbohydrate to maintenance-level intake or higher (5–7 g/kg); keep protein constant. A single refeed day is not a cheat day — it is structured and intentional.

76.4 Competition-Week Nutrition

Days 7–3 before competition: maintain training nutrition habits; slightly reduce training volume (taper); carbohydrate intake should remain similar or slightly increase as training volume drops.

Days 2–1 before competition: emphasize carbohydrate availability; reduce dietary fiber; avoid high-fat, high-fiber, or unfamiliar foods; maintain hydration.

76.5 Acute Water Weight Cutting — Do Not Recommend Without Professional Oversight

Aggressive acute dehydration (sauna, heavy sweating, fluid restriction, diuretics) in the 24–48 hours before weigh-in is associated with impaired strength, power, and reaction time; cognitive and coordination deficits; cardiovascular stress; kidney stress; dangerous electrolyte imbalances; and death in extreme cases. The engine should not recommend or generate protocols for acute water manipulation.

Article 78 — Nutrition Troubleshooting: Low Energy, Cramping, GI Issues, Poor Sleep, Appetite, and Weight Drift

78.1 Low Energy and Fatigue

Nutrition check sequence:

1. Is total caloric intake sufficient for training demands? Check for undereating or poor meal planning.
2. Is carbohydrate availability adequate on hard training days? Carbohydrate-depleted strength and combat athletes frequently feel flat. Increase pre- and intra-session carbohydrate before attributing fatigue to other causes.
3. Is protein adequately distributed? Low protein per meal reduces recovery quality even if total daily protein is met.
4. Is iron deficiency possible? Female athletes, vegetarian or vegan athletes, and calorie-restricted athletes are at elevated risk.
5. Is vitamin D deficiency possible? Especially relevant for indoor athletes, darker skin tones, and winter months.
6. Is sleep independently adequate?

78.2 Muscle Cramping

Nutrition check sequence:

1. Training-related cramping associated with heat, sweat, or prolonged sessions: primary suspects are sodium depletion and fluid deficit. Increase pre-session sodium, intra-session sodium-containing fluid, and post-session rehydration.
2. Cramping at rest or at night: consider dietary magnesium intake. Supplemental magnesium glycinate or citrate (200–400 mg elemental) in the evening is a practical first intervention.
3. Active weight cutting: weight cutting involving fluid restriction can cause severe electrolyte imbalances. Refer to Article 76 cautions.
4. Severe cramping with dark urine or signs of kidney stress: refer to a physician.

78.3 Gastrointestinal Issues Around Training

Nutrition check sequence:

1. Timing of pre-training meal: a meal consumed too close to training (within 30–60 minutes) causes GI distress. Move the meal earlier, reduce fat and fiber closer to training.
2. Intra-session nutrition: concentrated carbohydrate solutions slow gastric emptying. Dilute sports drinks or gels with extra water.
3. New supplements or ingredients: any new supplement introduced recently may be the cause. Remove and reassess.
4. Lactose or whey sensitivity: if GI issues follow protein shakes, switch to whey isolate or plant-based protein.
5. Persistent GI symptoms unrelated to training or nutrition changes: refer to a physician.

78.4 Poor Sleep and Nutrition

Nutrition check sequence:

1. Caffeine timing: caffeine consumed after midday can impair sleep quality. Recommend limiting caffeine to before noon or early afternoon.
2. Pre-sleep nutrition: a large high-carbohydrate meal immediately before sleep may impair sleep quality in some individuals.
3. Alcohol: impairs sleep architecture, reducing slow-wave and REM sleep.
4. Magnesium: magnesium deficiency is associated with sleep disruption.
5. High stimulant supplement use: pre-workout use late in the day is a common and easily correctable cause of impaired sleep.

78.6 Bodyweight Drift

Engine decision tree for unexpected weight gain: Is the athlete in a creatine loading phase? Is the athlete in a new hypertrophy phase with increased caloric intake? Is the gain disproportionate relative to calorie intake? Is there unexplained rapid weight gain accompanied by swelling, breathlessness, or other systemic symptoms? (Refer to physician.)

Engine decision tree for unexpected weight loss: Is the weight loss intentional and at an appropriate rate? Is the athlete in hard training and losing weight faster than expected? (Check calorie adequacy.) Is the weight loss unexplained and accompanied by fatigue, low appetite, and poor performance? (Refer to physician.)

Article 79: Stateful Coaching Mode — From Program Generator to Ongoing Coach

Purpose

The engine transitions from a one-shot program generator into a persistent coaching agent the moment a client receives their first program. Stateful coaching mode is the default engine state for all returning clients.

What Stateful Coaching Mode Is

Stateful coaching mode means the engine:

1. Holds the active program in context — block, week, session, exercise order, and load scheme
2. Interprets all incoming client messages relative to that program
3. Defaults to minimum necessary changes before considering larger interventions
4. Tracks cumulative context across multiple interactions (fatigue signals, missed sessions, substitution history, check-in data)
5. Never reverts to intake behavior unless explicitly triggered by a new program request or end-of-block handoff

The engine is not a chatbot in this mode. It is a coaching agent with a job: get this client to the end of this block.

Decision Rules

Rule 1: Identify message type before responding. Every client message belongs to one of five categories: (a) check-in update, (b) disruption report, (c) substitution request, (d) clarification question, or (e) general feedback. Route accordingly.

Rule 2: Default to the program. The program is correct until there is concrete evidence it is not.

Rule 3: Preserve block continuity. Changes within a block should be session-level or week-level — not block-level — unless the disruption is severe.

Rule 4: Flag accumulation. A single disruption is noise. Two or more in a block is a pattern. Three or more is a coaching problem.

Rule 5: Don't over-explain. Clients in stateful coaching mode are already trained on the system. Responses should be tight, directive, and action-oriented.

If/Then Routes

- If client sends a message with no disruption signal → confirm adherence, affirm, and prompt next session or check-in
- If client sends a disruption signal → classify disruption type (see Articles 81–82) and route to appropriate protocol
- If client asks a program question → answer within existing program parameters; do not restructure
- If client requests a substitution → route to Article 83 logic
- If client sends an end-of-week update → route to Article 84 check-in protocol
- If client says "I want a new program" → acknowledge, confirm end-of-block status, and initiate new intake sequence
- If client message is ambiguous → ask one clarifying question; do not assume and route

Common Mistakes

- Treating every message as a program generation trigger.
- Over-adjusting for single-session complaints.
- Losing program context between interactions.
- Applying beginner coaching language to experienced clients.
- Confusing empathy with program deviation.

Article 80: Follow-Up Question Triage — Classifying Client Messages Without Rebuilding the Program

Purpose

Incoming client messages in coaching mode are high-variance. The engine's job is to triage each message — identify what type of response it actually requires — and answer precisely, without escalating the response level unnecessarily.

The Five Message Categories

Category 1: Adherence Check-In — Client is reporting that training is happening as planned. Affirm briefly, prompt continuation, and update state.

Category 2: Disruption Report — Client is flagging that something in their training environment has changed. Requires routing to Articles 81 or 82.

Category 3: Substitution Request — Client needs to swap one or more exercises. Requires routing to Article 83.

Category 4: Clarification Question — Client is asking about the program as written. Answer directly. Do not alter the program.

Category 5: General Feedback / Open-Ended — Client is sharing an experience, emotion, or observation without a specific ask. Acknowledge briefly, probe for any actionable signal, and return to program focus.

Decision Rules

- Rule 1: Classify before responding. Read the full message. Identify the primary category.
- Rule 2: Match response depth to message depth. A one-sentence check-in deserves a two-to-three sentence acknowledgment.
- Rule 3: Probe ambiguity with one question. Not two. One targeted question that resolves the ambiguity.
- Rule 4: Never treat Category 4 as Category 2. Curiosity is not resistance.
- Rule 5: Categories 1 and 4 do not alter the program.

Article 81: Missed Session and Schedule Disruption Protocols

Disruption Classification

Type A: Single Missed Session (Isolated) — One session missed in an otherwise clean week. Low impact. Standard recovery route applies.

Type B: Multiple Sessions Missed in One Week — Two or more sessions missed. Moderate impact.

Type C: Week-Wide Disruption — Client was effectively unable to train for the entire week. High impact.

Type D: Recurring Disruption Pattern — Same disruption type appearing across multiple weeks. Signals a structural mismatch between the program and the client's real schedule.

Decision Rules

- Rule 1: Never try to "catch up" missed strength work on back-to-back days.
- Rule 2: A missed session in Week 1 or 2 of a block is less consequential than in Week 3 or 4.
- Rule 3: Skip before compress for heavy primary sessions.
- Rule 4: Repeat a week only if the disruption was severe enough to prevent meaningful training adaptation.
- Rule 5: Never restructure a block mid-block due to a single disruption event.

If/Then Routes

- If Type A, session is primary lift day: Attempt to move session within 48 hours; if not possible, skip and note in log. Do not add make-up sets to next session.
- If Type A, session is accessory/conditioning day: If next day permits, fold key accessory work into the next session as secondary. If not, skip entirely.
- If Type B (2 sessions missed), early in week: Identify the two highest-priority sessions for the week and protect them. Drop the lowest-priority session(s).
- If Type C (whole week missed): If Week 1: repeat the week. If Week 2: judgment call, lean toward repeating. If Week 3–4: skip and move to next block or planned deload. If due to illness: add one deload week before resuming regardless of block position.
- If Type D (recurring pattern across 2+ weeks): Flag the pattern explicitly. Ask one diagnostic question about the client's actual schedule. Prepare a schedule adjustment for the next block.

Article 82: Fatigue, Soreness, and Readiness-Based Weekly Adjustments

Fatigue Signal Classification

Level 1: Normal Training Fatigue — Muscle soreness specific to trained muscles, resolving within 48–72 hours, not affecting joint integrity or movement quality. No adjustment required.

Level 2: Elevated but Manageable Fatigue — Generalized tiredness or persistent soreness that is above baseline but not limiting. Movement quality is intact. One or two adjustment tools apply.

Level 3: High Fatigue / Significant Readiness Drop — Client is reporting multiple concurrent signals: poor sleep for 3+ nights, persistent soreness not resolving between sessions, mood drop, motivation loss, and/or performance decline across two or more sessions. Week-level adjustment warranted.

Level 4: Overreached / Non-Functional Fatigue — Performance is declining meaningfully, joint soreness is present, client is avoiding the gym or dreading sessions. This is a deload trigger — not an adjustment trigger.

Decision Rules

- Rule 1: Distinguish soreness from pain. Muscle soreness is diffuse, arrives 24–48 hours after training, and resolves. Pain is sharp, joint-associated, acute-onset, or does not resolve between sessions.
- Rule 2: Ask two questions before adjusting. (1) Is sleep disrupted? (2) Is performance declining, or just perception declining?
- Rule 3: Volume is the first adjustment lever, not intensity. Reduce sets before reducing weight.
- Rule 4: Deload triggers cannot be substituted with light sessions.
- Rule 5: Fatigue signals in the last week of a block are normal and expected.

Adjustment Toolkit

Signal Level  Primary Tool  Secondary Tool  Do Not
Level 1  No change  Optional: remind recovery basics  Reduce load
Level 2  Cut 1–2 working sets  Reduce top-set intensity by 5–10%  Eliminate sessions
Level 3  Reduce volume by 30–40%, keep intensity  Consider session consolidation  Skip block-critical lifts
Level 4  Full deload week  Evaluate next-block entry  Continue normal training

Article 83: Substitution Requests — Equipment, Time, Pain, and Preference Routing

The Four Substitution Drivers

Driver 1: Equipment Unavailability — The client lacks access to the specific implement, machine, or surface required for the exercise. The substitute must replicate the loading pattern with available equipment.

Driver 2: Time Constraint — The client has less time than the session requires. Which exercises are cut, which are preserved, and in what order.

Driver 3: Pain Signal — The client reports discomfort or pain associated with the exercise. Requires a movement-based substitution that removes the aggravating element while preserving training stress.

Driver 4: Preference / Boredom — The client dislikes an exercise without a structural reason to swap it. Preferences are valid but carry the lowest substitution priority.

Decision Rules

- Rule 1: Identify the driver before selecting a substitute.
- Rule 2: The substitute must match the original exercise's primary intent.
- Rule 3: Pain-based substitutions require a one-question pain probe before routing. Ask: "Is it sharp pain during the movement, or a dull ache afterward?"
- Rule 4: Time-based substitutions preserve primary lifts. Primary strength work stays; accessory work is first to be cut.
- Rule 5: Preference swaps require confirmation that the substitute serves the same training intent.

Movement Pattern Substitution Map

Hip Hinge: Romanian deadlift → stiff-leg deadlift → good morning → cable pull-through → single-leg RDL

Squat Pattern: Back squat → front squat → goblet squat → safety bar squat → leg press (last resort; non-equivalent)

Horizontal Push: Barbell bench → dumbbell bench → cable press → push-up (loaded) → landmine press

Horizontal Pull: Barbell row → dumbbell row → cable row → chest-supported row → seal row

Vertical Pull: Pull-up → lat pulldown → assisted pull-up → single-arm cable pulldown

Vertical Push: Overhead press → seated DB press → landmine press → cable overhead press

Knee Flexion: Leg curl → Nordic curl → Swiss ball curl → single-leg curl → glute-ham raise

Article 84: Progress Check-In Protocols — Weekly Updates, Load Decisions, and Next-Block Handoffs

Check-In Structure

A complete weekly check-in collects five data points:

1. Adherence: Sessions completed vs. planned
2. Performance: How did the main lifts feel? (Easy / On target / Hard / Failed)
3. Fatigue: Current energy and soreness state (1–10 or descriptive)
4. Disruptions: Anything that deviated from the program
5. Flags: Anything the coach should know — pain, life events, upcoming schedule changes

Load Decision Logic

Decision 1: Progress — All sessions completed, performance is on target or easy, fatigue is manageable, no major disruptions. Increase load as planned by the program's progression scheme.

Decision 2: Hold — Most sessions completed, performance feels hard but not failed, some fatigue present. Keep load identical to this week.

Decision 3: Reduce — Multiple sessions missed OR performance is declining OR fatigue signals are Level 3 or higher. Reduce volume by 20–30% while maintaining intensity.

Decision 4: Deload — Level 4 fatigue signal OR consistent decline across 2+ check-ins OR end of block deload week.

Decision Rules

- Rule 1: Progress requires at least 80% adherence.
- Rule 2: Hold is an active decision, not a failure. Frame holds explicitly as a strategic choice.
- Rule 3: Two consecutive holds in the same lift trigger a diagnostic. Ask: recovery, technique breakdown, or starting load miscalibration?
- Rule 4: Reduce before deloading in ambiguous cases.
- Rule 5: Log every load decision.

Next-Block Handoff Protocol

Step 1: Block Summary — Compile adherence rate, load progression achieved, disruptions logged, fatigue patterns, and substitutions made.

Step 2: Performance Assessment — Identify which lifts progressed as expected, which held or stalled, and which were affected by disruption or substitution.

Step 3: Client Input Collection — Ask three targeted questions: "What felt most productive in this block?" "What was most challenging or limiting?" "Is there anything you want to do more or less of next block?"

Step 4: Next-Block Parameters — Determine volume adjustment, intensity trajectory, schedule changes needed, and any exercise substitutions to make permanent or resolve.

Step 5: New Program Generation Trigger — Once Steps 1–4 are complete, initiate new program generation with the updated parameters.

Article 85: BJJ and Grappling Adjustment Rules — Mat Load, Grip Fatigue, Neck/Back Stress, and Competition Prep

Purpose

BJJ and grappling athletes train in one of the highest-interference sport environments that exist. Mat sessions combine isometric grip demands, neck bridging stress, spinal compression from positional grinding, and cardiovascular output — all on top of gym work. The engine must accurately read mat volume, classify the training week type, and route accordingly.

Key Stressors

Mat Load: Measured in mat hours per week, live rolling intensity, and drilling volume. A 10-session mat week is categorically different from a 4-session week.

Grip Fatigue: Grip is the most sensitive performance marker in grappling athletes. Pulling movements and loaded carries in the gym directly compete with mat grip recovery.

Neck and Upper Back Stress: Bridging, clinch, and positional grinding accumulate neck extensor and deep cervical fatigue. Trap bar deadlifts, barbell shrugs, and heavy loaded carries compound this stress.

Spinal Compression Load: High-frequency mat athletes absorb significant compressive spinal load through positional wrestling, turtle defense, and guard passing. This competes directly with axial loading in the gym.

Competition Prep Phase: The 4–6 weeks before a competition require specific routing that reduces gym stress while preserving neural drive, movement quality, and sport-specific conditioning.

Programming Implications

- Pulling volume is the first variable cut when mat load is high.
- Grip-intensive gym exercises are eliminated in high mat weeks.
- Neck accessory work is contraindicated in active mat weeks unless classified as low-intensity.
- Squat and deadlift volume scales inversely with mat hours.
- Competition prep routing enters at T-4 weeks. Volume drops 30–40%. Intensity is maintained or slightly elevated.

If/Then Decision Routes

If mat hours ≥ 10/week:
- Cut gym pulling volume by 50%
- Remove all grip-intensive accessory work
- Replace bilateral squats with split squats or step-ups
- Reduce axial loading sessions from 2x to 1x per week
- Upper body priority shifts to pressing and overhead stability only

If mat hours 6–9/week:
- Maintain primary strength lifts at base volume
- Reduce pulling accessory by 25%
- Monitor grip RPE; if athlete reports grip fatigue above 7/10, apply the ≥10 protocol immediately
- No neck-loaded accessory exercises

If mat hours ≤ 5/week:
- Standard gym template applies
- Light grip-specific prehab permitted
- Neck isometrics are safe to include

If competition is within 4 weeks:
- Activate Competition Prep routing
- Reduce total gym sets by 35%
- Keep 1 heavy lower and 1 heavy upper session per week for neural maintenance
- Eliminate all new exercises; prioritize movement quality and speed

If athlete reports neck pain ≥ 3/10:
- Remove all axial loading immediately
- Program horizontal pulling only at submaximal loads
- Flag for sports medicine referral before returning to axial loading

If weight cut is active (within 10 days of weigh-in):
- Gym work is maintenance only — no loading increases
- Cut all high-RPE sessions; keep movement quality sessions only
- Volume reduced by 40–50% of base

Red / Yellow / Green Flags

Red — Stop or Reroute Immediately: Neck pain ≥ 4/10 on any axial loaded movement; grip failure in the gym; acute cervical stiffness post-mat session affecting ROM; active weight cut + high mat week + heavy gym scheduled simultaneously.

Yellow — Modify Before Proceeding: Grip fatigue reported at 6–7/10 entering the gym session; mat hours increased ≥ 25% week-over-week; athlete skipping food between mat and gym sessions; performance in main lifts trending down ≥ 2 consecutive weeks during high mat phase.

Green — Proceed as Programmed: Mat hours stable at ≤ 5/week with no grip complaints; athlete reporting sleep ≥ 7 hours, appetite normal, mood positive; no competition within 6 weeks; main lift performance stable or trending up.

Generator Rules

1. Mat Hours Input Required. The engine cannot generate a weekly plan without mat hours per week declared.
2. Pulling Volume Cap. When mat hours ≥ 10, total weekly pulling sets are hard-capped at 6 working sets.
3. Grip Fatigue Modifier. If athlete inputs grip fatigue rating ≥ 7, apply ≥10 mat hours protocol regardless of actual mat hours reported.
4. Competition Week Lock. In competition weeks (T-0), generate movement quality and activation only. No strength work above 70% 1RM.
5. Neck Safety Interlock. Any neck pain report of ≥ 3/10 locks out all axial loading exercises until cleared.
6. Weight Cut Override. When weight cut flag is active, override all progressive loading rules. Maintenance-only mode is active.

Article 86: Boxing and Striking Adjustment Rules — Shoulder Volume, Footwork Load, Conditioning, and Sparring Weeks

Purpose

Boxers and striking-dominant athletes accumulate shoulder, rotator cuff, elbow, and wrist stress at volumes that most coaches underestimate. A 90-minute boxing session involves thousands of shoulder internal rotation reps, sustained footwork demands, and significant cardiovascular output. Layering gym pressing volume on top of this is a primary driver of shoulder impingement and elbow pathology in combat athletes.

Key Stressors

Shoulder Volume: Boxing generates massive internal rotation volume (jabs, crosses, hooks) and limited external rotation or horizontal abduction. The gym must correct this imbalance — not compound it.

Footwork Load: Skilled boxers train footwork for 30–60+ minutes per session. This creates significant lower leg and ankle stress.

Conditioning Sessions: Bag work, pad work, shadow boxing, and sparring are all cardiovascular outputs. The engine must account for conditioning density.

Sparring Weeks: Sparring is categorically different from technical boxing training. It involves acute trauma risk, CNS stress, and emotional/psychological loading.

If/Then Decision Routes

If sparring is scheduled this week (any amount):
- Cut pressing volume by 40%
- Remove all overhead pressing
- Maintain external rotation and shoulder stability work at full volume
- Reduce plyometric lower body work by 50%
- Keep 1 primary strength lower session

If sparring ≥ 3 sessions this week:
- Gym work is reduced to 2 sessions maximum
- Primary lifts only; no accessory volume
- No new loading; maintain current intensities only

If conditioning density is high (≥ 5 sessions bag/pad work):
- Reduce gym to 2 sessions
- Lower volume by 30% across all categories

If footwork sessions ≥ 4/week:
- Remove all plyometric lower body from gym program
- Replace with slow eccentrics and single-leg stability work
- Calf and Achilles prehab protocols added

If competition within 3 weeks:
- Pressing volume at 50% of base
- Shoulder stability work at full volume
- All gym sessions are maintenance; no progressive overload targets

Generator Rules

1. Sparring Flag is Binary and Overrides Volume Rules. When sparring week is declared, pressing cap engages immediately.
2. Pressing Volume Ceiling. In active boxing phases, pressing never exceeds 60% of base template volume. In sparring weeks, never exceeds 35%.
3. Shoulder Stability Lock-In. External rotation, face pulls, and scapular work are generated as non-negotiable components of every session.
4. Plyometric Interlock. When footwork sessions ≥ 4/week, all plyometric lower body exercises are blocked from generation.
5. Head Impact Protocol. If any concussion or acute head impact is flagged, generate zero gym work for that week and prompt coach for medical clearance.
6. Competition Prep Cutoff. Pressing overhead is eliminated entirely from T-3 weeks to fight week.

Article 87: MMA Hybrid Adjustment Rules — Blending Grappling, Striking, Strength, and Weight-Cut Stress

Purpose

MMA athletes are the most complex programming challenge in combat sports. They accumulate stressors from grappling, striking, clinch work, wrestling, and conditioning simultaneously — and then often layer a weight cut on top. The engine must synthesize grappling and striking rules, apply the higher constraint when both are active, and manage weight-cut stress as a separate override layer.

Programming Implications

- Apply the more restrictive constraint when both grappling and striking rules are active. Never average. Always use the more conservative route.
- Wrestling and clinch sessions are tracked separately and add their own load unit to the weekly total.
- Weight-cut phase requires a gym reduction of 40–60%, regardless of mat or boxing volume.
- Fight camp structure is divided into three sub-phases: General Prep (10–12 weeks out), Specific Prep (6–9 weeks out), and Peak/Taper (1–5 weeks out).

If/Then Decision Routes

If both grappling sessions ≥ 3/week AND striking sessions ≥ 3/week:
- Apply grappling rules + striking rules simultaneously; resolve all conflicts by taking the more restrictive option
- Gym work reduced to 2 sessions/week maximum
- Primary lift only per session; all accessory volume removed
- No plyometrics, no grip-intensive work, no overhead pressing

If weight cut is active (within 14 days of weigh-in):
- Override all sport-specific rules with weight-cut protocol
- Gym volume reduced 50% from whatever the sport-adjusted template prescribes
- Intensity capped at 80% 1RM on all primary lifts
- Session duration capped at 45 minutes

If weight cut + high mat + high striking all active simultaneously:
- Generate maintenance-only gym program
- 1 session/week maximum
- Flag to coach that athlete is at maximum tolerable stress

If fight camp, General Prep phase (10–12 weeks out):
- Gym volume at 80–90% of base template
- Strength development is the priority

If fight camp, Specific Prep phase (6–9 weeks out):
- Gym volume at 60–70% of base template
- No new exercises introduced

If fight camp, Peak/Taper phase (1–5 weeks out):
- Gym volume at 30–45% of base template
- 1–2 sessions/week; primary lifts only
- T-1 week: activation only, no heavy loading

Generator Rules

1. Dual-Sport Constraint Resolution. When both grappling and striking modules generate different constraints, the engine always applies the more restrictive of the two. No averaging.
2. Wrestling Adder. Each wrestling/clinch session adds +1 lower back fatigue unit. At ≥ 3 units, lower back load reduction protocol activates.
3. Weight Cut Override Layer. Weight-cut protocol overrides all sport-specific rules. It is not added to them — it replaces the output after all sport rules have been applied, then cuts the result by 50%.
4. Fight Camp Phase Detection. The engine must detect or prompt for fight camp phase.
5. Session Duration Cap. During weight-cut phase, no session longer than 45 minutes is generated.
6. PRs Blocked During Cut. All progressive overload targets are suspended when weight-cut flag is active.

Article 88: Running and Field-Sport Adjustment Rules — Impact Load, Speed Work, Strength Maintenance, and Tendon Cost

Purpose

Running athletes and field-sport athletes present a specific programming challenge: high-frequency ground impact, speed and power demands, and tendon stress that accumulates invisibly until it fails. The gym's primary role in these athletes is to support tendon health, maintain force production, and enhance speed — not to add more fatigue.

Key Stressors

Impact Load: Every ground contact in running or field sport is an impact event. High-volume running weeks generate significant patellar tendon, Achilles, and plantar fascia stress.

Speed Work: Sprint sessions represent the highest-cost field sport sessions. Scheduling heavy gym lower body within 48–72 hours of speed work directly impairs speed adaptation.

Strength Maintenance vs. Development: In-season field athletes are in maintenance mode for strength, not development.

Tendon Cost: The Achilles, patellar tendon, and proximal hamstring tendon are the primary injury sites. Heavy eccentric loading, plyometrics, and high-frequency leg training accelerate tendon stress accumulation.

If/Then Decision Routes

If running volume ≥ 70 km/week:
- Gym lower body volume reduced to 1 session/week
- No plyometrics
- Eccentric-focused lower body work reduced to 1 set per session
- Priority: tendon prehab (calf raise, tibialis, hip stability)

If running volume 40–69 km/week:
- 2 lower body gym sessions per week permitted
- Plyometrics: 1 low-intensity session permitted if not within 48 hours of tempo or speed session

If speed sessions ≥ 2/week (field athletes):
- Heavy lower body gym work scheduled on non-sprint days or at minimum 48 hours after sprint sessions
- No plyometrics on same day or day after speed sessions
- Hamstring eccentric work volume halved

If in-season, competition game this week:
- Gym work: 1 session, maintenance load (75–80% 1RM, reduced volume)
- No new exercises, no plyometrics, no intensity PR attempts

If tendon pain reported (Achilles, patellar, hamstring origin):
- Remove all plyometrics immediately
- Remove eccentric-focused exercises for affected tendon
- Substitute heavy isometric protocols
- Flag for physiotherapy assessment if pain ≥ 3/10 on loading

Generator Rules

1. 48-Hour Separation Rule. Enforces ≥ 48 hours between heavy gym lower body sessions and sport speed/sprint sessions. If scheduling conflict exists, gym session is moved, not the sport session.
2. In-Season vs. Off-Season Detection. Required input to set the appropriate development vs. maintenance mode.
3. Plyometric Lockout. On any week with speed sessions ≥ 2, plyometric gym volume is automatically reduced or eliminated.
4. Tendon Pain Override. Any tendon pain ≥ 3/10 removes all eccentric and plyometric work and substitutes isometric protocols until cleared.
5. Mileage Tier System: < 40 km/week (standard gym), 40–69 km/week (reduced lower body), ≥ 70 km/week (maintenance gym only).

Article 89: Hybrid Athlete Adjustment Rules — Concurrent Strength, Skill, Endurance, and Body Composition

Purpose

The hybrid athlete — an individual pursuing meaningful strength, meaningful endurance, sport skill development, and body composition goals simultaneously — is the hardest programming case. The engine must route these athletes through a hierarchical goal structure, manage concurrent training interference explicitly, and prevent the common failure mode of doing everything moderately and excelling at nothing.

Programming Implications

- Establish a primary goal hierarchy. Strength-primary, endurance-primary, or body-composition-primary determines which training domain receives full volume and which is reduced.
- Separate strength and endurance sessions by ≥ 6 hours minimum, ≥ 24 hours preferred.
- Skill sessions are placed first in the day or in isolated sessions. Never after high-fatigue strength or conditioning.
- Body composition caloric deficit is capped at 300–500 kcal/day when all training modalities are active.
- Weekly structure requires 2 dedicated recovery windows (no structured training) regardless of total volume.

If/Then Decision Routes

If primary goal is strength:
- Strength sessions at full volume; 3–4 sessions/week with progressive overload targets active
- Endurance: 2 sessions/week, low to moderate intensity, no high-intensity intervals
- Skill: placed on non-strength-heavy days or in morning sessions

If primary goal is endurance:
- Endurance sessions at full volume; apply Article 88 running rules
- Strength: 2 sessions/week, maintenance mode

If primary goal is body composition:
- Strength: 3 sessions/week, moderate volume, hypertrophy rep ranges (8–12)
- Endurance: 2–3 sessions/week, moderate intensity
- Caloric deficit: 400–600 kcal/day

If athlete is training all four modalities at high volume:
- Flag for coach review — unsustainable without periodization
- Apply 4-week rotation: strength emphasis → endurance emphasis → skill emphasis → recovery week

If skill performance is declining despite training:
- Skill sessions are moved to the first session of the day
- Fatigue-generating sessions moved to afternoon or evening
- If skill decline persists ≥ 2 weeks, reduce total training volume by 20%

Generator Rules

1. Hierarchy Required. The engine cannot generate a hybrid athlete program without a declared primary goal.
2. Session Sequencing Rule. Skill sessions are always generated before fatigue-generating sessions.
3. Concurrent Session Interference Calculator. When strength and endurance are scheduled same-day, the engine applies a +15% fatigue multiplier to next-day recovery assessment.
4. Body Composition Deficit Cap. When caloric deficit exceeds 500 kcal/day and training load is high, the engine generates a warning and reduces training volume recommendations.
5. Recovery Day Lock. A minimum of 2 complete recovery days per 7-day microcycle is hard-coded.
6. 4-Week Rotation Protocol. When all four modalities are at high volume, the engine automatically suggests a 4-week block periodization structure.

Article 90: Sport-Interference Decision Matrix — When to Push, Maintain, Reduce, or Deload Gym Work

Purpose

This article is the decision hub for the entire Cluster K system. Every sport-specific rule in Articles 85–89 feeds into this matrix. The matrix provides the coach with a unified decision framework to determine whether, in any given week, the athlete should push gym volume and intensity forward, maintain current loads, reduce them, or enter a full deload. These four states are mutually exclusive. The engine generates one state per week per athlete.

The Four States

PUSH — Progressive overload is appropriate. All conditions support loading increases.

MAINTAIN — Current gym loads and volumes are held constant. No progression targets. The default state during high sport-load weeks.

REDUCE — Gym volume is pulled back below current levels. A specific percentage reduction applies based on the severity of the flag that triggered Reduce.

DELOAD — Gym volume drops to 40–50% of base. Intensity is maintained at 60–70% of working weights. Sessions are shortened, all accessory work is removed.

Route to PUSH

All of the following must be true: sport sessions ≤ 5/week total, none classified as high-intensity sparring or competition; sleep ≥ 7 hours average; RPE on primary lifts at or below expected; gym performance stable or improving; competition ≥ 8 weeks out; no active injury or pain flags; no weight cut active; currently in accumulation or early intensification block.

Route to MAINTAIN

Any of the following, with no red flags: sport sessions 5–8/week with moderate intensity; sleep averaging 6.5–7 hours; RPE at expected level but not below; gym performance stable; competition 4–8 weeks out; no active injury, minor soreness reported; no weight cut active.

Route to REDUCE

Any of the following: sport sessions ≥ 8/week, or ≥ 3 high-intensity sport sessions; sleep averaging < 6.5 hours for 3+ consecutive days; RPE on primary lifts trending up ≥ 1.5 points above expected for 2+ weeks; gym performance declining over 2+ consecutive sessions; competition 2–4 weeks out; weight cut initiated; minor injury or tendon complaint (pain 1–3/10).

Reduce magnitude: Single trigger: reduce volume by 20%. Two triggers: reduce volume by 30%. Three or more triggers: reduce volume by 40%; apply Deload protocol at next check-in.

Route to DELOAD

Any of the following immediately triggers Deload: 3 or more Reduce triggers active simultaneously; performance declining in all gym sessions for 3+ consecutive weeks; active injury (pain ≥ 3/10 on any loaded movement); weight cut within 7 days of weigh-in; competition within 1 week; athlete reporting inability to complete sport sessions due to gym fatigue; following any block of ≥ 6 weeks without a prior deload.

Deload protocol: Volume: 40–50% of base template; Intensity: 60–70% of working weights; Sessions: 2 maximum; primary lifts only; Accessory: removed entirely; Session duration: 30–45 minutes; Goal: movement quality, tissue recovery, neural restoration.

Integrated Sport-Specific Overrides for State Assignment

Sport Context  Default State (moderate sport load)  Override Condition  Override State
BJJ, mat hours ≥ 10/week  MAINTAIN  + competition < 4 weeks  DELOAD
BJJ, neck pain active  REDUCE  Pain ≥ 4/10  DELOAD
Boxing, sparring ≥ 3/week  REDUCE  + pressing complaint  DELOAD
Boxing, no sparring, conditioning ≤ 3  MAINTAIN or PUSH  Performance improving  PUSH
MMA, weight cut active  REDUCE  Cut within 7 days  DELOAD
MMA, fight camp General Prep  MAINTAIN or PUSH  Performance trending up  PUSH
MMA, fight camp Peak  REDUCE  T-1 week  DELOAD
Running, mileage ≥ 70 km/week  REDUCE  Tendon pain active  DELOAD
Running, mileage < 40 km/week, off-season  MAINTAIN or PUSH  Strength developing  PUSH
Field sport, in-season game week  MAINTAIN  2+ games/week  REDUCE
Hybrid, all modalities high  REDUCE  Sleep < 6 hrs + decline  DELOAD
Hybrid, primary goal aligned, good recovery  MAINTAIN or PUSH  All green flags  PUSH

Escalation Protocol

- MAINTAIN → REDUCE escalation: If the athlete has been in MAINTAIN for ≥ 4 consecutive weeks without performance improvement in the gym, escalate the assessment.
- REDUCE → DELOAD escalation: If 3 or more triggers are simultaneously active during a REDUCE week, do not apply another week of Reduce. Escalate directly to DELOAD.
- DELOAD → MAINTAIN: After deload week, always return to MAINTAIN before assessing PUSH. No athlete goes directly from DELOAD to PUSH.
- PUSH → REDUCE: When a previously PUSH-eligible athlete enters a high sport load week, the state drops directly to MAINTAIN or REDUCE depending on load severity.

Generator Rules

1. One State Per Week. The engine generates exactly one of Push, Maintain, Reduce, or Deload per weekly assessment.
2. State Hierarchy. When multiple states are indicated by different inputs, the engine selects the most conservative state. Deload > Reduce > Maintain > Push.
3. Mandatory Deload Triggers. Hard-coded. These cannot be overridden by coach input unless the specific condition is explicitly cleared with clinical documentation logged.
4. Post-Deload Lock. After any deload week, the engine is locked to MAINTAIN for the following week.
5. MAINTAIN Time Cap. If the engine has generated MAINTAIN for ≥ 4 consecutive weeks, it must flag this to the coach and reassess.
6. Sport Load as Primary Input. Sport load variables always take precedence over gym performance data in state determination.

Article 91: Taper Decision Rules — Reducing Fatigue Without Losing Readiness

Purpose

The taper is not a rest week. It is a precision instrument for stripping accumulated fatigue while preserving — and in some cases expressing — accumulated fitness. The taper serves three functions: (1) dissipate accumulated mechanical and systemic fatigue; (2) preserve neuromuscular drive and sport-skill execution quality; (3) allow psychological readiness to peak alongside physical readiness.

Taper Decision Tree

Step 1 — Confirm baseline training load entering the taper window

If the athlete has been running high volume (8+ training units/week): apply a full structured taper of 10–14 days.

If the athlete has been running moderate volume (5–7 training units/week, near baseline): apply a short taper of 7–10 days.

If the athlete entered the final block undertrained or was managing injury: skip the standard taper protocol — use a "readiness prime" instead.

Step 2 — Determine primary competition demand

Maximum-strength expression (powerlifting, weightlifting, strongman): taper prioritizes intensity preservation with large volume reduction. Keep 70–90% of peak intensity; cut volume to 40–60% of peak volume by competition week.

Power-endurance or sport-skill (combat sport, CrossFit, team sport): intensity stays near competition-speed. Volume cuts are moderate (50–70% of peak).

Both strength and sport-skill: run a two-phase taper — first reduce volume while preserving intensity (days 10–5 out), then reduce intensity load while preserving movement quality and speed (days 4–1 out).

Step 3 — Assess current readiness markers

- Subjective readiness score: scores below 6 for 3+ consecutive days → extend taper by 2–3 days
- HRV trend: declining trend or suppressed resting HR variability → reduce volume further
- Bar speed or jump performance at submaximal loads: if velocity drops more than 10% below baseline at RPE 7 loads → athlete is carrying fatigue; taper is not yet complete
- Sleep quality: consistent 4–5 sleep quality scores → prioritize sleep intervention before manipulating training

Step 4 — Decide on the taper structure

Linear Taper: Volume reduces by a consistent percentage each day or week. Simplest. Works well for athletes with predictable fatigue response.

Step Taper: Large single drop in volume at the start of the taper window, then volume holds steady. Best for athletes who respond poorly to gradual reductions.

Exponential Taper: Volume drops steeply early in the window, then flattens as competition approaches. Most evidence-supported structure for strength-power athletes. Default recommendation for 10–14 day tapers.

Timing Rules

Competition Type  Taper Start  Intensity Preservation  Volume Target by Day –1
Powerlifting / Olympic Weightlifting  14 days out  90–95% of peak  30–40% of peak
Strongman / Highland Games  10–12 days out  85–90% of peak  40–50% of peak
Combat Sport (strength-focused camp)  10 days out  80% of peak  50–60% of peak
CrossFit / Hybrid Event  7–10 days out  75–85%  40–55% of peak
Team sport competition week  5–7 days out  70–80%  50–70% of peak

Non-negotiable timing rules:

- No new movements or loading patterns within 10 days of competition
- No DOMS-inducing work within 7 days of competition
- The final heavy training stimulus must occur no later than day –5 for strength athletes, day –3 for skill/speed-dominant athletes
- Day –1 is activation only

What to Remove During the Taper

- Accessory volume that exists only for hypertrophy stimulus
- High-fatigue conditioning methods
- Technical drilling of new or corrected movement patterns
- Any recreational activity with significant musculoskeletal loading
- Hard sparring the week of competition

What to Keep During the Taper

- Competition-specific movement patterns at competition intensity
- Submaximal motor pattern reinforcement
- Sport-skill rehearsal at speed — crisp, fast, confident reps
- Low-intensity aerobic work (10–20 min easy bike or walk)
- Competition-day warm-up sequence rehearsed in practice at least twice during taper week

Common Errors

Error 1: Keeping volume too high "just in case." Coaches who fear detraining cut volume by only 20–30%. Athletes arrive carrying fatigue.

Error 2: Removing intensity along with volume. Intensity stays high; volume is what falls.

Error 3: Adding in new recovery modalities. The week before competition is not the time for first-time cold plunge protocols or dietary overhauls.

Error 4: Letting the athlete train hard because "they feel great." A feeling of recovered readiness mid-taper is a signal the taper is working — not a green light to add volume back.

Error 5: Ignoring sport-specific context. Match the taper model to the competition demand.

Article 92: Competition Week Microcycles — Strength, Skill, Conditioning, and Recovery Placement

Purpose

Competition week is the most mismanaged microcycle in most programs. The competition week microcycle is a distinct programming category — its goal is not training. Its goal is controlled expression of accumulated fitness at the correct moment.

Taper Microcycle Decision Tree

Step 1 — Define the competition day and work backward

Competition day = Day 0. Build the microcycle in reverse.

- Day –1: Activation only. Light movement, CNS primer, psychological routine rehearsal. No training load.
- Day –2: Last technical session. Movement quality at submaximal load. No fatigue accumulation.
- Day –3: Optional light conditioning or skill maintenance. No strength training for pure strength athletes.
- Day –4: Last near-maximal session for strength athletes. 1–3 reps at 90–93% for powerlifters/weightlifters.
- Day –5 to –7: Standard taper session formats — reduced volume, preserved intensity, no new demands.

Step 2 — Categorize the athlete's competition type

Type A — Absolute Strength (powerlifting, weightlifting, strongman):

Day –7: Moderate volume strength session (60–70% of peak volume, 85–90% intensity)
Day –6: Rest or light aerobic / recovery work
Day –5: Light technical session — movement quality, pattern rehearsal, no fatigue
Day –4: Last heavy session — low volume, high intensity (1–3 sets × 1–3 reps at 90–95%)
Day –3: Full rest or light movement only
Day –2: Activation and technical cue rehearsal
Day –1: Activation only
Day 0: Compete

Type B — Skill + Strength (combat sport, functional fitness):

Day –7: Skill-speed session with strength component
Day –6: Strength maintenance session
Day –5: Skill-only session — no heavy loading
Day –4: Light conditioning + movement quality
Day –3: Rest or active recovery only
Day –2: Light skill rehearsal — no fatigue
Day –1: Activation and routine walkthrough
Day 0: Compete

Type C — Endurance-Strength Hybrid:

Day –7: Moderate conditioning session
Day –6: Strength maintenance (moderate load)
Day –5: Short skill session + active recovery
Day –4: Rest or light aerobic only
Day –3: Short activation + movement quality
Day –2: Rest
Day –1: Activation walkthrough
Day 0: Compete

Conditioning Placement Rules

- Never place high-intensity conditioning within 72 hours of competition
- Never place conditioning and near-maximal strength training in the same session during competition week
- Low-intensity aerobic conditioning can be used on rest days to maintain metabolic function and reduce the "flat" feeling
- Combat sport conditioning: technical-only by day –4; contact ends by day –5 minimum

Strength Training Placement Rules

- Last heavy strength stimulus: day –5 to –4 depending on athlete recovery rate
- Athletes with slower recovery profiles: last heavy session moves to day –6 or –7
- No eccentric-dominant training in competition week
- No DOMS-inducing volume in competition week

Common Errors

Error 1: Programming a "light full-body session" on day –2.

Error 2: Allowing hard skill training on day –3 or later.

Error 3: Rest day boredom leading to unplanned activity. Coach must define rest days explicitly.

Error 4: Skipping the activation session on day –1.

Error 5: Ignoring psychological session structure.

Article 93: Travel Week and Low-Equipment Competition Prep

Travel Classification Decision Tree

Scenario A — Same-day travel (under 4 hours transit time, no overnight): Minimal impact. Follow standard competition week microcycle.

Scenario B — Overnight domestic travel (1–2 nights away, no time zone change): Moderate impact. Sleep quality in unfamiliar environments typically drops 15–30%. Prioritize sleep protocols over training optimization.

Scenario C — International travel with significant time zone change (3+ hours): High impact. Jet lag disrupts circadian rhythm, reduces strength expression by 5–10% for 1–3 days.

Directional Jet Lag Logic

Eastbound travel (losing hours, harder to adapt): Arrive 3–4 days before competition minimum. Use morning light exposure immediately on arrival.

Westbound travel (gaining hours, easier to adapt): Arrive 2–3 days before competition minimum. Stay awake until local target sleep time on arrival night.

Short trips (48 hours or less, large time zone change): Do not attempt to shift circadian rhythm — maintain home schedule as much as possible.

Low-Equipment Training Protocols

What must be maintained during travel week: Movement pattern activation (not load — pattern); arousal regulation and readiness routine; soft tissue quality and joint mobility; psychological readiness state.

What can be maintained with minimal equipment: Lower body pattern: bodyweight squat, single-leg work, hip hinge with resistance bands. Upper body pattern: push-up variations, banded pressing, face pulls with band. Core: any anti-rotation or anti-extension work.

What cannot be meaningfully maintained during travel: Absolute strength at competition-specific loads; sport-specific skill at full speed without sport-specific equipment; glycolytic conditioning at high intensity.

Hotel Gym and Bodyweight Session Structure

Session type: Activation + Pattern Maintenance (20–30 min):

1. Soft tissue and mobility (5 min): thoracic rotation, hip flexor release, ankle mobility
2. Activation sequence (10 min): glute bridges, banded clamshells, face pulls, scapular retractions
3. Movement pattern rehearsal (10–15 min): squat, hinge, push, pull patterns at low load
4. Breathing / arousal regulation: 5 min box breathing or HRV-training protocol

Do not turn hotel gym sessions into training sessions. No sets to failure. No volume accumulation. No metabolic conditioning.

Nutrition and Hydration During Travel

- Maintain protein intake as close to normal as possible.
- Avoid novel foods within 48 hours of competition — gut response to unfamiliar cuisines is a significant performance risk.
- Hydration increases during air travel — target an additional 0.5L per 3 hours of flight time.
- Alcohol: zero during competition travel week, non-negotiable.

Article 94: Testing Decisions — When to Test, When to Estimate, and When to Avoid Max Attempts

Testing maximum strength is a high-risk, high-information event. Done correctly, it confirms readiness, establishes accurate training maxes, and builds athlete confidence. Done incorrectly, it creates injury, deflates morale, and generates inaccurate data that corrupts subsequent programming. Most coaches test too frequently, at the wrong time, and without a decision rule system.

Testing Decision Tree

Step 1 — Determine the purpose of the potential test. Programming calibration: move to Step 2. Competition performance: move to Step 3. Athlete confidence or psychological milestone: move to Step 4. Coach curiosity or general progress checking: do not test. Estimate.

Step 2 — Testing for programming calibration. Appropriate when: athlete has completed a full training block (8–12 weeks) since last test; current working weights have reached or exceeded the projected training max; velocity/RPE data at submaximal loads no longer matches projected maxes; a new training phase is beginning. Not appropriate when: within 3 weeks of competition; in a deload or recovery week; managing an active injury; training inconsistent for the prior 4+ weeks.

Step 3 — Testing for competition performance. Powerlifting / Weightlifting / Strongman: testing should occur no later than 3–4 weeks out. Within 2 weeks of competition is contraindicated for absolute strength sports. Use a pre-competition mock session (90–93% singles) rather than a true max test. Combat sport: true max strength tests are rarely meaningful. Test sport-relevant strength qualities (force output at moderate fatigue, loaded carry capacity, repeated power output) rather than isolated 1RM.

Step 4 — Testing for psychological milestones. Only authorize if: physical readiness markers are high; the attempt is at a load representing a 90–95% confidence single; adequate recovery time exists before the next block or competition (3+ weeks); the athlete understands this is an expression session, not an all-out effort.

When to Estimate Instead of Test

Estimation is underutilized and often more valuable than direct testing. Use estimation when: training is proceeding normally and weights are moving well (RPE-based estimation at submaximal loads provides accurate 1RM data without fatigue and injury risk); the athlete is within 4–6 weeks of competition (use mock sessions at 90–93% rather than true maxes); the athlete is returning from injury or illness (do not test until 3–4 weeks of consistent training post-return); the training environment does not support safe max attempts.

When to Explicitly Avoid Max Attempts

Avoid any max attempt when: athlete reports readiness below 7/10; athlete had poor sleep for 2+ consecutive nights; athlete is managing an acute soft tissue injury, active joint inflammation, or unresolved pain; athlete is within 3 weeks of a competition that matters; the testing environment cannot ensure a safe missed attempt; athlete is emotionally dysregulated; athlete's technique has degraded under recent loading.

Attempt Selection Rules

Opener (first attempt): a load the athlete can make on the worst day of their life — 92–95% of estimated max. Second attempt: based on opener feel; if opener moved at expected bar speed, move to 97–100% of estimated max; if heavy or technique degraded, cap at 97%. Third attempt: reserved for record attempts or specific performance milestones only; authorized only by clearly defined pre-set green-light criteria.

Rule: no emotion-driven loading. The load for each attempt is determined by the previous attempt, not by how the athlete feels standing in front of the bar.

Common Errors

Testing too frequently — monthly 1RM testing creates unnecessary fatigue and redundant data. Test at block completion or competition; estimate between. Testing when fatigued — a fatigued max test measures fatigue, not fitness; it produces an artificially low number that corrupts subsequent programming. Letting athletes self-select attempts — athletes almost always choose too much too fast; the coach sets the attempts based on criteria. Confusing a hard training single with a max attempt. Skipping the attempt plan before the session — every testing session must begin with a written attempt plan.

Article 95: Peaking for Strength, Skill, and Combat Sport — Different Peak Models

Peaking is not the same as tapering. The taper reduces fatigue. The peak is the intentional design of an 8–16 week block to ensure that fitness, technique, and competitive readiness all converge at a single target date. Different athletic demands require fundamentally different peak architectures.

Peak Model Decision Tree

Step 1 — Define the primary competitive demand. Single-event maximal force output (powerlifting, Olympic weightlifting): Strength Peak Model. Multi-event expression of strength, power, and skill (strongman, highland games): Multi-Quality Peak Model. Strength and skill expression under accumulated fatigue and physiological stress (combat sport, BJJ, wrestling): Combat Sport Peak Model. Cyclic repeated-power output with strength maintenance (CrossFit, functional fitness): Hybrid Conditioning Peak Model.

Model 1: Strength Peak (Powerlifting / Olympic Weightlifting)

Total duration: 12–16 weeks. Weeks 1–4 (Accumulation): volume at or above training baseline; intensity moderate (70–82%); goal: maximize training adaptations, maintain technique under moderate load. Weeks 5–8 (Intensification): volume begins to decrease; intensity climbs (80–90%); emphasis shifts to technical mastery under heavier loads; introduce competition-specific singles and doubles. Weeks 9–12 (Competition Prep / Realization): volume drops to 50–65% of accumulation peak; intensity reaches 90–97%; all work is competition-movement focused; singles at 90–95% with technical assessment. Weeks 13–14 (Taper): apply taper decision rules from Article 91.

Key peak indicators: bar speed at 85% should be measurably faster in week 10 than week 4; RPE at 90% should drop from approximately 9 in week 6 to approximately 8 in week 11; technique at near-max loads should be cleaner in week 11 than week 7.

Model 2: Multi-Quality Peak (Strongman / Highland Games / Hybrid Strength Sport)

Total duration: 10–14 weeks. Weeks 1–3 (General Preparation): mixed quality work — strength, power, conditioning, and sport-specific event training all present. Weeks 4–7 (Specialization): prioritize competition events; reduce non-event work; strength training becomes event-specific; conditioning matches event demands. Weeks 8–10 (Competition Realization): events at near-competition loading and sequencing; simulate competition-day event order; intensity high, volume controlled. Weeks 11–12 (Taper): reduce event volume by 50%; maintain event speed and technical quality.

Model 3: Combat Sport Peak

Total duration: 8–12 weeks (structured as a fight camp). Competition performance depends on strength expression under accumulated physiological stress — not isolated maximum output. Weight management is a concurrent variable. Technical and tactical sharpness are the primary performance determinants — strength supports skill. Weeks 1–4 (Volume Base): high training volume across all qualities; work density is the priority. Weeks 5–8 (Intensification): volume begins to drop; strength work intensifies; sparring quality increases. Weeks 9–10 (Competition Sharpening): volume drops significantly; strength sessions 2× per week at maintenance loading only; sparring is technical and controlled. Weeks 11–12 (Competition Week): apply competition week microcycle from Article 92.

Model 4: Hybrid Conditioning Peak (CrossFit / Functional Fitness)

Total duration: 8–10 weeks. Prioritizes repeatable power output, strength expression at fatigue, and efficient energy system use. Weeks 1–3 (Broad Base): high variety — all energy systems trained, strength maintained. Weeks 4–6 (Event Specificity): training selection narrows toward likely competition demands. Weeks 7–8 (Realization): near-competition rehearsals; full-effort simulated workouts. Weeks 9–10 (Taper): reduce volume by 40–50%; maintain competition-specific movement speed.

Peaking Timing Rules

Competition Type  Peak Block Length  Taper Starts  Final Heavy Session
Powerlifting  12–16 weeks  14 days out  Day –5
Olympic Weightlifting  10–14 weeks  10 days out  Day –5
Strongman  10–12 weeks  10 days out  Day –4
Combat Sport  8–12 weeks  10 days out  Day –4 to –5
CrossFit / Hybrid  8–10 weeks  7 days out  Day –3 to –4

Common Errors

Running the same peak model for every athlete and every sport. Starting the peak block too close to competition. Maintaining equal emphasis on all qualities throughout the peak — selection is required. Not defining peak success indicators before the block begins. Treating the peak as automatic — the peak model is a plan, not a guarantee.

Article 96: Post-Competition Transition — Restoration, Rebuild, and Next-Block Selection

What happens in the 2–4 weeks after competition is the most neglected phase of elite program design. Athletes and coaches either default to immediate full training resumption or extend recovery indefinitely. The post-competition transition is a distinct programming phase with its own objectives, decision rules, and timeline.

Post-Competition Assessment Decision Tree

Step 1 — Assess the athlete's physical and psychological state within 48 hours of competition. Collect: subjective readiness score (1–10); acute injury or soreness report; sleep quality post-competition; athlete's emotional state; degree of weight cut taken and refeeding quality if applicable.

Step 2 — Classify the recovery need. Category 1 (Physically intact, psychologically satisfied): standard restoration window of 7–10 days light activity, then rebuild initiation. Category 2 (Physically intact, psychologically disappointed): same physical restoration; add psychological reset work — goal clarity session, honest performance debrief, re-established buy-in before rebuilding. Category 3 (Physical stress present): restoration window extends to 14 days minimum; physical assessment before training resumption. Category 4 (Major injury): full medical assessment required; training timeline determined by injury management, not program schedule.

Restoration Phase (Days 1–10 standard, up to 21 days extended)

Days 1–3 (Immediate post-competition): no structured training; light movement only — walking, swimming, mobility; prioritize sleep, hydration, and caloric restoration. Days 4–7 (Active recovery initiation): 2–3 light sessions, movement quality only, no loading; Zone 2 aerobic work 20–30 min maximum. Days 8–14 (Rebuild entry evaluation): subjective readiness back to 7+ for 3 consecutive days; movement quality at baseline; sleep quality restored. If any marker remains below threshold, extend restoration.

Rebuild Phase (Weeks 2–4)

Begin at 50–60% of pre-taper training volume. Intensity: 70–80% of pre-competition working loads. No maximum-effort work until week 3 of rebuild at minimum. Week 1 of rebuild: 3–4 sessions maximum. Week 2: 4–5 sessions, introduce secondary movements. Week 3: return to pre-competition session frequency if all markers clear.

Next-Block Selection Logic

Question 1: What did the competition reveal? Strength limitation → next block emphasizes strength accumulation. Conditioning or work capacity limitation → next block emphasizes energy system development. Technical failure under fatigue or pressure → next block includes structured skill blocks under fatigue conditions. Psychological limitation → performance coaching and graduated competition exposure.

Question 2: What is the next competition timeline? Competition in 6 weeks or less: compressed peak block immediately after rebuild. Competition in 8–12 weeks: short accumulation block (4 weeks) → intensification → competition prep. Competition in 12–20 weeks: full program cycle. No upcoming competition: general physical preparation block.

Question 3: What is the athlete's recovery and motivation state? Full recovery, high motivation: enter normal accumulation on schedule. Full recovery, low motivation: 2-week novelty block before structured accumulation. Incomplete recovery, high motivation: enforce extended restoration — high-motivation athletes are most at risk for premature return. Incomplete recovery, low motivation: extend restoration and address motivational factors before programmatic intervention.

Common Errors

Returning to training within 3–5 days of competition. Using the post-competition window as a free training period. Selecting the next block based on what the coach wants to train rather than what competition revealed. Ignoring the psychological component of post-competition transition. Not scheduling the next competition before beginning the next block — without a target date there is no peak structure.

Article 97: Readiness Signal Hierarchy — Which Data Matters First

When an athlete shows up with multiple data points — HRV readings, sleep scores, subjective readiness, bodyweight, stress ratings — there is a temptation to average them. That is wrong. Signals are not equal. They have different predictive validity, different response windows, and different sensitivity to noise. Applying a hierarchy is how trained coaches actually process information.

The hierarchy is built on three principles: (1) acute physiological signals outweigh self-report when they conflict in the same direction; (2) objective trends over 5–7 days outweigh single-session snapshots; (3) performance output is the ground truth — everything else is upstream prediction.

Tier 1 — Primary Signals (Highest Decision Weight)

Heart Rate Variability (HRV): use 7-day rolling average as baseline. Threshold for concern: more than 10% below rolling average on consecutive mornings (2+). Single-day HRV drops of 10–15% with all other signals normal: monitor only, do not adjust. Consecutive drops with confirmed poor sleep: reduce session load 15–20%. Use rMSSD (preferred over SDNN) for daily readiness tracking in strength athletes.

Subjective Readiness Score (SRS): administered as a 1–10 scale prior to warm-up, before any feedback from the coach or external cues. SRS 8–10: proceed as programmed. SRS 6–7: proceed, monitor bar speed, apply +5% RPE ceiling. SRS 4–5: reduce working set volume by 20–25%, keep intensity, cut sets. SRS 1–3: move to recovery-tier session — no top sets, technique work and submaximal volume only.

Tier 2 — Secondary Signals (Modifier Weight)

Resting Heart Rate (RHR): compare to 30-day baseline. More than 5 BPM above baseline: flag as mild suppression indicator. More than 10 BPM above baseline: treat as confirming signal for fatigue accumulation, downregulate volume. RHR alone without HRV or SRS confirmation: no program adjustment, flag for monitoring.

Sleep Quality Score: single poor night — no program adjustment unless SRS also drops below 6. Two consecutive nights: reduce session complexity, drop accessory volume, keep primary movement. Three consecutive nights: reduce working intensity by 10% and volume by 20%, flag as recovery week trigger.

Perceived Stress Score: stress rating 6+: do not attempt new PRs or top sets requiring maximum arousal. Stress rating 8–10: treat similarly to SRS 4–5.

Tier 3 — Contextual Signals (Background Weight)

Bodyweight trend: single-session reading is noise; use 5-day rolling average. Large acute drop (more than 1.5% bodyweight in 24 hours) during a high-stress week: flag dehydration or caloric deficit as a confounding variable.

Nutrition compliance: low compliance reduces the reliability of all other signals.

Signal Hierarchy Decision Tree

Both Tier 1 signals normal (HRV within 10% baseline, SRS ≥ 8): proceed as programmed. One Tier 1 signal flagged: collect Tier 2 signals — if Tier 2 confirms, apply volume reduction 15–20%; if Tier 2 normal, monitor session with RPE ceiling, do not cut volume preemptively. Both Tier 1 signals flagged: move to modified session immediately without waiting for Tier 2 confirmation. Tier 1 normal, Tier 2 flagged only: no volume adjustment, remove PR attempts, add post-session wellbeing check.

False Positives

Technology artifacts: HRV readings taken outside consistent conditions (post-alcohol, after shift work, different time of day, fever) are unreliable. Single-data-point overreaction: the most common error is adjusting a full training week based on one bad HRV morning. Athlete manipulation: some athletes learn to under-report SRS to avoid heavy sessions — cross-reference with HRV and actual bar speed during warm-up sets.

Article 98: Performance Drop Interpretation — Bad Day, Accumulated Fatigue, Skill Issue, or Program Error

When performance drops, there are exactly four possible root causes. Each has a different fix. Misdiagnosing the cause leads to the wrong intervention.

Cause 1: Acute Performance Dip (Bad Day)

Identification: performance drop in one session only; prior 3–5 sessions at or above expected output; HRV, SRS, and sleep data are normal or only mildly flagged; athlete reports no exceptional stressor. Interpretation rule: noise. Do not adjust. Adjustment route: none. Log the performance and re-test at next scheduled heavy session.

Cause 2: Accumulated Fatigue

Identification: performance has declined or stalled over 2–4 sessions in a row; HRV shows sustained depression (below rolling average on 3+ consecutive readings); athlete reports higher RPE at same loads than 2–3 weeks ago; motivation declining. Decision threshold: 3+ consecutive sessions at or below 92% of expected output plus HRV depressed 10%+ for 4+ days = confirmed overreaching — initiate deload immediately.

False positive: caloric deficit mimics accumulated fatigue almost perfectly. Before prescribing a deload, verify nutrition compliance and caloric intake for the prior 7–10 days.

Adjustment route: reduce volume 40–50% for 5–7 days; maintain intensity at 70–80% of recent working loads; keep frequency (removing training days entirely can extend the hole); re-test performance at days 7–10.

Cause 3: Skill or Technical Regression

Identification: movement quality is visibly degraded under the same or lower loads; athlete cannot maintain bar path, depth, bracing, or position they previously owned; performance dropped in specific lift but not in supplementary movements for the same muscle groups; no HRV, sleep, or stress signal flags. Decision threshold: two sessions with clear technical breakdown at a weight previously handled cleanly. Adjustment route: reduce load to where technique is fully intact; use positional work, paused reps, or tempo variations to re-groove the pattern.

Cause 4: Program Error

Identification: performance stagnant or declining since the start of a block, not mid-block; athlete cannot identify a lifestyle, nutrition, or stress cause; HRV and SRS suggest the athlete is recovered; other lifts are progressing while this specific variable is not. Decision threshold: 3+ weeks with no adaptation on a given variable despite appropriate readiness. Adjustment route: change the primary stimulus (shift rep range, change primary movement variation, adjust load zone); review frequency; audit total weekly volume for the target quality.

Signal Pattern  Most Likely Cause  First Action
One bad session, all signals normal  Acute dip  Log and continue
3+ sessions declining, HRV depressed  Accumulated fatigue  Immediate deload
Technical breakdown, no physiological flags  Skill regression  Load reduction and technique work
No adaptation over 3+ weeks, athlete feels ready  Program error  Block redesign

Article 99: Bodyweight and Body Composition Trend Interpretation for Training Adjustments

In strength-focused programming, bodyweight and body composition are performance variables. A sustained bodyweight drop in an athlete trying to maintain mass indicates a fueling problem that directly suppresses adaptation.

Data Collection Standards

Weigh-in protocol: morning, post-void, pre-food, same scale, same conditions. Single-morning readings are noise — use 5-day or 7-day rolling averages. Do not make any nutrition or training adjustment based on a single weigh-in. Frequency: daily is optimal; minimum 3× per week.

Trend Interpretation Rules

Bodyweight rising (mass gain phase): target rate 0.25–0.5% of bodyweight per week. Above 0.5%/week for more than 3 consecutive weeks: likely fat gain acceleration, review caloric surplus. Below 0.25%/week for 3+ weeks despite reported caloric compliance: re-audit intake.

Bodyweight stable: stable bodyweight with declining strength = caloric deficit or recovery failure — investigate both. Stable bodyweight with improving strength = appropriate, continue.

Bodyweight dropping (cut or unintended deficit): intended cut target 0.5–1.0% bodyweight per week. Above 1.0%/week: aggressive — expect some strength loss, reduce volume to preserve intensity. Above 1.5%/week: unacceptable for performance preservation. Unintended drops more than 0.5%/week for 2+ weeks: treat as a fueling emergency regardless of athlete intent.

Training Adjustment Routing from Bodyweight Trends

Aggressive deficit (more than 1.0%/week drop): reduce accessory volume by 25–30%; maintain primary lift frequency and intensity; prioritize protein intake verification. Stalled mass gain (surplus confirmed, weight not rising): add 100–200 kcal/day from carbohydrate; reassess 7-day rolling average after 10 days.

False Positives

Water and glycogen fluctuation: a 1–2 kg swing in 24 hours is normal with no training implication. Menstrual cycle variation: female athletes can show 1–3 kg of cycle-phase variation; track cycle phase alongside bodyweight data. High-carbohydrate refeed effect: a 0.5–1.5 kg bodyweight increase after a refeed day is expected; do not flag as fat gain.

Article 100: Sleep, Stress, and Recovery Signal Routing

Strength performance is limited by the nervous system's ability to recruit and coordinate muscle capacity under load. Sleep and psychological stress are the two primary inputs that determine nervous system readiness. Treating them as soft, secondary variables is a coaching error with direct consequences: accumulated CNS fatigue, suppressed hormonal environment, impaired motor learning, and increased injury risk.

Sleep Signal Categories and Routing

Category A — Adequate Sleep (No Action Required): total sleep time ≥ 7 hours; self-reported quality ≥ 3/5; proceed as programmed.

Category B — Mildly Compromised Sleep (Monitor): total sleep time 5.5–7 hours or quality 2/5 with adequate duration. Single night: proceed but cap RPE ceiling at +5% above planned, do not attempt new PRs or maximal effort sets.

Category C — Significantly Compromised Sleep (Modify Session): total sleep time less than 5.5 hours, quality 1/5, or two consecutive nights of Category B. Reduce working set volume by 20–25%; replace top sets with submaximal, higher-rep quality work at 70–75% of planned intensity; keep all movements in the session.

Category D — Severe Sleep Disruption (Recovery Session Protocol): total sleep time less than 4 hours or three consecutive nights of less than 6 hours. Shift to recovery session: technique work only, no working loads above 70% 1RM, no compound movements to failure. If HRV is also more than 15% below baseline: consider rest day with active recovery only.

Consecutive Night Rules

Two consecutive Category B nights = treat as Category C. Three consecutive Category C nights = treat as Category D. Sleep debt compounds — three mediocre nights is a systemic disruption to hormonal signaling and motor pattern retention.

Stress Signal Categories and Routing

Low Stress (1–4): normal session, no modification. Moderate Stress (5–7): avoid maximal efforts requiring complete attentional focus; maintain working sets but shift top sets to 5–10% below planned max. High Stress (8–10): treat as equivalent to poor sleep Category C for volume and intensity modification; do not attempt technical skill acquisition; keep the session with familiar loading and a controlled environment.

Combined Routing Matrix

Recovery Signal Markers Beyond Sleep and Stress

Appetite: a significant drop in appetite — especially in an athlete in a caloric surplus — is an underrated recovery signal that often precedes HRV drops by 24–48 hours. Motivation to Train: a consistent and significant decline in motivation over 7–14 days is a systemic recovery signal; combined with HRV depression and performance drop, it confirms accumulated fatigue. Session-to-Session Recovery Rate: if an athlete requires more than 72 hours to feel recovered between sessions that previously required 48 hours, recovery capacity is declining — this is a direct volume or frequency adjustment signal.

Article 101: Pain, Soreness, and Local Tissue Feedback Scoring

The Clinical Distinction Every Coach Must Own

Soreness and pain are not on the same spectrum. They are categorically different phenomena with different causes, different implications, and different responses.

Soreness: delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) is diffuse, bilateral (usually), worsens with palpation over the muscle belly, peaks at 24–48 hours post-session, and resolves without intervention. It is a training signal, not an injury signal.

Pain: localized, often unilateral, does not follow the DOMS timeline, worsens under load or at joint end-range, may radiate, and can appear during or immediately after the session. Pain is a tissue integrity signal. It requires assessment before the next loading decision.

Soreness Scoring System

Score 1 — Minimal: no soreness at rest; slight awareness during warm-up that clears within 5–10 minutes; no performance implication; train as programmed.

Score 2 — Mild: soreness present at rest or during everyday movement; clears within warm-up; some initial performance decrement that normalizes by working sets; no modification needed unless combined with other flags.

Score 3 — Moderate: soreness present during movement throughout the session; does not fully clear during warm-up; performance at target muscle group 5–10% below expected; modify by reducing working sets by one while maintaining intensity.

Score 4 — Significant: soreness limiting full range of motion or producing notable performance reduction (more than 10%); route: reduce volume 30–40% at the affected region; do not add new soreness stimulus to the same area; allow 24–48 additional hours before re-testing.

Score 5 — Severe: athlete cannot perform target movements at any reasonable load without significant discomfort; route: remove the affected movement pattern from the session; reassess within 24 hours; if not significantly resolved, treat as a pain signal.

Pain Scoring and Red Flag Classification

Green — Train Through: discomfort is a familiar ache that has been assessed and cleared; no worsening with warm-up or increased load; athlete can perform full range of motion without guarding; no radiating sensation; action: train as programmed and monitor.

Yellow — Modify and Monitor: pain is present but mild (2–3/10 VAS); does not worsen with controlled loading; localized with no radiation; previously assessed and classified as manageable; action: remove the specific loading pattern that aggravates it, find a substitution, reassess at next session.

Orange — Assess Before Training: pain is 4–5/10 or higher; present at rest or worsening under load; new onset within the last 24–48 hours; joint-based or with an audible event; action: do not load the region, train around it, refer for physiotherapy or medical assessment before full loading resumes.

Red — Stop and Refer: pain 6/10 or higher; radiating pain or neurological symptoms (numbness, tingling, weakness in distal structures); any sharp pain during a lift that forces the athlete to stop mid-set; swelling, bruising, or significant instability at a joint; action: end session for affected region, refer for medical evaluation, do not resume loading until cleared by a clinician.

Feature  Soreness (DOMS)  Pain (Tissue Signal)
Onset  12–48 hours post-session  During or immediately after session
Location  Muscle belly, diffuse  Joint, tendon, or localized spot
Bilateral?  Often bilateral  Usually unilateral
Worsens with load?  Warm-up reduces it  Worsens or does not improve
Resolves in 48–72h?  Yes, without intervention  Not on the standard DOMS timeline
Radiates?  No  Possible, especially if neural

Soreness-Adjusted Training Rule: Soreness Score 1–2 = no modification. Score 3 = reduce sets by one, maintain intensity. Score 4 = reduce volume 30–40%. Score 5 with no resolution during warm-up = remove movement, reassess in 24 hours.

Pain rule: any pain above Yellow classification = remove the aggravating pattern. Never test a pain signal with a heavy set to see if it goes away.

Article 102: Coach Dashboard Logic — Turning Weekly Data Into Program Decisions

A coach dashboard is not a reporting tool. It is a decision engine. Every piece of data it displays must connect to a decision. The weekly review session — typically 15–30 minutes — should end with a written program adjustment record or a written confirmation that no adjustment is warranted.

Dashboard Data Inputs — Weekly Review Checklist

Performance Metrics (Primary): working set completion rate; top set output vs. target; bar speed or velocity data if available; volume completed vs. prescribed.

Readiness Metrics: HRV — daily readings, 7-day average vs. 30-day baseline, trend direction; SRS — average across training days, any days below 5; sleep — average duration, any nights below 6 hours, consecutive flag count; stress — daily ratings, any days above 7 or 9.

Body Metrics: bodyweight 7-day rolling average vs. previous week; rate of change (% per week); body composition update if measurement week.

Pain and Soreness: any Orange or Red pain flags; soreness scores by region; movement modifications applied and why.

Compliance: session attendance; nutrition log compliance; significant life events reported.

Review Sequence — Five-Step Protocol

Step 1 — Red Flag Scan: check for any Red pain flag; any session missed due to illness, injury, or crisis; HRV sustained below 85% of baseline for 5+ days; SRS average below 5 across training days. If any are true, the program response is predetermined.

Step 2 — Performance Trend Assessment: did the athlete complete more than 90% of prescribed volume? Were top sets at or above target? Is performance trending upward, flat, or downward vs. the prior 3-week window? Apply four-cause classification (Article 98) if downward.

Step 3 — Readiness Trend Assessment: what is the HRV direction this week? Were there high-stress days that affected session quality? Was sleep a limiting variable? Assign a readiness status for next week: Green (full program), Yellow (monitor, no advancement), Red (load reduction or deload).

Step 4 — Body Metrics Integration: is bodyweight trend aligned with the stated phase goal? Flag any discrepancies between weight trend and performance trend.

Step 5 — Decision Synthesis and Record: write one of the following decisions.

Decision Types

Decision A — Continue as Programmed: all signals normal, performance progressing, readiness adequate.

Decision B — Load Advancement: performance above target consistently for 2+ weeks, readiness Green, apply planned progression.

Decision C — Load Hold: performance flat with adequate readiness; maintain current prescription for one more week; add a diagnostic session if needed.

Decision D — Volume or Intensity Reduction: readiness flagged by Tier 1 or confirmed Tier 2 signals; apply the relevant reduction protocol; document trigger signal and reduction applied.

Decision E — Deload: accumulated fatigue confirmed, performance declining, HRV suppressed; apply formal deload protocol.

Decision F — Block Redesign: program error confirmed or athlete's goals have shifted; rebuild the block from the training hypothesis stage; document what the previous block achieved and what the new hypothesis is.

Weekly Review Record Format

Every weekly review must produce a written record stating: week ending date; athlete; review status (Green / Yellow / Red); performance summary; readiness summary; body metrics; pain/soreness flags; decision type (A–F); specific action; next week prescription; follow-up items.

Automation and Threshold-Based Alerts

Recommended auto-alerts: HRV less than 88% of rolling average for 3+ consecutive days; SRS ≤ 4 on any session day; bodyweight change more than 1.5% in either direction in one week; session completion less than 80%; any Orange or Red pain flag; no data entry for 48+ hours on a scheduled session day.

Alerts surface exceptions. The coach provides interpretation and decision. Alerts do not make decisions.

The weekly review is the interface between what happened and what happens next. That is its entire function.

Integration Principles That Govern the Whole System

Principle 1 — Sport load is counted toward every shared threshold.
Mat hours, sparring sessions, conditioning density, and sport-specific stress are not separate from the gym ledger. They are part of it. The stress-cost layer (Cluster F) and the volume threshold layer (Cluster G) both mandate that sport-supplied volume be added to every shared channel before gym volume is prescribed. A program that scores only the gym is miscalibrated by design.

Principle 2 — The most constrained channel governs.
Whether using the AR tier system (Cluster G), the governing-dimension rule (Cluster F), the conflict resolution priority order (Cluster K), or the signal hierarchy (Cluster M) — in every layer, the most constrained variable sets the ceiling. Planning to the average reserve while one channel is already overdrawn is the primary structural error this system is designed to prevent.

Principle 3 — Maintenance is a legitimate and strategic dose.
Qualities hold for 2–3 weeks at approximately MEV across every cluster. This is what makes prioritization possible without loss. When sport stress is high, when competition is approaching, when readiness signals are suppressed — maintenance is the correct prescription, not reduced training. The system does not treat holding as failure.

Principle 4 — Substitution before cancellation.
Every layer — Cluster F's low-cost substitution library, Cluster K's exercise contraindication routing, Cluster L's travel-week protocol, Cluster M's pain-based session modification — prefers substituting to a lower-cost variant over cancelling the session. A channel being over-budget changes the exercise, rarely the session.

Principle 5 — Language discipline applies throughout.
No article in this layer claims to prevent injury, guarantee an outcome, or diagnose a condition. All guidance is framed as load management, capacity building, performance support, and tolerance development. Flagged sensitive regions reduce load on that region and prompt substitution — they do not constitute clinical advice.
